Porcelain Stoneware Facade Cladding

Porcelain stoneware is a practical material for the main facade surfaces of buildings. TECHNOFORMA Group clads facades and plinths with porcelain stoneware and often combines it with glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) decoration and natural stone.

Porcelain stoneware for facades

Porcelain stoneware is a dense ceramic with low water absorption, high frost resistance and stable dimensions. Tiles come in various formats and textures: matt, structured, stone-look, concrete-look, wood-look. Large-format tiles are chosen for ventilated facades, and strong tiles with a textured surface for plinths.

Installation methods

On a ventilated facade substructure

The main method for multi-storey buildings: tiles are fixed with clips (visible fixing) or concealed fixings to the rails. See ventilated facade installation.

With adhesive

Adhesive-fixed porcelain stoneware cladding is used on plinths and at low heights, on a prepared substrate.

Installation requirements

The durability of a porcelain stoneware facade depends not on the tiles but on the system: the load-bearing capacity of the anchors in the substrate, rail spacing, gaps between tiles for thermal movement and the quality of junctions with windows and parapets. Anchor pull-out tests are carried out before installation, and the substructure design takes into account the wind zone and building height. Insulation and a wind barrier are laid under the cladding as in any ventilated facade; see the facade insulation page.

Line items in the estimate

In an estimate, porcelain stoneware facade cladding is usually broken down into items: substructure installation, insulation, wind barrier, tile cladding, reveals and junctions, trim elements. Plinth cladding with porcelain stoneware is calculated separately. We prepare the estimate from the design after a site inspection.

Combining with GFRC and stone

Porcelain stoneware covers large surfaces, while decoration, cornices and accents are made from GFRC. This approach was used on the Dominanta residential complex (13,000 m² of porcelain stoneware and 14,000 m² of GFRC) and the Dinastiya club house (700 m² of porcelain stoneware). Formats, thickness and surface finish: what is specified for facades

Ventilated facades normally use porcelain stoneware 8–12 mm thick in formats from 600×600 to 1200×600 mm. Larger slabs call for a reinforced subframe and are more often fixed with concealed fasteners. The format affects more than the appearance of the building: it determines the spacing of the vertical rails, the amount of cutting and the share of waste.

The layout is tied to window axes and floor slab levels so that joints line up with the openings and no narrow make-up pieces appear at corners and reveals. When the slab module is coordinated with the spacing of the openings, cutting waste is, as a rule, noticeably lower than with a layout started from the corner.

Polished slabs on large surfaces produce glare and highlight the slightest deviation of the frame, so a matt or lightly structured finish is usually chosen for ordinary wall areas. Slabs are ordered as a single batch with a reserve for repairs, because shade and calibre differ between batches.

Clips or concealed fixing: how do the systems differ?

Visible fixing with clips is simpler and quicker to install; concealed fixing gives a clean surface with no metal on the face, but requires a thicker slab and factory preparation. For residential buildings, clips painted to match the slabs are usually sufficient: from a few metres away they cannot be seen. Concealed systems are chosen for entrance areas, lower storeys and buildings with higher requirements for appearance.

The frame material — aluminium, galvanised or stainless steel — is selected according to building height, the aggressiveness of the environment and fire safety requirements; the criteria are described in the article on choosing a ventilated facade subframe. Details of a porcelain stoneware facade: corners, reveals, plinth, parapet

The appearance and service life of the cladding are decided at the points where the surface ends or changes direction. These details are worked out in the design before the slabs are ordered, not resolved on the scaffolding.

  • External corners. Mitring the edges at 45° looks neat, but the arris is prone to chipping; the alternatives are an overlapping joint with an exposed edge or a metal corner profile.
  • Window reveals. Made of porcelain stoneware on their own frame or of painted galvanised steel. A sill flashing with a drip edge and the fire barrier box specified in the system documentation are mandatory.
  • Plinth. Thicker slabs, a gap between the bottom row and the paving, and a perforated profile at the cavity inlet — it keeps out debris and rodents without blocking the air intake.
  • Parapet. Air leaves the cavity under the parapet coping; the coping projects and has a drip edge so that water does not run down the slabs.
  • Building movement joints. Repeated in the frame and in the cladding; a slab must not bridge the joint.

Standard solutions are collected in the article on ventilated facade junction details. If the facade has cornices, string courses or surrounds made of GFRC, the joint between slab and decorative element is drawn separately: the element must cover the edge of the cladding and lead water away from the joint.

Which mistakes shorten the service life of porcelain stoneware facade cladding?

Most defects are caused by departures from the design during installation rather than by the quality of the slabs themselves. The following are the most common.

  1. Slabs are installed without gaps or are clamped tight by the clips. When heated, the cladding has no room to expand, and chips and cracks appear.
  2. Rails are fixed rigidly along their full length, without thermal breaks. The frame deforms and pulls the surface out of plane.
  3. Brackets, anchors or clips are replaced with unapproved ones. The system no longer corresponds to its technical certificate and structural calculation.
  4. The air inlet or outlet is blocked. The insulation accumulates moisture and the thermal performance of the wall falls.
  5. Adhesive-fixed cladding is applied to an unprepared substrate or without movement joints. The result is slabs coming away after a few seasons.
  6. Batches of different shade and calibre are mixed on one surface. The joints wander and the facade looks patchy.

Some of these risks are removed by stage-by-stage acceptance: anchor test reports, records of concealed insulation works and the flatness of the rails are checked before the slabs are hung. Correcting the frame after cladding is considerably more expensive.

A separate group of mistakes belongs to the design stage. The layout is approved without reference to the actual dimensions of the building, and on site it turns out that the deviation of the walls from the vertical exceeds the adjustment range of the brackets. For this reason a geodetic survey of the facades is carried out before materials are ordered, and the projection of the frame is corrected according to its results.

Where porcelain stoneware suits a facade and where another material is needed

Porcelain stoneware is a rational choice for large flat surfaces with a repeating module: residential complexes, business centres, retail, educational and healthcare buildings. It does not fade, absorbs almost no water and is dimensionally stable within a single batch.

The limitation of the material is that the slab is flat. Curved surfaces, deep relief and profiled details cannot be produced from it. For such areas GFRC facade panels are used, which can be cast with any pattern and radius. Lower storeys and entrance areas, which are seen at close range and may be subject to impact, are often clad in natural granite. An example of this division of materials by zone is the facades of the Dominanta residential complex.

  • Residential complexes: ordinary wall areas above the ground floor, colour-matched clips, GFRC decorative elements and window surrounds.
  • Business centres: large format, concealed fixing, combination with glazing and metal.
  • Hotels and club houses: porcelain stoneware serves as the background, with accents in stone and architectural decoration.
  • Public buildings: impact resistance of the lower tier and the ability to replace a slab quickly are important.

Care and repair: how to maintain a porcelain stoneware facade

Maintenance comes down to periodic inspections and washing. An inspection is usually carried out once a year and after strong winds: the position of the slabs and the condition of clips, sill flashings and parapet copings are checked. The cladding is washed with water and a neutral detergent; acidic products and abrasives are used with caution, as they damage the paint on the clips and the metal of the reveals.

A damaged slab is replaced locally without dismantling the area. For this purpose a reserve from the same batch — as a rule, a few per cent of the area — is kept at the time of purchase and handed over to the facilities management team with the article number, shade and calibre recorded.

Once installation is complete, the facade is cleaned of construction dust, mortar traces and sealant: old soiling is considerably harder to remove from matt and structured surfaces. On the lower tier, which is within reach of passers-by, it makes sense to plan a method of graffiti removal in advance and to agree the permitted cleaning products with the slab supplier.

The facilities management team receives the as-built documentation: layout drawings, the type of frame and fasteners, details of the slab batch and inspection recommendations. With this information a slab or a whole area can be replaced several years later without having to select the system again.

What to prepare for an estimate of porcelain stoneware facade cladding

For a preliminary estimate, dimensioned facade drawings and information on the wall material are sufficient; exact quantities are determined after a measured survey and the design of the frame. The more complete the input data, the fewer assumptions in the estimate and the closer the preliminary figure is to the final one.

  • Facade elevations with levels, opening sizes and reference to grid axes; for an existing building — a measured survey or dimensioned photographs.
  • Material and condition of the walls: in-situ concrete, brick, aerated concrete, any old finish that has to be removed.
  • Preferred format, colour and surface of the slabs, type of fixing — visible or concealed.
  • Insulation requirements: thickness according to the thermal calculation, if it has already been carried out.
  • A list of areas in other materials — plinth, entrance areas, decorative elements — so that quantities can be separated.
  • Site conditions: whether scaffolding can be erected, the programme of adjacent trades, the desired completion dates.

The cladding area is calculated from the elevation less the openings; reveals, corners and trim elements are accounted for separately. The allowance for cutting depends on the layout and is usually from a few to ten or fifteen per cent; on facades with many openings and corners it is higher. The number of brackets and anchors is determined by calculation rather than taken as an average per square metre, so frame consumption may differ between areas of the same building.

FAQ

How much does porcelain stoneware facade cladding cost?

The cost is determined after a site inspection and depends on the tiles, fixing method and substructure.

Which is better for a facade: porcelain stoneware or stone?

Porcelain stoneware is cheaper and more dimensionally stable, while natural stone is more durable and expressive; they are often combined.

Can a plinth be clad with porcelain stoneware?

Yes, with adhesive or on a substructure, using high-strength tiles.

Who carries out the installation?

The company's own installation division of more than 100 specialists; installation supervision is also available.

What thickness of porcelain stoneware is needed for a ventilated facade?

Usually 8–12 mm. For visible fixing with clips 8–10 mm is sufficient; for concealed fixing and plinths slabs from 10–12 mm are used. The exact value is set by the technical documentation of the chosen facade system.

Will an aerated concrete or hollow brick wall carry porcelain stoneware cladding?

This is determined by anchor pull-out tests on the specific building. For weak substrates special anchors are selected, the bracket spacing is reduced or the frame fixing is transferred to the floor slabs — this solution is built into the subframe design.

Can porcelain stoneware and GFRC decorative elements be combined on one facade?

Yes, this is a common arrangement. The design provides a shared frame with different brackets and spacing for each material, and the joints between slabs and GFRC elements are detailed separately.
