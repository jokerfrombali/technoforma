Efflorescence on Stone and Concrete: Causes and Removal

Efflorescence on a facade is a white or greyish deposit of salts carried by moisture from inside stone, concrete, masonry or mortar joints to the surface. The water evaporates, the salts crystallise, and stains, streaks and crusts appear on the cladding.

The key point is that efflorescence is a symptom of water movement, not a defect of the stone itself. As long as moisture keeps entering and evaporating through the cladding, the deposit will return after any cleaning. So the source of moisture and salts is found first, then the deposit is removed by a method suitable for the stone, and only then is the surface protected.

The article will be useful to clients and facility management teams of buildings clad in natural stone, concrete, GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) and brick, and to technical supervisors at handover. A general overview of stones is in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Where efflorescence comes from

Efflorescence needs three conditions at once: soluble salts, water to dissolve and carry them, and evaporation from the surface. Remove any one and no deposit forms. The main sources:

  • Cement mortars and adhesives. As cement hardens, calcium hydroxide forms; water carries it to the surface, where it reacts with carbon dioxide and turns into calcium carbonate, the most persistent type of efflorescence.
  • New concrete and masonry. Primary efflorescence in the first year after construction is typical: construction moisture leaves the structure.
  • Ground moisture. Without a damp-proof course, water rises up the plinth by capillary action and carries salts from the soil.
  • De-icing salts. Splashes from pavements saturate plinths and steps with chlorides.
  • Leaks. Poor sills, unsealed parapets, missing drip edges: water regularly runs down the cladding along the same lines.
  • The stone itself. Some porous stones contain soluble compounds, but this is less common than external sources.

To remove efflorescence for good, first answer the question: where is the water coming from? The shape of the stains is a clue: a band along the plinth means rising damp, streaks under a window mean the sill, outlines around slabs mean mortar joints.

Prevention

Prevention is built in at the design and installation stage; it is cheaper than washing the facade for years.

  1. A ventilated rainscreen system instead of wet fixing. When stone, GFRC or porcelain stoneware hangs on a substructure with an air gap, there is no mortar between the cladding and the wall from which lime can leach. This is the main design measure for reducing the risk of efflorescence on cladding.
  2. A damp-proof course in the plinth and drainage away from the building: apron, falls, downpipes.
  3. Facade drainage. Sills projecting beyond the cladding with a drip, capped parapets, sealed junctions.
  4. Low-salt materials. For wet-laid stone, adhesives and grouts designed for natural stone; for masonry, mortars with reduced free lime.
  5. Site storage. Slabs on pallets, under cover, off the ground: stone that has stood in a puddle for a month arrives on the facade already saturated with salts.
  6. Water-repellent treatment of all faces of porous stone before installation.

Removal

The removal method depends on the type of salt and the stone.

SituationMethodLimitations
Fresh loose depositDry stiff synthetic brush, then vacuumNo water: washing dissolves the salts and drives them back into the pores
Deposit on granite, basalt, silica-based sandstoneSpecial acid-based efflorescence removersPre-wet with water, follow instructions strictly, rinse thoroughly
Deposit on marble, limestone, travertine, shell limestone, dolomiteOnly products approved for carbonate stones, absorbent poulticesAcid dissolves the stone itself and leaves matt patches
Persistent carbonate crustMechanical cleaning, professional products, micro-abrasive cleaningCarried out by specialists after a trial area
Concrete and GFRCDry cleaning, then special concrete cleanersDo not damage the texture or the protective layer

The rule for any method is a trial area. After cleaning, the surface is rinsed and allowed to dry completely, and only then is it decided whether water-repellent treatment is needed. Uncontrolled high-pressure washing can damage soft stones and joints.

When it keeps coming back

Primary efflorescence on a new building often disappears by itself: construction moisture leaves, the stock of free salts in the mortar is exhausted, and after one or two cleanings the deposit no longer appears. If it does return, there is a constant source of water. Typical cases:

  • On the plinth after every winter: rising damp from the ground or de-icing salts from the pavement.
  • Under reveals and sills: water gets behind the cladding through unsealed junctions.
  • Around slab edges: mortar is washed out of joints or the adhesive bed in wet-fixed cladding.
  • On parapets and cornices: there is no protective flashing and horizontal surfaces are constantly wet.
  • After incorrect cleaning: the deposit was washed off with water, the salts went deeper and came out again.

In such cases, repeated cleaning without repairing the drainage is useless. The source of moisture is eliminated first, then the surface is cleaned and protected.

Protection

Protection against efflorescence means reducing the amount of water passing through the cladding. After the causes have been eliminated and the surface cleaned, porous stone, concrete and joints are treated with a vapour-permeable silane or siloxane water repellent. It prevents rainwater from soaking in but lets moisture from the structure escape as vapour, without carrying salts to the face.

What not to do: coat the facade with film-forming lacquers and paints. The film traps moisture, salts crystallise beneath it, and the coating peels off together with the surface layer. Treat a dry, clean surface; if treating before installation, treat all faces of the slab including the edges. More on products and intervals in the article on natural stone protection.

Types of efflorescence on stone and concrete: the salts involved and how to tell them apart

Efflorescence varies in composition, and the composition determines whether it will wash off with water or require special products. In practice there are three main groups of salts; a single facade often carries a mixture of them.

TypeAppearanceTypical sourceBehaviour
Carbonate (lime)Dense white crust, runs, build-up below jointsCement mortars, adhesives, the concrete substratePractically insoluble in water, hardens over time
SulphateLoose fluffy deposit, easily rubbed off by handMasonry materials, ground moisture, certain mortar additivesSoluble in water, reappears after wetting
ChlorideDamp dark patches with a white edge, mostly near the groundDe-icing agents, sea airHygroscopic: attracts moisture, the area stays damp for a long time

A simple test on site: if the deposit disappears when wetted and returns after drying, these are soluble salts. If it does not react to water and holds like a crust, it is a lime deposit, and a dry brush will no longer help. For a precise answer a sample of the deposit is sent to a laboratory; on large projects this is justified before the cleaning method is chosen.

The type of salt also points to the source of moisture. Lime runs indicate water passing through a cement layer; loose sulphate deposits are more often associated with wetting of the masonry or substrate; chloride patches near the ground with de-icing agents and splashing from the pavement.

Efflorescence or something else? What it is confused with

Not every light or dark mark on cladding is efflorescence. A wrong diagnosis leads to the wrong chemistry and a damaged surface, so similar defects should be ruled out before cleaning.

  • Cement haze after grouting. An even whitish veil along the joints that appears immediately after the works, not after rain. This is mortar residue, not salt migration.
  • Lime leaching. Runs and deposits below cornices, balcony slabs and at leak points. A sign of a constant flow of water through concrete rather than a single wetting.
  • A dark "frame" around the slab perimeter. Moisture or components of adhesive and sealant absorbed into the edge of the stone. It cannot be brushed off.
  • Biological growth. Green, black or grey deposits on north-facing and shaded areas; requires biocidal treatment.
  • Rust stains. Oxidation of iron-bearing minerals in the stone, or runs from fixings and railings.
  • Atmospheric soiling. Grey-black deposits beneath projections where rain does not wash the surface.

Each of these defects has its own remedy. An overview of methods and products for different types of soiling is given in the article on facade cleaning.

How to find the source of moisture: a survey procedure

The search for the cause begins with a map of the stains: where they are, when they appeared and how they change after precipitation. A few steps are usually enough to narrow the field to one or two details.

  1. Photograph the whole facade and plot the efflorescence zones on the elevations; repeat the photographs after rain and after a dry week.
  2. Relate the stains to the construction: plinth, parapet, sills, window abutments, downpipes, balconies, movement joints.
  3. Check the drainage: falls of parapet copings and window sills, drips, joint sealing, the condition of roof abutments.
  4. Inspect the cladding joints: cracks, crumbling grout, debonded sealant.
  5. At the plinth, establish whether there is a damp-proof course between foundation and wall and how the perimeter paving is formed.
  6. Measure substrate moisture with a moisture meter in the stained zone and on a clean area for comparison.
  7. If necessary, remove one or two slabs and assess the condition of the adhesive bed or the subframe.

The outcome should be not only a cleaning plan but also a schedule of repairs to the details. If efflorescence affects large areas or is accompanied by slabs debonding, a full facade survey with a report is carried out.

It is useful to keep observations for at least one full season: some leaks show only in driving rain from a particular direction or when snow melts on the parapets.

How efflorescence behaves on different materials

Susceptibility to efflorescence is determined by the porosity of the material and the fixing method. The higher the water absorption, the more water passes through the cladding and the more noticeable the salt migration; this property is discussed in more detail in the article on water absorption of facade materials.

MaterialSusceptibility to efflorescenceCleaning restrictions
Granite, basaltLow; salts emerge mainly through the jointsResistant to most products, but a polished finish is checked on a trial area
Marble, limestone, travertine, dolomiteMedium to high, especially with adhesive fixingAcidic products attack the surface; only products for carbonate stone are used
SandstoneHigh owing to porosityAbrasives and high-pressure water must not be used
Concrete, GFRCPrimary efflorescence is possible in the first monthsMild acidic cleaners are acceptable after trials; painted elements are cleaned with gentle products
Brickwork and mortar jointsHigh; the source of salts is the mortar and the brick itselfThe masonry is pre-wetted before treatment so the product is not drawn in deep

The plinth is a risk zone in its own right: ground moisture, splashing and de-icing agents all meet here. Dense stones are chosen for it and a damp-proof course is provided; the solutions are examined in the article on stone plinth cladding.

When does efflorescence appear, and at what time of year should it be removed?

The deposit most often shows in cool, damp weather — in spring and autumn. Water evaporates slowly at this time, has time to dissolve salts within the material and carry them to the surface; in summer evaporation is faster and the salts often crystallise inside the pores without emerging.

Cleaning and protective treatment are planned for a dry, warm period. The substrate must be dry, and the air and surface temperature within the limits stated by the product manufacturer, usually no lower than +5…+10 °C. After wet cleaning the facade is allowed to dry: the time depends on the weather and the porosity of the material and may range from a few days to a couple of weeks.

On a new building it is sensible to wait one season before applying a water repellent: the bulk of the construction moisture leaves during this time. Treatment over a damp substrate seals the salts inside and impairs the bond of the product to the surface.

What to provide for in the design and on site to prevent efflorescence

Most causes of salt deposits are built in long before the first rain — at the stage of detailing and work organisation. Below is a list of measures that are checked when the design is approved and when work stages are accepted.

  • Drainage details. Parapet copings with an overhang and drip, sills laid to a fall, sealed abutments to windows and roof. Standard solutions are collected in the article on facade junction details.
  • Fixing method. On a subframe with an air gap the cladding is not in contact with cement-based products, so the main source of salts is eliminated; this is how ventilated facades are built.
  • Materials for wet fixing. Adhesives and grouts with a reduced content of soluble salts intended for natural stone; clean mixing water.
  • Storage on site. Slabs are kept on pallets under cover, not on the ground: stone that has soaked up water before installation will release it through the face.
  • Protection of unfinished areas. Wall heads and exposed cladding edges are covered against precipitation until copings and sills are fitted.
  • Sequence of works. Cladding follows the roof and rainwater system, not the other way round.

It is useful to stipulate separately in the contract who removes primary efflorescence after handover and within what period: this avoids disputes in the first year of use.

Typical mistakes when removing salt deposits

  • Washing loose salts with pressurised water: the solution goes into the pores and returns after drying.
  • Hydrochloric and other strong acids on marble, limestone and travertine — the surface loses its texture and colour.
  • Wire brushes: they leave steel particles that later produce rust stains.
  • Treatment without a trial area and without neutralising and rinsing after an acidic product.
  • Applying an impregnation to a damp or uncleaned facade.
  • Repeated cleaning every season without fixing the leak that keeps feeding the salts.

The general order of safe working is the same: first dry mechanical cleaning, then a trial of a mild product on an inconspicuous area, and only if necessary a specialised product followed by rinsing. Work with chemicals is carried out in safety goggles and gloves, and windows, metal and planting near the facade are covered with sheeting.

FAQ

What is the white deposit that appears on stone after rain?

Most often it is efflorescence: salts carried by water from mortar, concrete or soil and crystallised on the surface. Less often it is lime run-off from fresh mortar or hard-water marks.

How can I remove efflorescence from a facade myself?

A loose deposit is removed with a dry brush without water. Persistent salts need special cleaners chosen for the stone: acidic products must not be used on marble and limestone. A trial on an inconspicuous area is essential.

Will efflorescence go away by itself?

Primary efflorescence on a new building often disappears within one or two seasons as construction moisture leaves. If the deposit returns every year, there is a constant source of water that has to be found.

Does a water repellent help against efflorescence?

It helps as prevention by reducing rainwater absorption. But it does not stop rising damp from the ground or leaks; those are fixed through construction measures.

Can efflorescence appear on GFRC?

As with any cement-based material, it can, especially on fresh products. Rainscreen installation without a mortar layer and proper drainage reduce this risk.

Is efflorescence harmful to the stone and fixings?

A thin surface deposit is a cosmetic defect. The danger arises when salts crystallise inside the pores, beneath the surface: repeated many times, this leads to scaling and delamination of the face layer of porous stones and mortar joints. The constant moisture in the construction that efflorescence indicates also accelerates corrosion of unprotected fixings.

How soon after cleaning can a water repellent be applied?

Once the substrate has dried completely. The time depends on the weather, the porosity of the material and the cleaning method: after dry cleaning a short pause is enough, after wet cleaning it is usual to wait from a few days to two weeks of dry weather. Moisture meter readings and the product manufacturer's instructions serve as the guide.

Why has efflorescence appeared on only one part of the facade?

A localised deposit almost always points to a specific detail: a leaking parapet joint, a missing drip, a leaking downpipe, a damaged joint. Once that defect is remedied, salts generally stop emerging in the area after cleaning.
