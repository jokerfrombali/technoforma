3D Visualisation of a Stone Facade Before Purchase

A 3D facade visualisation is a photorealistic image or model of a building showing the chosen materials, their colour, texture, layout and articulation under real lighting and in context. For a natural stone facade visualisation is especially useful: stone is expensive, variable and bought in batches, so a mistake in choosing the stone or pattern is cheaper to spot on screen than on the wall.

The main criterion of a good visualisation is that it is built on real data: surveys or working drawings, textures of specific slabs or samples, the design layout of the slabs. A pretty picture with a generic library marble helps sell an idea but does not help decide on a purchase.

Visualisation is needed by architects, developers choosing cladding, private house clients and those preparing materials for approvals. TECHNOFORMA employs designers and 3D visualisers. An overview of stones and applications is in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Why visualise

A palm-sized stone sample and a facade of thousands of square metres are perceived differently. Over a large area a colour looks lighter or darker, a bold pattern starts to look busy, and the combination with windows, roof and neighbouring materials may not work. Visualising stone cladding answers these questions before purchase:

  • Choice of stone and colour: several options compared on one model under the same lighting.
  • Layout check: how the joints fall, where narrow cuts appear, how book-matched marble looks.
  • Combination of materials: stone on the plinth, porcelain stoneware or GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) on the main areas, ornament on cornices and window surrounds.
  • Surface finish: polished, honed, flamed or brushed stone reflects light differently.
  • Scale of details: cornice depth, pilaster projection, rustication depth.
  • Communication: investor, architect, sales team and contractor discuss the same image rather than verbal descriptions.

Input data

The accuracy of a visualisation depends on the input data. The minimum set:

DataWhy
Architectural design (plans, elevations, sections) or a modelBuilding geometry, openings, levels
Survey of an existing building (for refurbishment)Actual dimensions, which often differ from archive drawings
Photos of the site and surroundingsContext: neighbouring buildings, landscaping, viewpoints
Material informationStone type, finish, slab format, colours of adjacent materials
OrientationCorrect lighting and shadows
ConstraintsPlanning regulations, facade design code requirements, investor preferences

If there is no design yet, a visualisation can be built from a sketch, but then its purpose is the concept, not a layout check. TECHNOFORMA takes measurements with its own surveyors, which removes the problem of inaccurate input dimensions for existing buildings.

Materials in the model

The main difference between a working visualisation and a marketing one is the materials in the model. For stone the following are used:

  • Scans or photos of real slabs from the chosen batch, taken under even lighting with colour calibration. Then the pattern and tone in the model match what will arrive on site.
  • Physically correct surface parameters: reflection, roughness, texture depth. Polished and bush-hammered granite look like different materials.
  • The real slab format and joint width from the layout, not a seamless texture across the whole wall.
  • Variation: slabs from different source slabs with natural differences in tone, not one texture repeated across the facade.

For GFRC, the model uses the texture and colour achieved in production, for example stone-look or concrete-look. For porcelain stoneware, textures of the manufacturer's specific range are used. All this makes it possible to see the facade as it will be, with only the error introduced by screen and print: the colour on the monitor is still checked against physical samples.

Approvals

A visualisation is a working approval tool at several levels:

  1. Within the client's team: choosing between cladding options and recording the decision in minutes.
  2. With the project architect: checking that a change or refinement of material does not distort the design intent.
  3. With the investor and sales team: approving the appearance that will go into advertising; later deviations on site become immediately visible.
  4. With the authorities: in many cities, including Moscow, photomontages and visualisations in context are required to approve the architectural appearance of a building. Requirements for their content are set by each city's regulations.

The approved visualisation together with the stone samples becomes the benchmark for acceptance: on delivery the batch is checked against what was agreed. Changes at this stage cost hours of the visualiser's time, not money for redoing the facade.

Savings

A visualisation costs incomparably less than a mistake in buying stone. Where it saves money:

  • Rejecting an unsuitable stone before ordering. Stone brought to order from another country cannot be sent back.
  • Accurate quantities. A model with a layout gives the number of slabs, cutting and waste, reducing the risk of topping up from another batch.
  • Layout optimisation. Changing the grid module by a few centimetres can significantly reduce waste of expensive stone.
  • Less rework on site. Details at openings and corners resolved in the model do not have to be resolved on the scaffold.
  • Combining materials. The model shows where expensive stone is noticeable and where it can be replaced with porcelain stoneware or GFRC without losing the look.

The savings cannot be expressed as a single figure; they depend on the project. But the logic is simple: the more expensive the material and the larger the area, the more it pays to check the solution before purchase.

What types of 3D visualisation are used for a stone facade

The format of a visualisation is chosen to suit the decision that has to be made: a few viewpoints are enough to choose a stone, close-ups are needed to check the joints, and a photomontage in context is required for approvals. There is no need to order everything at once: the set of images is extended as the design is refined.

FormatWhat it showsWhen it is needed
Still images from key viewpointsOverall appearance, colour, combination of materialsChoosing the stone, presentation to the investor
Photomontage into the existing streetscapeThe building in its real surroundingsApprovals, assessment in the context of the street
Close-ups of fragmentsJoints, finish, details at openings and cornersChecking the layout and details
360° panorama or interactive modelThe facade from any point, including pedestrian levelComplex relief, entrance areas
Textured facade elevationsEvery slab in its placeHanding the decision over to layout and procurement

At an early stage two or three general views are usually produced in several cladding options. Once the stone has been chosen, fragments and elevations are added, now using the textures of the specific batch. This sequence avoids spending time on detailed work on options that will be rejected.

At what project stage should a stone cladding visualisation be ordered

It is useful to produce a visualisation twice: at concept stage, when the material is being chosen, and before purchase, when the stone batch is known and the layout is ready. Between these points the model is refined together with the design rather than created anew.

StagePurpose of the visualisationLevel of detail
ConceptCompare stones and combinations of materials, assess budget optionsGeneral views, typical textures
Design documentationPrepare materials for approvals and sign off the appearancePhotomontage, accurate geometry, joint grid
Working documentationCheck the layout, junctions and detailsFragments, elevations, batch textures
Before deliveryCompare the batch with the approved solutionElevations with the actual slabs

If a visualisation appears only before purchase, part of its value is lost: the openings, levels and divisions of the facade are already fixed, and the layout has to be fitted to finished geometry. The earlier the stone appears in the model, the easier it is to coordinate slab sizes with the window spacing and storey height, and therefore to reduce cutting and waste. For the client it is also a way to see in advance how a decision to change the stone or the format will affect the appearance of the building, before it reaches the cost estimate.

How the visualisation work proceeds: a step-by-step sequence

The work moves from geometry to material: first an accurate model of the building, then the stone, then light and viewpoints. Changing the order is undesirable, because textures applied to inaccurate geometry have to be redone after every dimensional correction.

  1. Collecting input data and defining the task. The decisions to be made at the end are recorded: choice of stone, slab format, combination with other materials, materials for approval.
  2. Building model. The facade geometry is built with openings, reveals, cornices and plinth; for refurbishment, from the actual survey.
  3. Preliminary joint grid. The cladding division is applied to the model, taking account of floor levels, window axes and permissible slab formats.
  4. Materials. Textures of the stone and adjacent materials are assigned, and reflection and relief are set for each finish.
  5. Light and viewpoints. The position of the sun is set according to the orientation of the site, and the viewpoints from which the building will actually be seen are selected.
  6. Draft images and revisions. The client and the architect review the options, discard the unnecessary ones and refine the details.
  7. Final images and elevations. The approved option is issued as a set of views and passed on to layout.

The time required depends on the complexity of the building and the number of options. Most of the time is usually taken not by rendering but by agreeing revisions, so it is useful to define the decision-makers in advance.

Light, weather and viewpoint: how stone changes in the model and in reality

The same stone at midday, in overcast weather and after rain looks like three different materials. Approving cladding on the basis of a single sunny image is therefore risky: the model should be viewed in at least two or three lighting conditions.

  • Direct sunlight. Emphasises the relief of split-face and bush-hammered finishes and produces highlights on polished surfaces; light stones look almost white.
  • Diffuse light. The most common condition in most regions; relief is flattened, and the stone's own tone and the difference between slabs become visible.
  • Wet surface. Porous stones darken unevenly, while polished ones hardly change; this is noticeable on the plinth and beneath cornices.
  • Evening and lighting. Grazing light from floodlights reveals unevenness of the plane and the joint pattern more strongly than daytime sun.

The effect of the finish on perception is covered in detail in the article on types of stone finish. If architectural lighting is planned for the building, evening views are best included in the brief from the start: the position of the luminaires affects the choice of finish.

The viewpoint is just as important. A bird's-eye view is striking, but almost no one will see the building that way. For a purchasing decision, views from pedestrian eye level, from the opposite side of the street and from the entrance are more useful, since it is from there that the stone pattern, the joints and the junctions with other materials can be seen.

Which mistakes make a visualisation useless for purchasing?

A visualisation stops working when the image diverges from what can be manufactured and installed. This most often happens for the following reasons.

  • The slab format in the image is larger than the block sizes of the stone or the load-bearing capacity of the fixings allow; additional joints will appear on site.
  • Joints are shown thinner than in reality or not shown at all, so the facade in the picture looks monolithic.
  • Expansion and movement joints, sills, downpipes, ventilation grilles and signage are not taken into account.
  • Corners are shown as solid, although in the design they are assembled from two slabs with a visible edge or a mitre.
  • The texture is taken from a catalogue photograph, while a batch of a different tone arrives at the warehouse.
  • The images are heavily processed in a graphics editor: contrast and saturation are increased and effects are added that will not exist in reality.

To avoid these discrepancies, the visualiser needs feedback from those who design the fixings and carry out the installation. Format limits depend on the stone and the fixing method; the general principles are set out in the article on the thickness of cladding slabs.

From image to layout, working documentation and order

An approved visualisation is not the end result but the input for the next stages. Facade elevations are derived from the model, in which every slab has a size and a position; these are the basis for the stone layout with slab marking and the procurement schedule.

If the project is run in a building information model, the facade is included in it as a separate section: this makes it possible to check clashes between the cladding and windows, service penetrations and the subframe. More on this in the article on BIM and 3D modelling of facades. For small projects the chain of model, elevations and slab schedule is sufficient.

At TECHNOFORMA, visualisation, facade design and the preparation of production documentation are carried out by a single design team, so the image does not have to be "translated" for another organisation. Stone for the approved solution is selected from stock or ordered from suppliers; the service as a whole is described on the page on supply and installation of natural stone.

Checklist: how to sign off a stone facade visualisation

Before approving the images and moving on to the order, it is worth going through a short list. Each item is a question that must have an unambiguous answer.

  • Does the model geometry match the current version of the design or the survey?
  • Were the textures taken from samples or slabs of the batch that is to be purchased?
  • Do the slab format and joint width match the design layout?
  • Are corners, reveals, the plinth and the junctions with the roof and windows shown?
  • Are there views from pedestrian level and at least two lighting conditions?
  • Have the images been compared with physical samples in daylight?
  • Has it been recorded which option was approved and by whom?

If the answer to some of the questions is no, the image remains conceptual: it can be used to discuss the character of the building, but it is too early to calculate quantities and place an order.

FAQ

How many cladding options can be compared in a visualisation?

As many as needed for a decision: usually two to four stones or material combinations are compared on one model from the same viewpoints.

Will the stone colour in the visualisation match reality?

If the model uses calibrated photos of real slabs, the tone and pattern will be close. But screens and print distort colour, so the final decision is made on physical samples.

Does a private house need a 3D facade visualisation?

For a house clad in natural stone, yes: it helps choose the stone, format and combination with the roof and windows before buying the material.

What is needed to order a stone cladding visualisation?

The design or a survey of the building, photos of the site, and preferences for stone type and finish. For existing buildings TECHNOFORMA takes measurements with its own specialists.

How does a visualisation differ from a stone layout?

A visualisation shows how the facade looks from outside: colour, finish and the combination of materials. A layout is a working document in which every slab is given a size, a number and a position on the facade. The former is needed to choose a solution, the latter for production and installation; in a good project both are built on the same model.

Does a 3D visualisation replace a sample panel of cladding on site?

No. The model helps to choose the stone and the layout, but the real colour, the feel of the finish and the quality of the joints are checked on samples and on a sample panel installed on the wall. On large projects both tools are normally used.

Can a visualisation be produced if the stone has not yet been chosen?

Yes, at this stage it is used precisely to compare options. Typical textures of the candidate stones are placed in the model and, once the choice is made, they are replaced with images of the specific batch and the layout is refined.
