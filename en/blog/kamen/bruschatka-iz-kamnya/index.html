Natural stone setts are a paved surface made of small individual units of natural stone: cubes, bars and tiles with a sawn, split or flamed surface. They are most often made of granite, less often of basalt, sandstone, dense dolomite and limestone. Such paving lasts for decades, withstands vehicle loads and does not lose its colour.

The key selection criterion is matching the rock, thickness and base to the expected load: almost any dense stone will do for a footpath, whereas driveways and parking areas need strong rocks of sufficient thickness on a reinforced base.

Setts are chosen for private courtyards, driveways, squares, pedestrian streets, and the grounds of public buildings and residential complexes. Other uses of stone are covered in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Types

Stone setts are distinguished by manufacturing method and material.

By manufacturing method:

  • Split: all faces produced by splitting; uneven surface, historic look, wide joints.
  • Sawn-split: sawn sides and bottom, split top. Even laying and a textured, non-slip face: the most popular option.
  • Sawn: all faces sawn; an even surface with narrow joints, comfortable for wheels, pushchairs and heels.
  • Sawn and flamed: even, but with a rough top face.
  • Tumbled: processed in a drum to round the edges, looks “aged”.

The sizes of natural stone setts are not standardised as strictly as those of concrete pavers, but typical formats have become established on the market.

Large-format paving slabs (300×300, 400×400, 600×300 mm, etc.) at least 30 mm thick are also produced; these are paving slabs rather than setts, but the two are often combined.

Thickness is the main parameter for load: the higher the load, the thicker the unit. Split setts have noticeably larger dimensional tolerances than sawn ones, which must be taken into account when designing the base.

Laying

Correct laying matters more than the choice of stone: most paving problems (subsidence, loose stones, puddles) are caused by the base.

  1. Design and setting out. Falls (usually 1–2% for run-off), drainage, the position of kerbs and drains are determined.
  2. Excavation to a depth depending on load and soil; removal of topsoil.
  3. Geotextile separates the soil from the crushed stone and stops the base sinking into the ground.
  4. A crushed-stone sub-base compacted in layers; for driveways the thickness is increased, and on weak soils a concrete base is built.
  5. Kerbs are installed before the setts to hold the edges of the paving.
  6. A bedding layer of sand, stone dust or cement-sand mix.
  7. Laying the setts in the chosen pattern: rows, herringbone, fans, arcs, circles.
  8. Jointing with sand, stone dust or polymer jointing compounds for setts; re-compaction with a plate compactor fitted with a rubber mat.

On a rigid (concrete) base, setts are laid on adhesive or cement mixes and joints are filled with mortar grouts; this system is used on squares and in high-traffic areas.

Loads

The load determines the rock, the thickness and the base construction.

AreaRockSett thicknessBase
Garden paths and footpathsgranite, basalt, sandstone, dolomitefrom 30–50 mmcrushed stone + sand
Patios, courtyardsgranite, basalt, sandstone50–60 mmcrushed stone + sand or mix
Car drivewaysgranite, basaltfrom 60 mm, more often 80–100 mmreinforced crushed stone, sometimes concrete

The values are approximate; for public areas and roadways the pavement construction is determined by the design. Stone strength and frost resistance are confirmed by batch test reports.

Besides static loads, paving is subject to dynamic actions: braking and turning wheels shift the units, especially at edges and entrances. That is why trafficked areas need a rigid kerb, a strong interlocking bond (herringbone or staggered rows) and fully filled joints. Small 50 mm cubes are used in trafficked areas only on a rigid base.

Point loads are considered separately: bin store supports, canopy posts, forklift wheels. The base is locally reinforced in such places.

Care

Caring for stone setts is simple:

  • regularly remove debris and leaves, as organic matter in joints encourages grass and moss;
  • wash with water and, if necessary, stone cleaners; direct pressure washing at an angle so as not to wash out the jointing;
  • top up joints with sand or stone dust as it washes out;
  • remove weeds from joints mechanically or with special products;
  • impregnation is optional for granite and basalt; for sandstone, dolomite and limestone water-repellent treatment is advisable;
  • in winter, use plastic shovels and sand or granite chippings instead of salt de-icers; metal scrapers and ice picks damage the edges.

If a section subsides, the setts are lifted, the base is restored and the same stones are relaid: the repair is local and requires no new materials.

Laying patterns for natural stone setts: which to choose

The laying pattern affects not only the appearance of the paving but also its resistance to shear, the consumption of stone and the labour involved. For driveways, patterns with staggered joints are chosen; for pedestrian areas any pattern is acceptable.

PatternCharacteristicsWhere to use
Running bond with offsetSimple, little waste; the rows are set across the direction of trafficPaths, courtyards, driveways
HerringboneDistributes wheel loads well, requires blocks with consistent dimensionsVehicle entrances, car parks
Fan (arc) patternTraditional for split cubes, conceals size variationSquares, pedestrian streets
Concentric circlesA feature pattern, with much selection and cuttingSmall squares, focal points
Modular, in several formatsRows of different widths or a combination of slabs and cubesTerraces, courtyards, seating areas
RandomStones of different sizes without a regular jointGarden paths

A complex pattern increases waste and laying time, so it is usually used in feature areas, while the main area is paved in rows. Combining two stones or two finishes, for example light granite and dark basalt, makes it possible to mark zone boundaries, directions of movement and parking bays without paint.

How to calculate the quantity of setts and the allowance?

Sawn and sawn-and-split setts are measured in square metres, while fully split setts are more often sold by weight. In both cases the paved area is first determined by zones of different thickness, and then an allowance is added for cutting and selection.

  1. Divide the site into zones by load: pedestrian, vehicle entrance, driveway; each has its own thickness and format.
  2. Calculate the area of each zone from the landscaping plan, deducting manholes, tree pits and channels.
  3. Determine the length of kerbs and edge courses; these are counted separately in linear metres.
  4. Add an allowance: as a rule, a few per cent for running bond and more for arc and circular patterns, curved boundaries and split stone.
  5. For split setts, convert the area into tonnes using the supplier's data on consumption per square metre for the specific format.

A guide to weight: granite has a density of about 2.6–2.7 t/m³, so paving 100 mm thick weighs in the order of 230–260 kg per square metre, allowing for joints. This matters for logistics: one lorry delivers a limited area of thick setts, and deliveries to a large project are planned in loads to match the laying rate.

The whole quantity for one zone should preferably come from a single batch. Stone bought later may differ in tone and, in the case of split cubes, in average size as well.

Drainage and frost heave

Water is the main cause of paving failure: it washes out the joints, softens the base and lifts the surface when it freezes. Drainage is therefore designed together with the laying pattern, not after it.

  • Surface run-off. Falls direct water to channels, gullies or the lawn; on large areas watersheds are formed so that water does not flow in a continuous stream across the whole site.
  • Stone channels. Two or three rows of setts laid lower on concrete act as an open channel and do not stand out from the overall appearance.
  • Joint permeability. Sand joints let part of the water into the base, so a drainage layer is needed beneath them; mortar joints do not let water through, and all run-off goes over the surface.
  • Base drainage. On clay soils, water is removed from the crushed stone layer by drainage pipes, otherwise the base acts as a trough.
  • Frost-susceptible soils. The thickness of the non-frost-susceptible layer is increased or the soil is replaced; a rigid concrete base on such soils cracks without preparation.

Next to the walls of a building the paving is given a fall away from the facade, and a movement joint is left between the paving and the plinth. How to protect the lower part of the wall from splashes and meltwater is explained in the article on plinth cladding in natural stone.

Kerbs, steps and other elements in the same stone

Paving looks coherent when the setts, kerbs, steps and copings of retaining walls are made of the same stone or in a coordinated palette. Such items are ordered together with the setts so that tone and finish match.

  • Kerb stones, sawn or split; in trafficked areas they are set on concrete with haunching.
  • Large-format paving slabs for terraces and entrance landings, where an even surface matters.
  • Treads and risers with a flamed or bush-hammered tread surface.
  • Recessed manhole covers for stone infill, tree-pit frames, drainage channels.
  • Copings for parapets and retaining walls with a drip groove.

Stone setts, concrete pavers or clinker: which to choose

Natural stone wins on service life and retention of appearance, but requires a larger initial budget and skilled laying. Concrete pavers and clinker are easier to work with and more accurate in size.

Service lifeDecades, can be relaidUsually shorter, depends on the quality of the concreteLong
Colour retentionNatural colour, does not fadePigment dulls over timeThe colour of fired clay is stable
Dimensional accuracyHigh for sawn, low for splitHighHigh
Resistance to de-icing agentsHigh for granite and basaltThe surface may scaleHigh
Initial costHigherLowerMedium or high
Choice of shapesCubes, blocks, slabs, bespoke cuttingMany profiled shapesMainly blocks

In practice the materials are often combined: stone at entrances, squares and vehicle entrances, where appearance and durability matter, and concrete pavers on secondary paths. For heavily loaded areas, dense granite and basalt for cladding and paving are suitable.

Typical mistakes in natural stone paving

Paving mistakes do not show at once but after the first or second winter, and they have to be corrected by relaying. The most common ones are known in advance.

  • The stone thickness is chosen for pedestrian load, but vehicles drive onto the area, if only a refuse lorry or a fire engine.
  • The base is compacted in a single layer over its full thickness instead of layer by layer.
  • The kerb is installed after laying or without concrete haunching, and the edges of the paving spread.
  • The joints of split setts are made too narrow: the stones touch at the edges and chip.
  • On a flexible base the joints are filled with rigid mortar, which cracks and crumbles.
  • Soft limestone or sandstone is laid where de-icing salts are used in winter.
  • The order is placed without an allowance, and the missing stone arrives in a different shade.

Most of these problems can be avoided if, before purchase, there is a landscaping design with loads, falls and the base construction, and the stone supplier knows which zone each format is intended for.

What to prepare when ordering setts and related items

To calculate a delivery, a site plan with zones and an understanding of the loads are needed. The rest is clarified along the way, but the earlier the format and finish are known, the easier it is to select stone from a single batch.

  • A landscaping plan or site layout with dimensions and levels.
  • The purpose of each zone: pedestrian, car entrance, access for goods and special vehicles.
  • The desired stone, colour, method of manufacture and laying pattern.
  • The type of base, if it has already been built or is specified by the design.
  • A list of kerbs, steps, channels and other one-off items.
  • The site address and the desired delivery schedule.

FAQ

Why are granite setts better than concrete pavers?

Granite setts are stronger and more frost-resistant, do not fade or wear; they last for decades and the same stones can be relaid during repairs.

Can limestone setts be used?

Yes, for decorative footpaths in warm climates. For driveways and regions with harsh winters, granite or basalt is more reliable.

How thick should setts be for a car driveway?

Usually from 60 mm, more often 80–100 mm, on a reinforced base.

Which setts are least slippery?

Sawn-split, split or flamed. Polished surfaces are not used outdoors.

What is best for filling the joints between setts?

For a flexible base, sand, stone dust or polymeric sand; for a rigid concrete base, mortar grouts for setts.

Can natural stone setts be laid in winter?

They must not be laid on frozen ground: it cannot be compacted, and the paving will settle in spring. Mortars and adhesives for a rigid base require above-zero temperatures or special conditions in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Paving is therefore usually planned for the warm season, with procurement and preparation carried out in winter.

When can the paving be used after laying?

Setts on a flexible base can be walked and driven on once the joints have been filled and final compaction is complete. Paving on mortar or adhesive is put into use after it has gained strength; the period is stated by the manufacturer of the mix and depends on temperature and humidity.

Can reclaimed setts be used?

Yes, granite and basalt setts can be relaid, which is one of the advantages of stone. Before work they are cleaned and sorted by size. It should be borne in mind that the surface of old stone may have been worn smooth by wheels and footwear and becomes more slippery than that of new split stone.
