Sandstone for Facades and Plinths

Facade sandstone is a natural cladding stone made of cemented sand grains, most often quartz. It copes well with outdoor conditions, has a pleasant matt texture and a warm palette, from grey and greenish to yellow, red and brown.

The key criterion when choosing sandstone is the type of cement binding the grains: siliceous sandstone is dense and frost-resistant, while clayey and calcareous types are softer and absorb more water. This determines whether the stone is suitable for a plinth or only for walls above splash level.

Sandstone is chosen for cladding private houses, plinths, fences and landscaping, as well as for facades of public buildings where a natural material of moderate weight is needed. Other rocks are described in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Types of Sandstone

Sandstone is classified by several features, and all of them matter for facade cladding.

By grain composition:

  • quartz sandstone: more than 90% quartz, the strongest and most wear-resistant;
  • arkose: containing feldspars, often in pinkish tones;
  • greywacke: with fragments of other rocks, dark, grey-green.

By cement: siliceous (the densest), ferruginous (red-brown, strong), calcareous (reacts with acids), clayey (soft, sensitive to water). Siliceous and ferruginous varieties are suitable for facade and especially plinth cladding.

By product form:

  • sawn tiles of calibrated thickness, for even sandstone cladding with thin joints;
  • tumbled tiles with rounded edges;
  • rock-faced (split) stone with a textured surface;
  • flagstone: natural irregular slabs, mostly for plinths, fences and paths.

Dagestan sandstone for facade cladding is a common option in southern Russia: yellow and beige tones and good workability. As with any stone, batch characteristics should be checked against the test report.

Properties

Typical sandstone characteristics are given below. The ranges are wide, depending on the deposit and cement type; exact values are stated in the product data sheet or test report.

ParameterTypical range
Densityabout 2000–2700 kg/m³
Compressive strengthfrom 30 to 150 MPa and above for siliceous grades
Water absorptionfrom a fraction of a percent to several percent
Frost resistancehigh for dense grades, low for clayey ones
Abrasionlow for quartz sandstones

Sandstone does not burn, is vapour-permeable, UV-resistant and does not fade. Quartz varieties hardly react with acids, which sets them apart favourably from limestone and marble. The rough surface is not slippery even when wet, so sandstone is suitable for steps and paths.

Weaknesses: porous grades absorb water and dirt, develop efflorescence and biological growth, and with poor cement the surface may flake.

Applications

On a facade sandstone is used in several zones:

  • Walls. Full cladding or accent areas: corners, piers, bay windows. Small and medium-format slabs on adhesive or large slabs on a rainscreen system.
  • Plinth. One of the main applications thanks to its resistance to abrasion and splashes. Dense grades with low water absorption are used for plinths.
  • Surrounds. Reveals, window sills, string courses, cornices.
  • Fences and pillars. Flagstone or sawn stone on a brick or concrete base.
  • Landscaping. Paths, steps, retaining walls, kerbs.

Indoors sandstone is used for lobby walls, fireplaces and staircases. For cladding a small house, adhesive-fixed tiles are usually chosen; multi-storey buildings and large slabs require a ventilated rainscreen system with calculated fixings.

Sandstone combines well with wood, render, glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) decoration and metal: its matt surface is neutral and does not compete with other materials.

Installation

The sandstone installation method depends on the building height and stone format.

Adhesive fixing is used for tiles up to 30–40 mm on brick, concrete and rendered substrates:

  1. substrate preparation: strength, flatness, priming;
  2. laying out the stone on site, matching by tone and texture;
  3. installation from bottom to top on frost-resistant natural stone adhesive with full back coverage;
  4. for large formats, additional mechanical fixing;
  5. pointing the joints with vapour-permeable grout;
  6. cleaning and water-repellent treatment.

A ventilated rainscreen facade is used at height, on insulated walls and for large slabs. The stone is fixed to a metal substructure with clips or concealed anchors; slab thickness, bracket spacing and fixings are selected by calculation for wind load and cladding weight. A ventilated gap between the stone and the insulation carries moisture out of the wall.

Protection

Even dense sandstone benefits from protection:

  • Water-repellent treatment with penetrating siloxane products reduces water absorption, soiling and the risk of efflorescence while keeping vapour permeability. Treatment is repeated as the water-repellent effect wears off.
  • Biocidal treatments are applied on north-facing walls and in shade, where moss and algae appear.
  • A waterproofing barrier between the foundation and the plinth cladding prevents the stone from absorbing ground moisture.
  • Sills and an apron sloping away from the wall protect the bottom course from run-off and splashes.

Film-forming varnishes are undesirable on facade sandstone: they impair vapour permeability and peel over time. For cleaning, low-pressure water and neutral products are used; abrasive high-pressure washing damages the surface of soft grades.

How to choose sandstone for a facade: what to check in the batch

Sandstone for cladding is chosen on the basis of the documents and samples of a specific batch, not the name of the deposit. Two quarries in the same district can produce stone whose water absorption differs several times over, so the decision is made after the test report has been checked and the slabs inspected.

What to request and examine before ordering:

  • a test report stating water absorption, compressive strength in the dry and water-saturated state, and the frost resistance grade;
  • samples taken from at least three or four slabs, which show the real variation in tone and pattern;
  • the behaviour of the stone when wetted: on a dense grade a drop of water stays on the surface for several minutes, on a loose one it disappears within seconds;
  • the condition of edges and corners: if an arris crumbles under the fingers, the cement is weak and the stone is unsuitable for a plinth;
  • clay interlayers and rusty spots, which turn into delamination and streaks after the first winter.

A marked loss of strength in the water-saturated state indicates a clay cement. Such stone is acceptable for interiors and dry wall areas under a wide roof overhang, but not for the splash zone. It is useful to wet and dry the samples several times: this shows whether the tone changes and whether salts appear on the surface.

Which surface finish to choose for sandstone

The finish determines not only the appearance but also how quickly the facade gets dirty and how the joints read on it. Sandstone is hardly ever polished: its granular structure does not give a mirror gloss, but it takes matt and textured finishes well.

FinishAppearanceWhere it is appropriate
SawnEven matt surface with saw marksWalls on adhesive and on a ventilated facade system, thin joints
HonedSmooth, velvety, slightly richer colourEntrance areas, interiors, opening surrounds
Bush-hammeredUniform fine roughnessPlinth, steps, landings: a non-slip surface
Split (rock-faced)Bold relief with deep light and shadePlinth, corners, rustication, retaining walls
TumbledSoftened edges, an aged-stone effectPrivate houses, fences, landscaping

The rougher the relief, the more dust and moisture it retains, so split stone in a plinth must be treated with a water repellent. Tonal variation is more noticeable on smooth planes, and sorting by shade has to be stricter. Finishing methods are discussed in more detail in the article on types of stone finishing.

Sandstone for a plinth: how to detail the junction so the stone does not deteriorate

A sandstone plinth lasts a long time if the stone does not draw water from below and does not stand in a puddle. Most damage in this zone is caused not by the rock but by the construction: a missing damp-proof course, cladding taken down into the ground, and an incorrect fall of the perimeter paving.

  1. A damp-proof course between the foundation and the wall, and a coating waterproofing layer under the cladding.
  2. A gap between the bottom course of slabs and the perimeter paving, usually 10–20 mm, filled with an elastic sealant rather than mortar.
  3. A fall of the paving away from the wall and organised roof drainage, so that the jet from a downpipe does not hit the stone.
  4. A drip sill or projecting band above the plinth that throws water from the wall beyond the plane of the cladding.
  5. Joints grouted to full depth with a compound for natural stone, without voids in which water can freeze.

Where de-icing agents are used in winter, it is reasonable to take a denser rock for the bottom course and start the sandstone higher. The junction is shown in detail in the article on cladding a plinth with stone.

Typical mistakes in sandstone cladding

Mistakes with sandstone show up after one or two winters and are expensive to correct because they require dismantling. The following are the most common.

  • Ordinary grey cement adhesive under light stone: moisture carries dark stains and salts to the face. Compounds for natural stone are needed, and for light grades they should be based on white cement.
  • Applying adhesive in dabs: water collects in the voids and the tile comes away when it freezes.
  • Installation without a layout and sorting by tone, which leaves patches of slabs from different pallets on the wall.
  • Acid cleaners on grades with a calcareous cement: the binder dissolves and the surface becomes friable.
  • A film-forming 'wet look' varnish outdoors instead of a penetrating impregnation.
  • Fixing heavy slabs with adhesive at height without mechanical restraint.

A white bloom after the first season is, as a rule, the result of excess moisture in the substrate or mortar rather than a defect of the stone. The causes and remedies are described in the article on efflorescence on stone and concrete.

How sandstone differs from other rocks for facade cladding

Sandstone occupies an intermediate position between hard igneous rocks and soft carbonate ones. It is easier to work than granite and, when its composition is quartz-based, more resistant to acid precipitation than limestone and travertine.

RockStrengthLimitation compared with sandstone
GraniteMinimal water absorption, resistance to de-icing agentsHeavier and more expensive to work, a cooler colour range
LimestoneUniform light tone, easily profiledSensitive to acids, softer
TravertineExpressive pattern, low densityOpen pores need filling and protection
DolomiteDense grades are suitable for a plinthA narrower colour palette

Where a warm-toned stone is needed for large planes on a limited budget, sandstone often proves a reasonable compromise between limestone and granite.

In practice the rocks are combined: dense stone in the plinth and on the steps, sandstone on the walls, and profiled details in stone or glass fibre reinforced concrete. Sandstone on a ventilated facade: what to allow for in the design

On a ventilated facade system sandstone works in milder conditions than on adhesive: the slab is separated from the wall by an air gap and dries quickly after rain. On the other hand, the requirements for the strength of the stone itself increase, because it transfers the load from its weight and from wind to the subframe through a few fixing points.

  • Thickness. For sedimentary rocks it is taken with a margin compared with granite: usually from 30 mm, and 40 mm or more for large formats and porous grades. The final value comes from a calculation based on the flexural strength in the test report.
  • Format. The weaker the stone, the smaller the slab should be; long narrow elements of bedded grades break during transport and installation.
  • Fixing. Visible clips are simpler and more tolerant of edge quality; concealed fixing in kerfs or on anchors in the back face requires dense stone with no bedding in the slot zone.
  • Orientation of bedding. The slab is positioned so that the natural bedding does not run parallel to the face right at the edge, otherwise delamination is possible at the fixing points.
  • Joints. Open joints between slabs are left for ventilation and to accommodate thermal movement; their width is set by the system design.

The load-bearing capacity of the anchors in the substrate is verified by on-site tests, while the spacing of brackets and rails is determined by calculation, taking account of the weight of the cladding and the wind zone. For buildings in seismic areas the fixing details are selected separately.

Maintaining a sandstone facade in service

Sandstone does not need complicated maintenance, but the facade should be inspected once a year. The condition of the joints, the sealant at junctions, the drip sills and the areas under downpipes is checked, because that is where the first darkening and biological growth appear.

Dirt is washed off with water and a soft brush or with a pressure washer at moderate pressure and a wide fan; a narrow jet at close range knocks grains out of the surface. Moss and algae are removed with biocidal compounds, after which the stone is dried and the water repellent renewed. The service life of the impregnation depends on its composition and the orientation of the wall; it is usually repeated every few years, when water stops beading on the surface. Methods and products are summarised in the article on cleaning a stone facade.

FAQ

Which sandstone is best for facade cladding?

Dense quartz sandstone with siliceous or ferruginous cement: it is stronger, absorbs less water and withstands frost better. Clayey grades are not suitable for facades and plinths.

Can a plinth be clad with sandstone?

Yes, it is one of the main applications. Dense grades, a waterproofing barrier, an apron and water-repellent treatment are required.

How thick should facade sandstone be?

For adhesive cladding usually 20–40 mm, for a rainscreen facade 30 mm or more; the final thickness is determined by calculation based on slab format and loads.

Which is better for a facade: sandstone or limestone?

Quartz sandstone is generally stronger and more resistant to acids and abrasion. Limestone is easier to work and is good for decorative details.

Does sandstone need to be impregnated?

It is advisable: a water repellent reduces water absorption, soiling and the risk of efflorescence, especially on plinths and porous grades.

Does sandstone change colour over time?

Only slightly. Outdoors the stone acquires a natural patina: light grades become a little warmer and darker, ferruginous ones more saturated. Sharp, patchy darkening is a sign of wetting rather than ageing: the drip sills, joints and the condition of the water-repellent impregnation should be checked.

Can sandstone be installed in winter?

Cladding on a ventilated facade subframe is installed all year round, as it involves no wet processes. The adhesive method is limited by temperature: most compounds are designed for use at no lower than +5 °C, so in winter a heated enclosure is needed or the work is moved to the warm season.

What allowance of stone should be included in the order?

For sawn tiles of regular format, 5–10 % for cutting and breakage is usually enough. For flagstone, split stone and complex layouts with many openings the allowance is higher. The exact figure comes from the layout, which is used to count the number of slabs of each size.
