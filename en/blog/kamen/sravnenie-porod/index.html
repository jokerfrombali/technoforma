Comparing Facade Stone Types: A Table of Properties

There is no universally best stone for a facade: the choice depends on the building zone, climate and budget. For plinths, steps and regions with frequent freeze-thaw cycles, granite and basalt are the most reliable. For walls in a temperate climate, dense dolomites, limestones, sandstones and travertine work well. Marble is appropriate for details and lower floors, shell limestone for sheltered areas and dry climates, and onyx only indoors.

The key criterion is not the name of the rock but the characteristics of the specific batch: water absorption, frost resistance and strength from the test report.

This table will be useful to architects at concept stage, developers choosing finishes and private clients comparing cladding options. Each rock is covered in detail in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Comparison Criteria

It makes sense to compare natural facade stone by several parameters.

  • Water absorption is the key indicator for outdoor use. The less water a stone absorbs, the less it suffers from frost, soiling and efflorescence.
  • Frost resistance is the number of freeze-thaw cycles without loss of strength. Requirements are higher for plinths and horizontal surfaces than for walls.
  • Compressive and flexural strength determine the permissible slab thickness, format size and fixing method.
  • Density determines the weight of the cladding and the load on the substructure and wall.
  • Acid resistance matters in cities: carbonate rocks (marble, limestone, travertine, dolomite) gradually lose their polish, silicate rocks (granite, basalt, quartz sandstone) do not.
  • Workability: soft rocks are easy to cut and profile, hard ones need diamond tools but last longer.
  • Appearance and batch consistency: colour, pattern, and the ability to obtain the required volume in one tone.

All numerical values depend on the deposit. For a project, values are taken from the product data sheet or test report, not averaged reference figures.

Climate

Climate determines how strict the stone requirements need to be.

  • Central and northern Russia. Many freeze-thaw cycles, wet snow, de-icing salts. For plinths and horizontal surfaces, granite or basalt; for walls, dense rocks with low water absorption and proven frost resistance. Porous rocks only with water-repellent treatment and on a rainscreen system.
  • Southern Russia and the Caucasus. Less frost, more sun and day-night temperature swings. Limestones, dolomites, sandstones and travertine work well here; shell limestone is traditionally used for walls. For dark rocks on sunny facades, heating is taken into account.
  • Coastal areas. Sea salt destroys porous stone and leaves efflorescence. Dense rocks and regular rinsing are preferable.
  • Cities with polluted air. Carbonate rocks lose their polish, so matt finishes or silicate stones are chosen.

Regardless of climate, durability is strongly affected by detailing: sills, an apron, a waterproofing barrier and a ventilated gap often matter more than the difference between two similar rocks.

Budget

The budget for a stone facade is made up of more than the price of the stone itself. It includes:

  • the cost of the stone, depending on rock type, deposit, colour, format and thickness;
  • processing: cutting to the layout, surface finishing, kerfs, chamfers;
  • substructure and fixings: heavy stone requires a stronger substructure;
  • installation and logistics.

General patterns: hard rocks (granite, basalt) are more expensive to process; soft rocks are cheaper to process but may need greater thickness. Imported stone in rare colours costs more than local rocks. Large formats are more expensive to process and install than standard tiles.

A sensible way to manage the budget is to combine materials: granite or basalt on the plinth and at the entrance, and a more affordable stone or glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) imitating stone on the main surface. At TECHNOFORMA the cost of construction and installation works is determined after a site inspection, and the estimate is based on the design or photos of the facade.

Recommendations

Brief recommendations for choosing facade stone:

  1. For plinths, steps and landings: granite, basalt or the densest dolomites and quartz sandstones with a rough finish.
  2. For walls of a private house in central Russia: dense dolomite, sandstone, travertine; granite if maximum durability is needed.
  3. For a multi-storey building: a ventilated rainscreen facade with calculated fixings; stone is chosen by flexural strength and weight.
  4. For prestigious portals and columns: marble or granite; marble bearing in mind the loss of polish outdoors.
  5. For southern regions and warm tones: limestone, dolomite, shell limestone with mandatory water-repellent treatment.
  6. For complex sculpted elements (cornices, capitals, large details): consider GFRC, which reproduces the look of stone but is lighter and allows any shape.
  7. Before ordering: test reports, samples from the current batch and a layout.

FAQ

Which stone is best for a private house facade?

For the plinth, granite or basalt; for walls, dense dolomite, sandstone or travertine. The final choice depends on the climate, finish and batch characteristics.

Which natural stone is the most durable outdoors?

Granite and basalt: they have very low water absorption and high frost and acid resistance.

Can marble and travertine be used on a facade?

Yes, on a rainscreen system, bearing in mind that the polished surface of carbonate rocks dulls outdoors over time.

What matters more when choosing stone: the rock type or its characteristics?

The characteristics of the specific batch: water absorption, frost resistance and strength from the test report. Variation within one rock type can be large.

How can I save on a stone facade?

Combine materials: durable stone on the plinth and at the entrance, and more affordable materials or GFRC on the main surface.
