There is no universally best stone for a facade: the choice depends on the building zone, climate and budget. For plinths, steps and regions with frequent freeze-thaw cycles, granite and basalt are the most reliable. For walls in a temperate climate, dense dolomites, limestones, sandstones and travertine work well. Marble suits details and lower floors, shell limestone (coquina) suits sheltered areas and dry climates, and onyx is for interiors only.

The main criterion is not the name of the stone but the properties of the specific batch: water absorption, frost resistance and strength from the test report.

This table is useful for architects at the concept stage, developers choosing finishes and private clients comparing cladding options. Each stone is covered in detail in the Natural stone for facades and interiors section.

Comparison Criteria

Natural stone for facades is best compared on several parameters.

  • Water absorption is the key outdoor indicator. The less water a stone absorbs, the less it suffers from frost, soiling and efflorescence.
  • Frost resistance is the number of freeze-thaw cycles without loss of strength. Requirements for plinths and horizontal surfaces are higher than for walls.
  • Strength in compression and bending determines the permissible slab thickness, format size and fixing method.
  • Density determines the weight of the cladding and the load on the substructure and wall.
  • Acid resistance matters in cities: carbonate stones (marble, limestone, travertine, dolomite) gradually lose their polish, while silicate stones (granite, basalt, quartz sandstone) do not.
  • Workability: soft stones are easy to cut and profile; hard stones need diamond tools but last longer.
  • Appearance and batch consistency: colour, pattern, and the ability to obtain the required volume in one tone.

All numerical values depend on the quarry. For a design, values are taken from the product data sheet or test report, not from averaged reference data.

A separate question is natural versus artificial stone. Cement-based artificial stone is lighter and cheaper, but its durability depends on the mix, and its colour is often set by pigment in the surface layer. Natural stone is more expensive and heavier, but its properties are confirmed by test reports and its appearance changes slowly. For multi-storey facades with complex decoration there is a third option: GFRC with a stone texture. It does not replace genuine natural texture, but it allows lightweight cornices and columns.

Climate

Climate determines how strict the requirements for stone must be.

  • Central and northern Russia. Many freeze-thaw cycles, wet snow, de-icing salts. Granite or basalt for plinths and horizontal surfaces; dense stones with low water absorption and proven frost resistance for walls. Porous stones only with water-repellent treatment and on a rainscreen system.
  • Southern Russia and the Caucasus. Less frost, more sun and day-night temperature swings. Limestones, dolomites, sandstones and travertine work well here; shell limestone is traditionally used for walls. For dark stones on sunny facades, heating is taken into account.
  • Coastal areas. Sea salt destroys porous stone and leaves efflorescence. Dense stones and regular washing are preferable.
  • Cities with polluted air. Carbonate stones lose their polish, so matt finishes or silicate stones are chosen.

Regardless of climate, construction details strongly affect durability: sills, the perimeter apron, damp-proof courses and the ventilated cavity often matter more than the difference between two similar stones.

For stone laid in mortar, porous stones need a damp-proof course at the plinth and a compatible mortar: an overly strong and dense cement mortar forces moisture and salts to leave through the stone rather than through the joints.

Budget

The budget for a stone facade is made up of more than the price of the stone itself. It includes:

  • the cost of the stone, which depends on the type, quarry, colour, format and thickness;
  • processing: cutting to the layout, surface finishing, kerfs, chamfers;
  • substructure and fixings: heavy stone requires a stronger substructure;
  • installation and logistics.

General patterns: hard stones (granite, basalt) cost more to process; soft stones are cheaper to process but may need a greater thickness. Imported stone in rare colours costs more than local stones. Large formats cost more to process and install than standard tiles.

A sensible way to manage the budget is to combine materials: granite or basalt on the plinth and at the entrance, and more affordable stone or stone-look GFRC on the main planes. At TECHNOFORMA the cost of construction and installation work is determined after a site inspection, and the estimate is based on the design or photos of the facade.

Recommendations

Short recommendations for choosing facade stone:

  1. For plinths, steps and landings: granite, basalt or the densest dolomites and quartz sandstones with a textured finish.
  2. For the walls of a private house in central Russia: dense dolomite, sandstone, travertine; granite if the maximum durability margin is needed.
  3. For a multi-storey building: a ventilated rainscreen facade with engineered fixings; stone is chosen by flexural strength and weight.
  4. For prestigious portals and columns: marble or granite; marble with the loss of polish outdoors in mind.
  5. For southern regions and warm tones: limestone, dolomite, shell limestone, always with water-repellent treatment.
  6. For complex sculptural forms (cornices, capitals, large details), consider GFRC: it reproduces the look of stone but is lighter and allows any shape.
  7. Before ordering: test reports, samples from the current batch and a layout.

Typical mistakes when choosing stone:

  • choosing from a photo or showroom sample, without test reports or a sample from the supplied batch;
  • a polished surface on steps and landings, which is slippery in winter and rain;
  • porous stone on the plinth without water-repellent treatment and protection from de-icing salts;
  • ordering without a margin for tone matching and breakage during processing;
  • thin slabs of soft stone on a rainscreen system without a flexural calculation;
  • mixing batches of different tone on one plane.

When natural stone is not the best choice: when the load-bearing capacity of the walls is limited and they cannot be strengthened, for large complex sculptural forms at height, and under tight deadlines when the required volume in one tone cannot be sourced from stock. Stone masonry (rubble, flagstone, sawn tiles) is chosen for low-rise buildings and plinths; for multi-storey buildings, slabs on a substructure as rainscreen cladding.

The zone adjoining the ground and the entrance area stand apart: here mechanical loads are added to the weather, namely impacts from trolleys, footwear and cleaning equipment, and salt in winter. Even when the main field of the wall is in a soft rock, it makes sense to clad the first 40–60 cm above the perimeter paving and the reveals of the entrance doors in granite or basalt of a similar tone. How much cladding in different rocks weighs and what that means for the wall

The weight of a square metre of cladding is determined by the density of the rock and the thickness of the slab, and the difference between rocks reaches two to three times. The weight governs the load-bearing capacity required of the substrate, the bracket spacing of the subframe, the type of anchors and the cost of logistics.

Dolomiteabout 69–84about 92–112

The figures are calculated from density ranges and given for comparison; the actual density is taken from the batch certificate. Bear in mind that soft rocks on a ventilated facade system need greater thickness, so a 'light' limestone in a 40 mm slab may turn out heavier than granite at 30 mm. The subframe, insulation and fixings are added to the weight of the stone.

If the load-bearing capacity of the wall is limited, it is reasonable to make some of the details, such as cornices, string courses and surrounds, in thin-walled glass fibre reinforced concrete. A comparison of the two materials is given in the article GFRC or natural stone.

How the rock affects the fixing method and slab thickness

The rock determines not only the appearance but also the permissible installation scheme. The key indicator here is flexural strength: a slab fixed at a few points and loaded by wind works precisely in bending.

  • Granite and basalt allow the smallest thickness and large formats, and hold concealed fixings in kerfs and on anchors in the back face well.
  • Marble and dense dolomite are installed on the same systems, but format and thickness are selected by calculation; with marble, possible bedding and non-uniformity are taken into account.
  • Travertine and limestone require increased thickness and a greater edge distance for slots; travertine slabs are oriented with regard to the direction of bedding, and cavities in the fixing zone are unacceptable.
  • Sandstone behaves differently depending on its cement: quartz grades are close to dense carbonate rocks, while clay-cemented ones are not used on a ventilated facade system.
  • Shell limestone is used in masonry or as thick tiles on adhesive on low-rise buildings; it is not suitable for thin ventilated cladding.

The seismicity of the area adds one more constraint. In seismic zones the aim is to lighten the cladding and divide it into smaller formats, and the fixing is designed to allow relative movement between the slabs and the substrate. Heavy rocks of great thickness and adhesive fixing of large slabs at height are used here with caution and only on the basis of calculation.

Adhesive fixing is limited by height and slab weight: at height it is, as a rule, supplemented with mechanical restraint. For porous rocks the adhesive method is also worse in that moisture from the mortar and the wall escapes through the stone, carrying salts with it. The relationship between rock, format and slab section is examined in more detail in the article on the thickness of cladding slabs.

The finish also affects how comparable the samples are. One and the same rock looks darker and more contrasting when polished than after bush-hammering or flaming, so candidates should be compared in the finish that will go on the facade. Samples are viewed outdoors, dry and wet, in daylight: indoors under artificial lighting the tone and pattern are perceived differently.

How to read a test report when comparing stone types

Comparing stone types for a facade from reference tables gives only a first approximation. The real choice is made from batch test reports, and they must be read in the same way for all candidates.

  1. Water absorption. Values obtained by the same method are compared; for a facade the aim is the lowest among the available options of the required appearance.
  2. Compressive strength in the dry and water-saturated state. The ratio of the second to the first is the softening coefficient: the closer it is to one, the better the stone tolerates wetting.
  3. Frost resistance. The grade shows the number of cycles the specimens withstood; for the plinth and horizontal details the stone is taken with a margin relative to the walls.
  4. Flexural strength. It is needed to design ventilated cladding; without it the slab thickness cannot be set correctly.
  5. Abrasion resistance. Important for steps, landings and paving.
  6. Specific activity of natural radionuclides. For residential and public buildings the stone must belong to the first class; for granites this indicator is always checked.
  7. Date and subject of the tests. The report must relate to the same deposit and, preferably, to the current extraction period: properties vary from bed to bed.

It is convenient to bring the reports together in one table for the project: the rows are the candidates, the columns the indicators, with a separate column for the design requirement for each zone. This shows at once which stone qualifies on all points, which is suitable only for walls and which drops out. The same table is then attached to the justification of the choice for the client and the technical supervisor.

If there is no report for one of the candidates, it is not compared 'by analogy' with the rock in general: tests are ordered or it is excluded from consideration. What the grades mean and how to relate them to climate is explained in the article on frost resistance of facade materials.

How different rocks age on a facade and what care they need

After ten to twenty years, facades in different rocks look different, even if they were equally flawless in the year of handover. This is worth taking into account in the choice on a par with price.

RockWhat happens over timeTypical care
Granite, basaltPractically unchanged; dust and streaks show on a polished finishPeriodic washing
MarbleThe polish turns matt; light grades may yellow and streakWashing with neutral products, water-repellent treatment, re-polishing of details if necessary
TravertinePores clog with dust; pore filler may break outWashing, making good the filler, impregnation
Limestone, dolomiteA patina forms; darkening and biological growth in wetted zonesWater-repellent treatment, biocidal treatment, checking the flashings
SandstoneThe tone deepens; efflorescence and moss are possible on porous gradesImpregnation, gentle cleaning without a narrow jet
Shell limestoneThe surface weathers and darkens where wettedRegular renewal of protection, local repair

Carbonate rocks must not be cleaned with acid products; for silicate rocks the choice of chemicals is wider, but the compounds are still tried on an inconspicuous area. The interval between water-repellent treatments depends on the composition and the orientation of the wall and is usually a few years. When comparing options it is useful to assess access to the facade in advance: on a tall building every wash and impregnation requires hoists or rope-access technicians, and an undemanding rock pays back the difference in price.

Choosing a stone type for a project: a step-by-step procedure

The choice of rock for a facade is easiest to make from constraints towards appearance, not the other way round. This order reduces the number of options to two or three even before samples are requested.

  1. Divide the facade into zones: plinth, walls, entrance area, horizontal details, ornament.
  2. Record the conditions: regional climate, proximity of a road and the sea, number of storeys, load-bearing capacity of the walls.
  3. Determine the fixing system by zone and the permissible mass of the cladding.
  4. Using the tables, shortlist the rocks that qualify on water absorption, frost resistance and strength for each zone.
  5. From those remaining, choose by colour, pattern and finish; request samples from the current batch and test reports.
  6. Check the availability of the volume in a single tone and the delivery times; for imported rocks allow for logistics.
  7. Cost the option including processing, subframe and installation, not only the price of the stone per square metre.
  8. Approve the reference sample and proceed to the layout.

At each step it is useful to record the reason why an option was excluded: when the budget or the programme changes, rejected rocks are sometimes reconsidered. If the project envisages imported stone, a fallback option from rocks of a similar tone available in stock is worked up in parallel, in case of a delay in delivery or a change of batch at the quarry.

In practice a building is seldom left with a single rock: more often it is two stones, or stone combined with another material. The most versatile option for the lower zone is described on the page granite for facades and plinths.

FAQ

Which stone is best for the facade of a private house?

Granite or basalt for the plinth; dense dolomite, sandstone or travertine for the walls. The final choice depends on climate, finish and the properties of the batch.

Which natural stone is the most durable outdoors?

Granite and basalt: they have very low water absorption and high frost and acid resistance.

Can marble and travertine be used on a facade?

Yes, on a rainscreen system, bearing in mind that the polished surface of carbonate stones dulls over time outdoors.

What matters more when choosing stone: the type or the properties?

The properties of the specific batch: water absorption, frost resistance and strength from the test report. The spread within one stone type can be large.

How can I save money on a stone facade?

Combine materials: durable stone on the plinth and at the entrance, and more affordable materials or GFRC on the main planes.

Which is better for a facade: natural or artificial stone?

Natural stone is more expensive and heavier, but its properties are confirmed by test reports and it keeps its appearance for a long time. Artificial stone is lighter and cheaper, but its durability depends on the mix. For complex decoration on multi-storey buildings, stone-textured GFRC is often chosen.

Can several stone types be combined on one facade?

Yes, this is a common solution: resistant stone in the plinth and on horizontals, and a softer, warmer-toned stone on the walls. Bear in mind that rocks age at different rates, and water running off carbonate stone can leave light streaks on dark granite below. Flashings or string courses with a drip are therefore provided between the zones.

Which stone gets least dirty on a facade?

Dense rocks with low water absorption, granite and basalt, soil less and wash more easily. Among them, mottled and mid-toned grades with a matt finish are more practical: dust and rain marks do not show on them. Light porous rocks darken more noticeably and require water-repellent treatment.

Does the colour of the stone affect how much the facade heats up?

It does. Dark stone on the sunny side heats up considerably more than light stone, which increases the thermal movement of the slabs. This is allowed for in the joint width, the type of fixing and the choice of sealants; on a ventilated facade system with open joints the problem is easier to solve than with adhesive fixing.
