Natural Stone in Landscaping: Paths, Steps, Kerbs

Natural stone for landscaping includes setts, paving slabs, steps, kerbs, flagstone, boulders and crushed stone from natural rocks. It lasts for decades, does not fade, withstands loads and blends naturally into gardens, courtyards and public spaces.

The key selection criterion is the frost resistance and water absorption of the rock combined with a proper base: even the strongest granite will subside on a poorly prepared base, and porous stone will break down even on a good one.

The material is chosen by private homeowners, landscape architects and developers for landscaping projects. An overview of rocks is in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Applications

Where natural stone is used in landscaping:

  • Paths and pavements: sawn slabs, setts, flagstone, large stepping stones in lawns.
  • Patios and terraces: seating areas, terraces, pool surrounds.
  • Driveways and parking areas: setts and thick slabs of strong rocks.
  • Steps and stairs: solid blocks or cladding treads and risers.
  • Kerbs and edging: framing paths, lawns and flower beds.
  • Retaining walls and terracing: dry-stone or mortared walls.
  • Decorative features: rock gardens, dry stream beds, pond edging.

Stone Setts

Stone setts are rectangular or cubic units laid tightly together or with a narrow joint. They are the strongest and most durable surface for pedestrian areas and roadways.

Types of stone setts:

  • Sawn: all faces flat, tight laying, comfortable to walk on.
  • Sawn-split: sawn sides and a split top face; textured, non-slip, a classic look.
  • Split: all faces split, the most historic look, uneven surface.
  • Tumbled: with rounded edges, “antiqued”.
  • Flamed: sawn with a rough top face.

Common sizes are 100×100×50 mm, 100×100×100 mm, 200×100×50–60 mm and others; thickness is chosen for the load. The main material is granite; basalt is also used, and less often sandstone and dense dolomite for pedestrian areas.

Setts are laid on a prepared base: excavation, geotextile, a compacted crushed-stone layer and a levelling layer of sand or dry mix. For trafficked areas the base is reinforced, in some cases with a concrete slab. Joints are filled with sand or special jointing compounds for setts. Falls and drainage are essential: water must not stand on the surface.

Unlike concrete pavers, stone setts do not wear down to grit or lose their colour: after decades they look the same or even better, acquiring a natural patina.

Steps

Stone steps are one of the most critical landscape elements: they are walked on every day, and in winter over snow and ice.

  • Solid blocks: massive steps of solid stone, usually granite. They are laid on a prepared base and last almost forever.
  • Cladding on a concrete base: treads 30–50 mm thick and risers 20–30 mm on a concrete flight.
  • Flagstone and natural slabs: for garden paths with changes in level.

Requirements: a non-slip finish (flamed, bush-hammered, sandblasted or natural split), a 1–2% fall on the tread away from the building for drainage, and anti-slip grooves or inserts at the nosing. Step height and tread width should be comfortable to walk; outdoor stairs are usually made with a gentler pitch than indoor ones.

Clad steps need a reliable concrete base on a non-frost-susceptible bed, separated from the house structure by a movement joint. If the base settles or cracks, no stone will save the stairs. Tread nosings are chamfered or rounded: they chip less and are safer.

Kerbs

A kerb separates the path surface from the lawn or roadway, holds the edge of the paving and stops it spreading.

  • Road kerb: tall, installed along a driveway, takes wheel impacts. Material: granite.
  • Garden edging: low, for paths, lawns and flower beds. Granite, basalt, sandstone, dolomite.
  • Radius kerb: for curved paths, made to a template.

A kerb is set on a concrete base and secured on the back with a concrete haunch. Natural stone is stronger than a concrete kerb, does not chip and needs no replacement for decades. The finish can be sawn, split or flamed, depending on the style of the landscaping.

The kerb is installed before the paving is laid: it serves as a guide and a restraint. The top of garden edging is usually set flush with the paving or a few centimetres higher, so that water does not run off the lawn onto the path and a lawnmower can pass along the edge unobstructed.

Frost Resistance

Frost resistance is the key characteristic of landscaping stone. A horizontal surface gets wet from rain and melting snow and then freezes; in central Russia there are dozens of such cycles per season. Water in the pores expands as it freezes and destroys weak stone.

The base under stone paving: what the build-up consists of and how to drain it

The service life of stone paving is determined first and foremost by the base: the load-bearing layer spreads the load, while drainage and falls keep water from standing beneath the stone. The build-up is selected according to the load and the soil type; there is no universal recipe.

On frost-susceptible clay soils the crushed-stone layer is made thicker and is always separated from the subsoil with a geotextile. The fall of the surface is usually set at 1–2% towards a lawn, a channel or a gully. Large areas can hardly do without linear drainage: water standing in the joints works to destroy the paving in winter.

Paving slabs and flagstone: how do they differ from setts?

Paving slabs are sawn units of regular shape and constant thickness; flagstone consists of natural plates of irregular outline obtained by splitting layered rock. Slabs give an even, formal surface, flagstone a free natural pattern.

Sawn slabs are convenient wherever geometry matters: terraces, entrance areas, paths along the facade. Common formats are 300×600, 400×600 and 600×600 mm; free lengths at a fixed width are also available. The larger the slab, the thicker it has to be and the stricter the requirements for the evenness of the base: a large format without continuous support breaks under a point load.

Sandstone or quartzite flagstone is laid by matching the outlines, so the joints are wider and more visible. It suits garden areas and secondary paths but is not suitable for driveways. Where small-unit paving for traffic is required, it is worth considering natural stone setts.

Retaining walls, parapets and plinth cladding

Stone on the vertical elements of a site works differently from paving: protection from water from above and from the soil side matters more here than abrasion resistance. A retaining wall is designed as a structure, and the stone in it is either the load-bearing material (dry-stone walling, rubble) or a cladding over concrete.

  • Dry-stone walling — low walls, usually up to 0.8–1 m, battered towards the soil and with a drainage backfill behind the wall.
  • Cladding over in-situ concrete — for high walls; waterproofing and drainage are provided behind the wall, and weep holes are left in its body.
  • Coping slabs — with an overhang and a drip groove so that water does not run down the face.

Walls and enclosures visually tie the site to the building, so the stone type and finish are usually coordinated with the lower tier of the house. The requirements for this zone are discussed in detail in the article on cladding the plinth with stone.

How to match landscape stone with the building facade?

Landscaping looks most coherent when the natural stone in the landscape repeats the stone type or tone of the facade but differs in finish and format. A honed or sawn surface suits the wall; a flamed or bush-hammered one suits the ground.

The usual practice is this: one principal stone is used for the entrance area, the steps and the plinth, and a simpler material of a similar shade is chosen for the more distant paths. Warm facades of travertine and limestone are well supported by sandstone in yellow and beige tones, grey and dark facades by granite for paving and steps. More than three stone types on one site usually look mottled.

If the facade and the landscaping are designed at the same time, the paving layout is tied to the axes of the building: the slab joints continue the divisions of the facade, and the width of the steps is a multiple of the slab format. This reduces cutting and waste.

Typical mistakes in stone paving

Most defects in stone paving are related not to the stone type but to the design and the laying. Below are the mistakes encountered most often.

  1. A thin slab under traffic load. Stone 2–3 cm thick on a driveway may crack within the first season.
  2. No falls and no drainage: puddles, ice, deterioration of the joints.
  3. A polished surface outdoors. It is slippery on wet and icy stone and is not used for external steps and paths.
  4. Laying on mortar over unprepared soil: a rigid covering on a moving base cracks.
  5. A porous stone in the zone of splashes and de-icing agents without checking its frost resistance.
  6. Paving tight against the facade without a movement joint and without draining water away from the wall.
  7. Ordering stone without a reserve: when more is bought from another batch, the shade almost always differs.

Stairs are a separate subject: they have their own requirements for tread dimensions and slip resistance, described in the article on external stone steps.

Special conditions: stone by water, in car parks and in public spaces

In certain areas of a site additional requirements apply to the stone, and a material that serves well on a garden path may not be suitable here. Three such cases are worth identifying in advance.

Swimming pool and pond

Around the basin a surface is needed that is rough yet does not hurt bare feet: a bush-hammered, brushed or other slip-resistant finish is usually chosen. Light stone heats up less in the sun; dark granite and basalt become hot in summer in southern regions. Coping slabs are specified with a rounded edge, and the stone is checked for resistance to chlorinated water: dense limestones and travertine behave differently, so it is better to test a sample on site.

Car parks and driveways

Here oils, fuel and de-icing agents are added to the wheel load. Dark grey granite and basalt with a flamed surface are the most practical: stains are less visible on them, and the dense structure absorbs almost no liquid. For light stones in a parking area it makes sense to provide a protective impregnation and regular cleaning.

Public spaces and courtyards of residential complexes

On squares, embankments and in the courtyards of apartment buildings the passage of cleaning and fire-fighting vehicles is taken into account, so even pedestrian areas are designed for traffic load. The paving is complemented by tactile strips, dropped kerbs and drainage channels in the same stone. Landscaping work is usually planned for the warm season and carried out after the facade work is finished and the scaffolding removed, so that the finished paving is not damaged by machinery or soiled with mortar.

Care of stone paving and what to prepare for an estimate

Natural stone in the landscape ages slowly and acquires a patina over time, which many clients regard as a merit rather than a defect. The aim of care is not to make the paving look new again but to preserve its evenness, clean joints and safety for pedestrians.

Stone paving does not need complicated maintenance: seasonal cleaning, topping up the joints and timely removal of stains are enough. Once a year the paving is inspected after the winter: areas that have settled are relaid locally without waiting for the deformation to spread.

Organic dirt and moss are removed with a brush and water at moderate pressure; acid cleaners are not used on limestone, dolomite and marble. A white bloom on new paving is most often caused by salts from the mortar or the base — the causes and removal methods are described in the article on efflorescence on stone. Metal shovels and crowbars chip the edges; plastic tools are better for clearing snow.

A preliminary supply estimate requires:

  • a site plan with the dimensions of the paved areas;
  • the purpose of each area: pedestrian, car traffic or goods vehicles;
  • the desired stone type, finish and format, or reference photographs;
  • the number and dimensions of steps and the length of kerbs;
  • the region of construction and the date by which the material is needed.

FAQ

Which stone is best for garden paths?

Granite, basalt, quartz sandstone or dense dolomite, with a rough finish and at least 30 mm thick for footpaths.

How thick should setts be for a driveway?

For cars, 60 mm or more; for footpaths, 30–50 mm is sufficient.

Can limestone be used for paths?

Only dense grades and mainly in southern regions; in central Russia granite or sandstone is more reliable.

Which finish should be chosen for outdoor steps?

A non-slip one: flamed, bush-hammered, sandblasted or natural split.

What should be spread on stone paths in winter?

Sand or granite chippings. Salt de-icers destroy the stone and the joints.

Can stone be laid on a concrete base?

Yes, a rigid base is used for driveways, car parks and areas with thin large-format slabs. The concrete slab is made with a fall and movement joints, and the stone is laid on mortar or an adhesive for external use. It is important to drain water from the surface of the slab, otherwise moisture under the stone will lead to debonding and efflorescence.

What are the joints between paving slabs filled with?

When laying on sand or stone dust, the joints are filled with washed sand or fine stone dust, which is topped up as it washes out. When laying on mortar, a frost-resistant grout for external use is applied. Wide flagstone joints in garden areas are sometimes sown with lawn grass.

What reserve of stone should be allowed for when ordering?

Usually 5–10% for a straight layout of regular-format slabs and more for diagonal laying, flagstone and curved paths. The exact percentage is determined from the layout drawing. The reserve is best ordered at once, from the same batch.
