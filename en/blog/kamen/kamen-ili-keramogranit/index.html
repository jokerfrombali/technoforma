Natural Stone or Porcelain Stoneware on a Facade

Choosing between natural stone and porcelain stoneware for a facade is a choice between a unique natural texture and industrial predictability. Natural stone has an inimitable pattern, ages gracefully and can be repaired: a slab can be reground. Porcelain stoneware is consistent from batch to batch, lighter for a comparable format, stable in its properties and usually easier to procure.

The main criterion is the purpose of the facade. If the building is prestigious, its appearance must last for decades and the budget allows, stone is chosen. If schedule, repeatability, large areas and budget control matter, porcelain stoneware. Many projects combine them: stone on the plinth and entrances, porcelain stoneware and GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) on the main wall areas.

This guide is for architects, developers and general contractors. A general overview of stones is in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Appearance

Natural stone is valued because every slab is unique: depth of colour, crystals, veining, natural variations in tone. On a facade this gives a living surface that looks different at different times of day. The downside is variability: slabs from different blocks differ, so a layout and batch selection are needed.

Porcelain stoneware is a manufactured material sintered from clays and mineral additives. Modern digital printing can imitate marble, granite, travertine, limestone, concrete and wood. Up close the imitation is noticeable: the pattern repeats every few tiles, the edge shows a body of a different colour (unless the material is through-body coloured), and the surface texture is shallower. On the other hand, porcelain stoneware is uniform: all tiles in a batch are identical, which suits strict modular architecture.

  • Stone: for classical and neoclassical facades, entrances, plinths, and buildings where the material must read well up close.
  • Porcelain stoneware: for modular contemporary facades and large areas where an even tone matters.

Durability

Both materials are durable if chosen for the climate and installed correctly, but their weak points differ.

ParameterNatural stonePorcelain stoneware
Water absorptionFrom very low (granite) to high (limestone, shell limestone), depending on the stoneVery low, consistent across the batch
Frost resistanceDepends on the quarry, confirmed by a test reportHigh for facade ranges
FadingDoes not fade, but may darken and soil without protectionDoes not fade
Surface repairCan be ground, polished, abrasive-cleanedA damaged tile is replaced
VulnerabilitiesAcid rain for marble and limestone, efflorescence on porous stonesBrittle on impact, chipped edges
MaintenanceWater-repellent treatment of porous stonesPeriodic washing

Exact values are taken from the product certificate or test report. Granite on a facade lasts for centuries, as historic buildings show. Porcelain stoneware has been used on ventilated facades for several decades, and its durability depends primarily on the quality of the substructure and fixings.

Weight

The weight of the cladding determines the substructure, the load on the wall and the number of anchors. Facade porcelain stoneware is usually 8–12 mm thick, while natural stone on a ventilated facade is more often 20–40 mm, because a thin stone slab is brittle and cannot withstand wind load with mechanical fixing. The densities of granite and porcelain stoneware are comparable, so the main difference is thickness: a square metre of stone cladding weighs several times more than porcelain stoneware.

Practical consequences:

  • stone needs a heavier substructure, more often steel or reinforced aluminium, with more anchors;
  • the load-bearing capacity of the substrate must be checked, especially on aerated concrete and hollow brick;
  • installing heavy slabs is slower and requires lifting equipment;
  • on high-rise buildings weight becomes a limiting factor.

If the look of stone is needed with low weight, GFRC with a stone texture is considered: at a thickness of about 10–15 mm it is noticeably lighter, and three-dimensional shapes are possible.

Cost

This article gives no exact prices: the cost of a facade is determined after a site inspection and calculation. But the cost structure of the two materials differs.

  • Material. Stone is more expensive on average, but the range is huge: mass-market Chinese granite can cost about the same as premium porcelain stoneware, while a rare marble or onyx costs several times more.
  • Processing and waste. Stone is cut to size, edges are finished and kerfs are milled; when matching the pattern, some slabs go to waste. Porcelain stoneware comes in standard formats.
  • Substructure. For stone it is heavier and more expensive, with more anchors.
  • Installation. Stone slabs are heavier and take longer to install.
  • Operation. Porous stones need periodic water-repellent treatment, but the surface is repairable.

So compare not the price of the slab but the cost per square metre of the finished facade including substructure and installation, plus maintenance costs over the service life.

How to choose

How to decide:

  1. Define the status of the building. Prestige buildings, hotels and club houses usually benefit from stone, at least on the plinth and entrances.
  2. Check the load-bearing capacity of the wall and the height of the building: this may immediately rule out heavy cladding.
  3. Assess the schedule. Mass-market porcelain stoneware is available faster; stone is too if the item is in stock.
  4. Decide what matters more: uniformity or uniqueness.
  5. Consider a combination. Stone for the plinth, portals and columns; porcelain stoneware for standard wall areas; GFRC for complex ornament, cornices and window surrounds.
  6. Check a visualisation with real samples before purchasing.

For a private house the logic is the same on a smaller scale: natural stone is more often chosen for the plinth, entrance steps and feature walls, where it is seen up close and resistance to abrasion and impact matters, while lighter materials are used for large areas. For multi-storey housing and business centres, weight, delivery times and repeatability are usually decisive, so stone is used selectively. In any case the final choice is made after calculating the substructure and comparing the cost per finished square metre for several options.

Natural stone and porcelain stoneware: comparison by technical parameters

In terms of basic indicators, namely non-combustibility, frost resistance and UV resistance, both materials are suitable for facades. They differ in slab thickness, stability of size and colour, edge behaviour and repair options. The figures in the table are indicative and depend on the particular stone and manufacturer.

ParameterNatural stonePorcelain stoneware
Facade slab thicknessUsually 20–40 mmUsually 8–12 mm
Water absorptionFractions of a per cent for granite, noticeably higher for travertine and limestoneAs a rule, no more than 0.5%
CombustibilityNon-combustibleNon-combustible
FormatsCut from the slab; any sizes and special pieces are possibleFixed factory formats, trimmed on site
Colour repeatabilityNatural variation; batch selection is neededStable within the shade and calibre of a batch
Three-dimensional elementsSolid corners, profiled pieces, carvingFlat tile only; corners are mitred or closed with a profile
Surface renewalRe-honing, re-polishing, local restorationTile replacement only

The table shows that porcelain stoneware wins on predictability, while stone wins on freedom of form and repairability. The properties of individual stones differ more than is commonly assumed, so before choosing it is worth looking at the comparison of stone types by density and water absorption.

How does the fixing of stone and porcelain stoneware on a subframe differ?

Both materials are installed on a ventilated system with an air gap, but their fixing details differ: a thin ceramic tile is held by its edge or by its back, while a stone slab is more often held by its edge thickness. This affects the appearance of the facade, the spacing of the rails and the labour involved.

  • Porcelain stoneware on clamps. The most common and economical method. The clamp tabs are visible on the facade and are painted to match the tile. The joint is usually a few millimetres wide.
  • Porcelain stoneware on concealed fixings. The tile is fixed by its back, on anchors in undercut holes or on adhesive systems with mechanical back-up. The surface is clean, but the requirements for tile thickness and installation accuracy increase.
  • Stone on concealed edge fixings. Kerfs or holes for pins are made in the slab edges; the fixings are not visible. The method requires sufficient thickness and uniform stone without cracks near the edge.
  • Stone on rear anchors. Used for large formats and critical areas: each slab is hung independently and can be replaced.

The subframe for stone is designed for a greater mass: reinforced brackets, a closer rail spacing and, where necessary, stainless steel are more common. For porcelain stoneware the structure is lighter and cheaper. The make-up and the stages of work are described in detail on the page about installing ventilated facades.

How the materials behave under impact, soiling and repair

In service, what matters is not surface hardness but what happens to the slab after damage. Porcelain stoneware is hard and does not absorb dirt, but a thin tile cracks under a strong point impact and cannot be repaired. Stone is thicker; a chip often remains local and can be filled or re-honed.

Dirt washes off porcelain stoneware more easily: the dense body does not absorb water or dust, and graffiti is removed with solvents. Porous stones such as limestone, travertine and sandstone need water-repellent treatment and more careful washing, but they age evenly and acquire a patina over time rather than looking worn. Dense granite is close to ceramics in this respect.

When a single tile is replaced, porcelain stoneware raises the problem of shade: collections are updated, and a few years later exactly the same tile is hard to find. A repair stock is therefore set aside from the start. Stone of the same type can be ordered years later, although the shade of a new batch will differ, so spare stock is needed here as well.

The behaviour of edges and corners deserves separate assessment. With porcelain stoneware that is not through-coloured, a cut edge differs in colour from the face, so external corners are closed with a profile or mitred, and such a joint is sensitive to impact. With stone, colour and structure are the same through the whole thickness: a corner can be made from a solid piece, and a chamfer or chip can be honed in place without loss of appearance.

Fire safety and the make-up of the ventilated system

Both natural stone and porcelain stoneware are non-combustible materials, so the cladding itself does not restrict use on high-rise and public buildings. The fire performance of a facade is determined by the system as a whole: the insulation, the wind barrier membrane, the subframe material and the design of the fire breaks.

In practice this means comparing not tile with slab but two complete sets: cladding, subframe, insulation and fixings. For taller buildings, non-combustible mineral wool insulation and a steel subframe are normally used, and the scope of application of a particular system is confirmed by its manufacturer's documents and by the design. A heavy stone slab places higher demands on brackets and anchors, which is taken into account in the calculation.

A separate issue is the behaviour of the cladding in a fire. A thin ceramic tile may crack under sudden heating and fall out of its fixings, so protective canopies or reinforced fixings are provided in the design above entrances and escape routes. A massive stone slab heats through more slowly, but its fall is more dangerous because of its weight, and the requirements for fixings above walkways are no less strict here. Specific solutions are determined by the design and by the regulations for the given building type.

How to join stone and porcelain stoneware on one facade

A combined facade works if the boundary between materials coincides with the architectural articulation: the plinth, a band above the ground floor, an entrance surround, a corner of the building. An arbitrary boundary in the middle of a surface exposes the difference in thickness and sheen.

  1. Define the transition level. Stone is usually taken up to plinth height or to the first or second storey, where the facade is seen at close range and is exposed to impact.
  2. Coordinate the joint module. The stone course height and the ceramic tile format should be multiples of each other, otherwise the joints will not align vertically.
  3. Resolve the step between planes. Stone is thicker, so the transition is covered by a flashing, a band or a moulding; GFRC elements are convenient for the band.
  4. Separate the subframes. Each material has its own bracket spacing and type, and the junction zones are worked out in the details.
  5. Match the tone using real samples outdoors, not a catalogue.

The lower tier is most often made of stone: its requirements are described in the article on plinth cladding.

What to choose for different building types

There is no universal answer: the choice is determined by the class of the building, its height, the budget and the distance from which the facade will be viewed. The guidelines by building type are as follows.

  • Mid-market residential complexes. The main field is usually porcelain stoneware, with stone at the plinth and entrances. This gives a durable base at a moderate budget.
  • Business-class and premium residential buildings, boutique residences. Stone over the full height or on the lower storeys combined with decorative elements; texture, solid corners and an individual pattern are valued here.
  • Business centres. Both options are possible. Large-format porcelain stoneware on concealed fixings gives a strict plane, while natural stone for the facade gives a prestigious entrance zone and podium.
  • Hotels and public buildings. More often stone in the zones accessible to visitors and lighter cladding above.
  • Private houses. With a small area, the difference in the overall estimate between the materials is smaller than it seems, and stone often proves an affordable solution.

Typical mistakes when choosing between stone and porcelain stoneware

Most mistakes arise because the material is chosen from a sample in the office and by tile price, without treating the facade as a system.

  • Comparing the price per square metre of tile without the subframe, fixings and installation.
  • Choosing marble-look porcelain stoneware with few pattern variants: on a large surface the repeat becomes noticeable.
  • Porous stone on a plinth next to a road without protection against de-icing agents.
  • Large format without checking wall tolerances and subframe capability.
  • No repair stock of slabs from the same batch.
  • Judging colour under artificial light rather than outdoors and in a wet state.

A mock-up helps to avoid them: several slabs of both materials installed on a fragment of the building's wall. Work with ceramic cladding is described on the page about porcelain stoneware facade cladding.

FAQ

How does stone-look porcelain stoneware on a facade differ from real stone?

The pattern is printed and repeats every few tiles, the edge is usually a different colour and the texture is shallower. On the other hand, the tiles are uniform, lighter and consistent in their properties.

Which lasts longer on a facade, stone or porcelain stoneware?

Granite and dense stones last for centuries and are repairable. Porcelain stoneware is also durable; the service life of such a facade is determined mainly by the substructure and fixings.

Which is cheaper, natural stone or porcelain stoneware?

On average porcelain stoneware, especially taking into account the lighter substructure and faster installation. But mass-market granites can be comparable in price; compare the cost of the finished facade.

Can stone and porcelain stoneware be combined on one facade?

Yes, this is a common solution: stone on the plinth and entrances, porcelain stoneware and GFRC on the main areas. The Dinastiya club house, for example, was built this way.

Can stone be replaced with porcelain stoneware in a completed facade design?

It can, but it is not a simple substitution of a line in the specification. The mass of the cladding, the fixing type, the rail spacing, the slab format and the junction details all change, so the subframe is recalculated and the layout is redone. If part of the subframe has already been installed for stone, it will as a rule be adequate in load-bearing capacity but will need different fixing components.

Do stone and porcelain stoneware fade in the sun?

The colour of porcelain stoneware is produced by firing and does not change in sunlight. Most granites are also stable. Some coloured marbles, limestones and sandstones shift slightly in shade over time and develop a patina; this is a natural process that is taken into account when choosing stone for south-facing facades.

Which material is more practical for the ground floor?

For a zone exposed to impact, de-icing agents and vandalism, dense stone is usually chosen, above all granite: the slab is thicker, a chip remains local and the surface can be restored. Porcelain stoneware is also used at ground-floor level, but a thin tile withstands point impacts less well and is replaced as a whole when damaged.
