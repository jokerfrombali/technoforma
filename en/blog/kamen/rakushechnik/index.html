Shell Limestone (Coquina): Properties and Facade Cladding

Shell limestone, or coquina, is a sedimentary rock made of the shells and shell fragments of marine organisms cemented together by calcium carbonate. It is a light, porous limestone in warm tones ranging from cream and sand to honey. In construction it is used both as a wall block and as a cladding slab for facades, plinths, fences and interiors.

The key criterion when choosing shell limestone is the density and uniformity of the specific batch: strength, water absorption and frost resistance all depend on it. Loose stone works well for walls in a dry climate, while facade cladding in a temperate climate calls for dense grades and mandatory water-repellent treatment.

The material is chosen by those who value a warm natural texture and an eco-friendly product: private homeowners, architects of resort and low-rise developments, and restorers. More on other stone types in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Origin

Shell limestone formed on the floor of warm, shallow seas. Mollusc shells, coral remains and other organisms with calcareous skeletons accumulated on the seabed, compacted and were cemented by calcite dissolved in the water. By origin, therefore, shell limestone is a biogenic (organogenic) sedimentary rock, a variety of limestone rather than a mineral: the main mineral in its composition is calcite.

Large shell limestone deposits are linked to ancient marine basins. In Russia and neighbouring regions it is found in Crimea, Dagestan, Krasnodar Krai and Rostov Oblast; the stone is widespread around the Black Sea and the Caspian. Dagestan varieties are a large topic of their own: there shell limestone occurs alongside dense limestones and dolomites.

Shell limestone is extracted by open-pit quarrying. The softness of the rock allows blocks to be sawn straight from the bedrock with disc-saw stone-cutting machines, without blasting. The same blocks are then cut on machines into cladding slabs of the required thickness.

Properties

The properties of shell limestone vary greatly depending on the deposit and the specific bed. The table below therefore gives typical ranges; exact values for a batch are stated in the product data sheet or test report.

Shell limestone is non-combustible, vapour-permeable and emits no harmful substances. It is easy to saw, drill and work with hand tools, and its rough, porous surface bonds well with render and masonry mortar. The flip side of porosity is inconsistency: a single block may contain voids and large shells.

Pros and Cons

It is easiest to decide on shell limestone by comparing its strengths and weaknesses side by side.

Pros:

  • expressive natural texture with visible shells, warm colours;
  • low weight compared with granite and marble, so less load on the wall and foundation;
  • vapour permeability: the wall “breathes” and does not accumulate condensation;
  • non-combustibility;
  • easy working: cutting, drilling and fitting on site;
  • good adhesion to mortars and adhesives.

Cons:

  • high water absorption: without protection the stone gets wet, darkens and develops efflorescence and moss;
  • limited frost resistance of loose grades;
  • low strength, so it is unsuitable for areas with mechanical loads;
  • inconsistent block geometry and density, meaning rejects during selection;
  • dusting and abrasion if the surface is not impregnated.

Bottom line: shell limestone is appropriate where texture and low weight are valued, provided the right grade is chosen and it is protected from moisture.

Cladding

Shell limestone for cladding is produced as sawn tiles and slabs. Typical thickness of shell limestone cladding tiles is 20 to 50 mm; for loose grades the slab is made thicker to keep it strong during cutting, transport and installation. The face can be sawn, honed, split (rock-faced) or “antiqued”.

There are two main ways of cladding with shell limestone:

  • Adhesive fixing to a prepared substrate: brick, concrete or aerated concrete with reinforced render. Frost-resistant adhesives for natural stone are used, with fully filled joints and movement joints.
  • Ventilated rainscreen facade for dense grades and large formats. Slabs are fixed to the substructure with clips or anchors; thickness and format are determined by calculation.

Before cladding work the stone is sorted by tone and texture and a layout is prepared so that the facade does not end up patchy with different batches. Near the ground, by the apron, shell limestone is best avoided or protected especially carefully: the plinth zone suffers most from splashes, snow and de-icing salts. For plinths a denser stone is usually chosen: granite, basalt or dense dolomite.

Shell limestone cladding tiles work well indoors: on lobby walls, fireplaces and lounge areas where there is no risk of constant wetting.

Protection

Protection is the main condition for the durability of shell limestone on a facade. The key measures are:

  1. Water-repellent treatment. Penetrating siloxane-based impregnations reduce water absorption without blocking vapour permeability. They are applied to clean, dry stone, usually in two coats, and renewed periodically.
  2. Design protection. Sills, parapets, canopies and projecting cornices, and drainage away from the plinth. Horizontal shell limestone surfaces should be covered or made of a denser stone.
  3. Capillary moisture barrier. Waterproofing between the foundation and the cladding so that the stone does not “draw” water from the ground.
  4. Joints. Filling with frost-resistant, vapour-permeable compounds, not high-strength cement mortars, which destroy soft stone at the contact surface.

Film-forming varnishes are undesirable on facades: moisture collects under the film and the surface spalls when it freezes. If a “wet stone” look is needed, colour-enhancing impregnations designed for exterior use are applied.

Grades of shell limestone: how cladding stone differs from walling stone

Shell limestone for cladding and shell limestone for wall masonry are materials of different density, although they are often quarried in the same pit. Suppliers conventionally divide the stone into grades by compressive strength; the higher the grade, the denser the structure, the finer the pores and the lower the water absorption.

Conventional gradeAppearanceWhere it is used
M15Coarse-pored, light yellow, crumbles easily at the arrisesNon-load-bearing partitions, frame infill, outbuildings; unsuitable for cladding
M25Medium porosity, shells distinguishable, arrises keep their shapeWalls of low-rise houses, tiles for interiors and sheltered parts of the facade
M35Dense, fine-grained, heavy, often white or greyishCladding tiles for facades, steps and details in dry zones

Apart from density, cladding stone is distinguished by format. Sawn tiles of regular geometry go on walls with even joints, slabs of increased thickness on a ventilated facade system, while blocks and half-blocks are sawn on site for reveals, corners and areas under windows. A split finish is rarely produced on shell limestone: because of the porosity, the split face comes out uneven and crumbles.

The grades reflect market practice rather than guaranteed properties: stone of the same grade differs from deposit to deposit. For facade tiles the reference is the test report for the specific batch, above all water absorption and frost resistance. Dense varieties are sawn into slabs of regular geometry; the range and formats are given on the page shell limestone for facades.

How to check a batch of shell limestone before purchase

The quality of shell limestone is assessed at the warehouse before payment: once it has been sawn and delivered, the stone is difficult to return. The check takes little time and requires no laboratory, although it does not replace a test report.

  • Weight. Tiles of one format from one batch should weigh roughly the same; noticeably light pieces come from a loose bed.
  • Sound. When tapped, dense stone rings clearly, while loose or cracked stone sounds dull.
  • Arrises. Try pressing the edge with a finger or a key: on a cladding grade it does not crumble.
  • Structure on the cut face. Large cavities, sandy and clayey lenses and through pores are grounds for rejection for a facade.
  • Geometry. Thickness and diagonals are measured on several tiles from different pallets; a wide spread will complicate installation and increase the joint width.
  • Tone. The stone is laid out side by side in a dry state: shell limestone from a single quarry ranges from almost white to yellow and pinkish.
  • Moisture. Freshly quarried stone is saturated with quarry sap and must dry out before installation, and all the more so before impregnation.

Why the absorption figure matters so much for porous rocks is explained in the article on water absorption of facade materials.

In which climate is shell limestone cladding justified?

Shell limestone performs best in a warm, dry climate with few freeze-thaw transitions. Historically it was used for building and cladding in the south, in Crimea and on the Black Sea and Caspian coasts, and that is where the most favourable service experience has been accumulated.

In seaside towns salt mist is added to wetting: salts crystallise in the pores and gradually loosen the surface, so water-repellent treatment and regular inspection are mandatory here. In the temperate zone, with its prolonged autumn and frequent thaws, facade shell limestone is used to a limited extent: dense grades, a ventilated facade system with an air gap, areas above the plinth and under the protection of cornices. In regions with severe winters it is more sensible to choose a rock with low water absorption and keep the shell limestone texture for interiors.

Apart from temperature, the orientation of the facade matters. Windward walls exposed to driving rain get several times wetter than sheltered ones; the densest stone in the batch is chosen for them and no savings are made on flashings. North-facing and shaded areas dry slowly, and algae and moss appear on them earlier, so biocidal treatment is carried out there more often.

The general approach to selecting materials by region is set out in the article on choosing a facade for the climate.

Where shell limestone is best avoided on a facade

The weak point of shell limestone is horizontal and permanently damp surfaces. Where water and snow linger on the stone, even a dense grade deteriorates noticeably faster than on a vertical wall.

  • The plinth in the splash zone and where snow is piled: granite, basalt or dense dolomite is preferable, and the shell limestone starts higher.
  • Parapet copings, window sills and the tops of balustrades: slabs of dense rock or metal flashings with a drip are needed.
  • Steps and porch landings: soft stone wears and becomes uneven.
  • Areas under downpipes and air-conditioning units, where the wall is regularly wetted.
  • Cladding taken into the ground or tight against the perimeter paving without a gap.
  • Walls beside a carriageway, where de-icing agents and traffic dirt reach the facade.

A combination of materials solves the problem without loss of appearance: the lower band and the horizontals are made of resistant stone, and the main field of the wall of shell limestone.

Typical mistakes in installing shell limestone

Most defects in shell limestone cladding are built in during installation and are related to moisture. The stone forgives geometric inaccuracies but tolerates unsuitable compounds and haste poorly.

  • Installing damp stone: moisture trapped by the adhesive and impregnation escapes through the face together with salts.
  • Hard, high-strength cement mortars for joints: the joint ends up stronger and denser than the stone, and the tile begins to fail at the edge.
  • Water-repellent treatment on a damp or dusty surface: the impregnation is uneven and lasts less than its rated life.
  • No movement joints on long walls and where the substrate changes.
  • Mechanical fixings that ignore the low strength: expansion anchors split the slab, so slots and holes are made with an increased edge distance, and slab thickness for a ventilated facade system is taken with a margin.
  • Cladding without checking the substrate: on weak old render the tile comes away together with the layer.

Adhesive is applied in a continuous layer to the substrate and to the back of the tile: because of its porosity, shell limestone quickly draws water out of the mortar, and a thin layer does not have time to gain strength. The back face is first cleaned of sawing dust. Light stone is bonded with compounds based on white cement, so that grey adhesive does not show through the pores and cause stains.

The selection and application of protective compounds is discussed in detail in the article on protecting natural stone.

Shell limestone, travertine or limestone: what to choose for walls

All three rocks are carbonate, light and warm in tone, so at concept stage they are often regarded as interchangeable. They differ in density, in the nature of their pores and in the way they age outdoors.

ParameterShell limestoneTravertineDense limestone
StructureVisible shells, open uneven porosityBanded pattern, elongated cavities, often filledUniform, fine-grained
Weight of claddingLowestMediumHighest of the three
Behaviour outdoorsNeeds protection and a mild climateDurable with the right system and filled poresMost predictable; dense grades are also suitable for a plinth
FinishingSawing, honing; does not take a polishHoning, fine honing, polishingHoning, fine honing, carving and profiles
Typical usePrivate houses, southern architecture, interiorsPublic and residential buildings, entrance areasClassical facades, profiled details

The choice also depends on the scale of the building. On a private house two or three storeys high, shell limestone on adhesive works without complex structures. On a multi-storey or public building, where the cladding is installed on a ventilated facade system designed for wind load, travertine or limestone with confirmed flexural strength is more often justified.

If the texture with shells is what matters, there is no alternative to shell limestone. When a facade of similar tone but greater durability is needed, consider travertine for cladding or the dense grades described in the article on limestone for facades.

Maintaining a shell limestone facade and repairing chips

A shell limestone facade needs more attentive care than a granite one, but all the operations are simple. The main task is to keep the stone from staying wet for long and to renew the protection in good time.

Once a year the joints, flashings and junctions are inspected, vegetation near the walls is removed and the gutters are cleared. Dust and dirt are removed with a soft brush and water at low pressure; acid products are ruled out for carbonate stone, and neutral or mildly alkaline compounds are used. Green growth is treated with a biocide and, after drying, the water-repellent treatment is restored.

Small chips and cavities are made good with a repair mix based on lime or white cement with crushed stone of the same kind, so that the patch matches in tone and vapour permeability. A tile with cracks or delamination is replaced entirely, for which a small reserve from the same batch is kept at the time of purchase.

FAQ

Is shell limestone a mineral or a rock?

A rock. It is an organogenic (biogenic) limestone made up of the shells of marine organisms cemented by calcite. Calcite is the main mineral in its composition.

Where is shell limestone quarried in Russia?

The main deposits are in Crimea, Dagestan, Krasnodar Krai and Rostov Oblast. It is extracted by open-pit quarrying, with blocks sawn from the bedrock by stone-cutting machines.

Shell limestone or aerated concrete: which is better?

Aerated concrete is more uniform in geometry and strength and has predictable characteristics. Shell limestone is a natural textured material, but density and strength vary from batch to batch. For facade cladding shell limestone is chosen for its appearance; as a wall material the decision depends on the climate and the project.

Is shell limestone suitable for facades?

Yes, if a dense grade is chosen and the stone is protected with a water repellent, sills and a moisture barrier. For the plinth it is better to choose a denser rock.

What should I use to drill shell limestone?

Soft stone is drilled with ordinary concrete drill bits without hammer action, or with core bits; hammer mode destroys the pores and crumbles the edge of the hole. Anchors should be fixings designed for porous substrates.

How do I render shell limestone?

The surface is cleaned of dust, primed and rendered with vapour-permeable lime or lime-cement mixes. An overly strong cement mortar will delaminate from soft stone.

How long does shell limestone cladding last?

The service life depends on the density of the stone, the climate and the quality of protection from water. In southern towns shell limestone buildings have stood for many decades. Dense tiles on a vertical wall with flashings, separation from the ground and renewed water-repellent treatment last a long time; on horizontal and permanently damp areas the service life is sharply reduced.

Can shell limestone be painted?

Yes, provided vapour-permeable facade paints are used, either silicate or silicone. Film-forming acrylic compounds trap moisture in the stone. Bear in mind that paint hides the natural texture and tone for which shell limestone is usually chosen, so a colourless impregnation is more often sufficient.

Does shell limestone cladding insulate the wall?

Practically not: tiles a few centimetres thick provide no appreciable thermal protection. If the wall needs insulating, a ventilated facade system with mineral wool insulation and an air gap is used, and the stone is fixed to the subframe.
