Shell Limestone (Coquina): Properties and Facade Cladding

Shell limestone, or coquina, is a sedimentary rock made of the shells and shell fragments of marine organisms cemented together by calcium carbonate. It is a light, porous limestone in warm tones ranging from cream and sand to honey. In construction it is used both as a wall block and as a cladding slab for facades, plinths, fences and interiors.

The key criterion when choosing shell limestone is the density and uniformity of the specific batch: strength, water absorption and frost resistance all depend on it. Loose stone works well for walls in a dry climate, while facade cladding in a temperate climate calls for dense grades and mandatory water-repellent treatment.

The material is chosen by those who value a warm natural texture and an eco-friendly product: private homeowners, architects of resort and low-rise developments, and restorers. More on other stone types in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Origin

Shell limestone formed on the floor of warm, shallow seas. Mollusc shells, coral remains and other organisms with calcareous skeletons accumulated on the seabed, compacted and were cemented by calcite dissolved in the water. By origin, therefore, shell limestone is a biogenic (organogenic) sedimentary rock, a variety of limestone rather than a mineral: the main mineral in its composition is calcite.

Large shell limestone deposits are linked to ancient marine basins. In Russia and neighbouring regions it is found in Crimea, Dagestan, Krasnodar Krai and Rostov Oblast; the stone is widespread around the Black Sea and the Caspian. Dagestan varieties are a large topic of their own: there shell limestone occurs alongside dense limestones and dolomites.

Shell limestone is extracted by open-pit quarrying. The softness of the rock allows blocks to be sawn straight from the bedrock with disc-saw stone-cutting machines, without blasting. The same blocks are then cut on machines into cladding slabs of the required thickness.

Properties

The properties of shell limestone vary greatly depending on the deposit and the specific bed. The table below therefore gives typical ranges; exact values for a batch are stated in the product data sheet or test report.

ParameterTypical rangeWhat it means in practice
Densityapprox. 900–2200 kg/m³light grades suit walls, dense grades suit cladding
Compressive strengthfrom a few to tens of MPaloose grades are not suitable for steps and paving
Water absorptionhigh; tens of percent for loose gradesmoisture protection is essential on facades and plinths
Frost resistancefrom low for porous grades to medium for dense onesdense stone is selected for temperate climates
Thermal conductivitylower than that of dense rocksa shell limestone wall feels “warm” to the touch

Shell limestone is non-combustible, vapour-permeable and emits no harmful substances. It is easy to saw, drill and work with hand tools, and its rough, porous surface bonds well with render and masonry mortar. The flip side of porosity is inconsistency: a single block may contain voids and large shells.

Pros and Cons

It is easiest to decide on shell limestone by comparing its strengths and weaknesses side by side.

Pros:

  • expressive natural texture with visible shells, warm colours;
  • low weight compared with granite and marble, so less load on the wall and foundation;
  • vapour permeability: the wall “breathes” and does not accumulate condensation;
  • non-combustibility;
  • easy working: cutting, drilling and fitting on site;
  • good adhesion to mortars and adhesives.

Cons:

  • high water absorption: without protection the stone gets wet, darkens and develops efflorescence and moss;
  • limited frost resistance of loose grades;
  • low strength, so it is unsuitable for areas with mechanical loads;
  • inconsistent block geometry and density, meaning rejects during selection;
  • dusting and abrasion if the surface is not impregnated.

Bottom line: shell limestone is appropriate where texture and low weight are valued, provided the right grade is chosen and it is protected from moisture.

Cladding

Shell limestone for cladding is produced as sawn tiles and slabs. Typical thickness of shell limestone cladding tiles is 20 to 50 mm; for loose grades the slab is made thicker to keep it strong during cutting, transport and installation. The face can be sawn, honed, split (rock-faced) or “antiqued”.

There are two main ways of cladding with shell limestone:

  • Adhesive fixing to a prepared substrate: brick, concrete or aerated concrete with reinforced render. Frost-resistant adhesives for natural stone are used, with fully filled joints and movement joints.
  • Ventilated rainscreen facade for dense grades and large formats. Slabs are fixed to the substructure with clips or anchors; thickness and format are determined by calculation.

Before cladding work the stone is sorted by tone and texture and a layout is prepared so that the facade does not end up patchy with different batches. Near the ground, by the apron, shell limestone is best avoided or protected especially carefully: the plinth zone suffers most from splashes, snow and de-icing salts. For plinths a denser stone is usually chosen: granite, basalt or dense dolomite.

Shell limestone cladding tiles work well indoors: on lobby walls, fireplaces and lounge areas where there is no risk of constant wetting.

Protection

Protection is the main condition for the durability of shell limestone on a facade. The key measures are:

  1. Water-repellent treatment. Penetrating siloxane-based impregnations reduce water absorption without blocking vapour permeability. They are applied to clean, dry stone, usually in two coats, and renewed periodically.
  2. Design protection. Sills, parapets, canopies and projecting cornices, and drainage away from the plinth. Horizontal shell limestone surfaces should be covered or made of a denser stone.
  3. Capillary moisture barrier. Waterproofing between the foundation and the cladding so that the stone does not “draw” water from the ground.
  4. Joints. Filling with frost-resistant, vapour-permeable compounds, not high-strength cement mortars, which destroy soft stone at the contact surface.

Film-forming varnishes are undesirable on facades: moisture collects under the film and the surface spalls when it freezes. If a “wet stone” look is needed, colour-enhancing impregnations designed for exterior use are applied.

FAQ

Is shell limestone a mineral or a rock?

A rock. It is an organogenic (biogenic) limestone made up of the shells of marine organisms cemented by calcite. Calcite is the main mineral in its composition.

Where is shell limestone quarried in Russia?

The main deposits are in Crimea, Dagestan, Krasnodar Krai and Rostov Oblast. It is extracted by open-pit quarrying, with blocks sawn from the bedrock by stone-cutting machines.

Shell limestone or aerated concrete: which is better?

Aerated concrete is more uniform in geometry and strength and has predictable characteristics. Shell limestone is a natural textured material, but density and strength vary from batch to batch. For facade cladding shell limestone is chosen for its appearance; as a wall material the decision depends on the climate and the project.

Is shell limestone suitable for facades?

Yes, if a dense grade is chosen and the stone is protected with a water repellent, sills and a moisture barrier. For the plinth it is better to choose a denser rock.

What should I use to drill shell limestone?

Soft stone is drilled with ordinary concrete drill bits without hammer action, or with core bits; hammer mode destroys the pores and crumbles the edge of the hole. Anchors should be fixings designed for porous substrates.

How do I render shell limestone?

The surface is cleaned of dust, primed and rendered with vapour-permeable lime or lime-cement mixes. An overly strong cement mortar will delaminate from soft stone.
