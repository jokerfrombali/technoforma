Natural Stone Protection: Water Repellents and Impregnators

A natural stone impregnator is a product that penetrates the pores and reduces absorption of water, oils and dirt without forming a film on the surface. Facades are mainly treated with water repellents (hydrophobic impregnators); interiors and paving are treated with combined water- and oil-repellent products, sometimes with a wet-look effect.

The main selection criterion is the porosity and mineral composition of the stone. Dense granite needs only light protection, while travertine, limestone, shell limestone and sandstone without impregnation quickly absorb moisture, darken, and develop efflorescence and biological growth. The second criterion is vapour permeability: on a facade the stone must breathe, otherwise moisture is trapped inside and destroys it when it freezes.

The topic matters to clients of facades and interiors in porous stones, facility management teams and technical supervisors. An overview of stones and applications is in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Why protect stone

Natural stone is strong, but most stones have open porosity. Water enters the stone through the pores, and with it dissolved salts, dust, soot, and spores of algae and moss. The consequences depend on the environment:

  • Freeze–thaw. Water in the pores expands on freezing and gradually destroys the surface layer: scaling, chipped edges and microcracks appear. Protection reduces the amount of water that can freeze in the pores.
  • Efflorescence. Moisture carries salts to the surface, where they crystallise as a white deposit. Less water inside means less efflorescence.
  • Soiling. Light porous stones (limestone, shell limestone, travertine) absorb urban dirt and run-off streaks from sills.
  • Biological growth. On north-facing and shaded elevations, constantly damp stone becomes overgrown with algae and lichen.
  • Stains indoors. Oil, wine and coffee penetrate marble and travertine within minutes and leave marks that are hard to remove.

Impregnation does not make stone stronger and does not cure already damaged material; it is prevention. That is why it is included in the design and carried out on new, clean and dry stone.

The solvent also matters: solvent-based products usually penetrate deeper, water-based ones are more convenient and safer to work with. The specific product is chosen from the manufacturer's technical data sheet for the stone and conditions, always with a trial application.

Application

The effectiveness of an impregnator depends more on preparation and application conditions than on the brand.

  1. Cleaning. The stone is cleaned of dust, cement residue, efflorescence and biological growth. Impregnation over dirt will seal it in.
  2. Drying. The surface must be dry: after rain or washing the facade is left to dry, usually for several days depending on the weather and the stone. Wet pores will not take up the product.
  3. Trial area. Absorption, colour change and uniformity are checked on an inconspicuous spot or a sample from the same batch.
  4. Application. By brush, roller or low-pressure sprayer, wet-on-wet to saturation, from top to bottom. Excess that has not soaked in is removed before it dries, so as not to leave glossy patches.
  5. Conditions. Air temperature and humidity within the limits set by the product manufacturer; do not apply in direct sun, in wind or before rain.
  6. Protection of adjacent surfaces. Glass, metal and PVC are covered: many products leave marks on them that are hard to remove.

On a facade it is most convenient to impregnate before installation, on all faces of the slab including the edges, or immediately after installation from the scaffolding before it is dismantled.

Reapplication intervals

How long an impregnator lasts depends on the stone, the product, facade orientation, sun, precipitation and mechanical wear. Product manufacturers usually state this in the technical data sheet; rely on that rather than on advertising figures.

A practical approach is not to wait for the stated period but to check the condition:

  • Water test. Water droplets on protected stone bead up and do not soak in. If water spreads and the stone darkens, the protection has weakened.
  • Seasonal inspection. In spring, after the winter, the facade is checked for efflorescence, darkening and scaling, especially at the plinth, under sills and at junctions.
  • Risk areas. Plinths, parapets, reveals, steps and floors wear faster than walls and need renewal more often.

Indoors, high-traffic floors and kitchen worktops are retreated considerably more often than walls. Before re-treatment the surface is cleaned and dried in the same way.

Mistakes

Typical mistakes that make an impregnator ineffective or harmful to the stone:

  • Film-forming lacquer on a facade. The film blocks vapour, moisture accumulates beneath it, and the coating peels off together with the surface layer of the stone.
  • Treating damp or dirty stone. The product does not penetrate the pores, stains remain and protection is uneven.
  • Face only. Water enters through the edges and back of the slab, and efflorescence appears despite the impregnation.
  • Excess on the surface. Product that has not soaked in leaves glossy patches and streaks that are hard to remove.
  • Impregnation as a cure. If the stone is already cracking or a facade sill is leaking, the cause is fixed first: drainage, junctions, sealing.
  • Acid cleaning before impregnation. Acid damages the surface of marble, limestone and travertine; products approved for carbonate stones are required.
  • No trial. Some products change the shade; without a sample there is a risk of getting a different colour on the facade.

Which stone types need protection first of all?

The higher the open porosity of a stone, the greater the benefit of impregnation. Dense granite on a vertical wall usually does without treatment, whereas shell limestone or soft limestone without a water repellent quickly gets wet, darkens and becomes soiled. It is best to rely on the test report for the particular batch: how to read this indicator is explained in the article on the water absorption of facade materials.

These ratings are qualitative. The spread within one stone type is wide: a dense limestone for cladding may absorb less water than a loose sandstone, and travertine with filled pores behaves differently from unfilled travertine.

The finish also affects the need for protection. A polished surface is denser and absorbs less than a sawn, bush-hammered or split one: textured finishes have a larger area in contact with water, and dirt lodges in the recesses. The coverage rate on rough stone is therefore noticeably higher, and this is allowed for in the estimate. Edges and kerfs for fixings absorb water more actively than the face — when treatment is carried out before installation, they are impregnated together with the slab.

Where on a building does stone need enhanced protection?

The water load is distributed unevenly over a facade, so the protection of natural stone is planned by zones rather than with one product for the whole building. The areas that suffer most are those where water lingers, splashes or rises from the ground.

  • The plinth and the first 30–50 cm above the perimeter paving — rain splashes, meltwater, de-icing agents, capillary rise where the damp-proofing is damaged.
  • Parapets, cornices, window sills and string courses — horizontal and gently sloping surfaces on which snow lies and water stands.
  • Entrance areas and steps — dirt from footwear, de-icing agents, mechanical wear; protection against stains, not only against moisture, is needed here.
  • Areas beneath downpipes, air-conditioning units and canopies — places of constant run-off.
  • North-facing and shaded walls — they dry more slowly and are more often overgrown with algae and moss.
  • Interior floors, worktops, areas near water — a risk of stains from oils, wine and cosmetics.

A denser stone than for the walls is often chosen for the lower tier, and this is more reliable than any impregnation. The requirements for this zone are discussed in detail in the article on cladding the plinth with natural stone.

Protection by design: why a water repellent does not replace flashings and drips

Impregnation reduces the absorption of water by the surface but does not stop water that gets behind the cladding through joints, junctions and unprotected edges. If a detail is wrongly resolved, a water repellent will merely postpone the appearance of streaks and efflorescence.

Design measures include flashings and copings with an overhang and a drip, falls on all horizontal planes, sealing of junctions with windows and the roof, and a damp-proof course between the foundation and the wall. In a ventilated system with an air gap the stone dries from both sides and therefore works under milder conditions than cladding bedded in mortar. With the wet method the composition of the mortar also matters: mixes with a high content of soluble salts produce a white bloom that comes to the surface together with moisture.

Joints deserve a separate mention. An open joint in a ventilated system needs no filling, whereas in mortar-bedded cladding a deteriorated grout becomes the main path for water. The condition of the joints is checked at every seasonal inspection and restored before the stone is treated again.

There is a downside as well: if water does get behind the water-repellent layer, for instance through a crack or from the ground, salts crystallise not on the surface but beneath it and may cause the face of the stone to flake off. For this reason impregnation is not applied to a wall with an active source of moisture — first the cause is eliminated, the structure is allowed to dry, and only then is the surface protected.

A trial area: how to choose a product and assess the result

The reliable way to choose an impregnation is a trial application on samples of the same stone and the same finish that will go on the building. One and the same product behaves differently on polished marble, sawn limestone and bush-hammered sandstone.

  1. Prepare two or three samples or an inconspicuous area of wall, as a rule no smaller than 0.5–1 m².
  2. Apply the product according to the manufacturer's instructions: at the stated coverage rate and number of coats.
  3. Allow the curing time, usually from one day to several days.
  4. Compare with an untreated control sample in daylight: whether the tone has changed, whether a sheen has appeared, whether there are stains or streaks.
  5. Check the effect: water — and, for stain-resistant products, oil — must not be absorbed or leave a dark mark.
  6. Record the brand, the coverage rate and the application conditions so that they can be repeated over the whole area.

It makes sense to keep the samples: in a few years they will help to judge how much the stone on the facade has changed and whether it is time to renew the protection.

How to protect stone from graffiti and stains in public areas?

For ground floors, underpasses, retaining walls and enclosures, anti-graffiti products are used: they prevent paint and marker ink from penetrating the pores, and the graffiti can be removed without grinding the stone. A water repellent does not solve this task — it repels water but not solvent-borne pigments.

The products fall into two groups. Sacrificial ones (wax- or polysaccharide-based) are washed off with hot water together with the graffiti, after which the coating is applied again. Permanent ones withstand several cleaning cycles with special removers but affect the appearance and vapour permeability more strongly, so on porous stones they are checked on a trial area with particular care.

Usually it is not the whole facade that is treated but a band up to the height a person can reach — about 2.5–3 m from the ground. Methods of removing dirt, from washing to chemical removers, are compared in the article on facade cleaning; for marble, limestone and travertine it is essential to exclude acid cleaners.

Protecting stone on site and during installation

A considerable share of stains and damage appears on the stone before the building is handed over: during storage, installation and adjacent trades. Removing them afterwards is harder than preventing them.

  • Slabs are stored on bearers, under a canopy or cover, without contact with the ground or water; spacers between the slabs must not leave marks — damp timber and rusting metal cause persistent stains.
  • Fixings and subframe elements in contact with the stone must be corrosion-resistant, otherwise rust-coloured streaks appear on light cladding over time.
  • Mortar, adhesive and sealant are removed from the face immediately, without waiting for them to set.
  • Installed cladding on the lower floors is shielded from splashes of concrete, render and paint, and entrance steps are covered with boards during the finishing works.
  • Unfinished masonry and the tops of walls are covered against rain so that water does not run behind the stone.

The impregnation is applied after construction dirt has been washed off and the stone has dried; treatment over a cement film will fix that film on the surface.

What to record in the design and in the maintenance manual

Protecting stone from moisture works for decades only when it is described in documents rather than resting on the contractor's memory. The maintenance team must know what the facade has been treated with and when the treatment is to be repeated.

  • the stone type, quarry and finish for each zone of the building;
  • the type and brand of the protective product, coverage rate, number of coats, date of application;
  • areas with special treatment: anti-graffiti, stain protection, consolidants;
  • permitted and prohibited cleaning agents and washing methods;
  • the frequency of inspections and the criteria for renewing the protection;
  • retained control samples and photographs of the facade after handover.

It is convenient to bring this information together in one document — its contents are described in the article on the facade passport. When the managing company or the contractor changes, it removes the need to select products blindly all over again.

FAQ

What is a stone water repellent?

It is an impregnator that makes pore walls water-repellent without blocking them. The stone stops absorbing water but remains vapour-permeable, which is why water repellents are the main type of protection for facade stone.

How do you protect facade stone from moisture?

With a combination of design and chemistry: correct sills, parapet copings, sealed junctions and a ventilated cavity, plus water-repellent treatment of all faces of the slab. Impregnation alone without proper drainage will not help.

Does granite need sealing?

Granite is dense but also porous. On facades it is usually treated with a water repellent to reduce soiling; indoors it is protected against oil stains, especially light-coloured varieties.

Does an impregnator change the colour of stone?

Colourless water repellents usually barely change the look, while wet-look products deliberately enhance the colour. In any case the result is checked on a sample from the same batch.

When is it better to apply the impregnator, before or after installation?

Preferably before installation, on all faces of the slab, and then again on the face after installation and cleaning. This protects the edges, through which water most often penetrates.

Can a water repellent be applied to damp stone?

Most products are intended for a dry surface: water in the pores prevents the impregnation from penetrating in depth, and the effect is weak and short-lived. After rain or washing the stone is left to dry, as a rule for at least one or two days in dry weather. Some water-based products tolerate a slightly damp substrate — this is stated in the manufacturer's instructions.

Does impregnation protect marble from acids?

No. Impregnation slows the absorption of liquid and gives time to remove it, but does not change the chemistry of the stone: acid still reacts with calcite and leaves a dull mark on the polish. Therefore, on marble, limestone and travertine, spilt juice, wine or vinegar is wiped up at once, and acid cleaners are not used.

Can stone be treated without a contractor?

Small surfaces — a worktop, the steps of a private house, a plinth — can be treated, provided the instructions are followed and a trial is first made on an inconspicuous area. On the facades of multi-storey buildings the work is carried out by specialists from scaffolding or access platforms: even application, control of the coverage rate and protection of windows and adjacent materials are important.
