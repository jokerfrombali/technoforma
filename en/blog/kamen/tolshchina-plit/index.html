Thickness of Stone Cladding Slabs

The thickness of cladding stone depends on the rock, slab format, fixing method and location on the building. Guidelines from practice: for interiors from 10–20 mm, for exterior adhesive cladding usually 20–40 mm, for a ventilated rainscreen facade as a rule 30 mm or more, and for steps, paving and parapets from 30 mm to 60 mm and more.

The key criterion is calculation: thickness is determined by the flexural strength of the stone, slab size, wind load and fixing layout. Soft and porous rocks are cut thicker than granite of the same format.

This article will be useful to rainscreen facade designers, technical supervisors and clients comparing supplier offers. Rocks and their properties are covered in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Standards

There is no single “correct thickness” figure in the standards: requirements are set through a calculation method and material characteristics.

  • Standards for natural stone products set the range of cladding slab thicknesses, dimensional tolerances and requirements for physical and mechanical properties. In practice slabs are produced in standard thicknesses of 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 mm and more.
  • Codes of practice on loads and actions set the wind loads a slab on a rainscreen facade must withstand; for tall buildings and corner zones these loads are significantly higher.
  • Technical documentation for the rainscreen facade system (a technical certificate or the substructure manufacturer’s design manual) defines the permissible stone formats and thicknesses for a specific fixing type, as well as fixing methods: clips, concealed anchors, kerfs.
  • Current fire safety codes of practice affect the choice of the system as a whole, but natural stone itself is non-combustible.

The final thickness and format for a facade are specified in the design: the calculation is based on the flexural strength of the specific stone batch (from the test report), with safety factors, slab size and support layout.

Soft Rocks

Soft rocks are those with low strength and increased porosity: shell limestone, limestone, clay-cemented sandstone, tuff, soft dolomites and travertine. Their characteristic feature is low flexural strength, so for the same format the slab is made thicker than a granite one.

ApplicationApproximate thickness
Interiors, walls15–30 mm
Adhesive facade cladding20–50 mm
Rainscreen facade30–50 mm or more, by calculation
Window sills, cornices, parapets30–60 mm or more
Paving, stepsfrom 30–40 mm; not recommended for loose rocks

The thickness of Dagestan stone for facade cladding is a frequent question: for dense Dagestan limestones and dolomites on adhesive, 20–30 mm tiles are usually used; for porous shell limestones, 30–50 mm. Exact values are determined from the batch characteristics.

Soft stone is easy to work, so profiles, cornices and window surrounds are easily cut from it. For such details thickness is determined by the geometry of the profile rather than by strength.

Another factor is transport and installation: a thin slab of soft rock can crack during loading or fitting. That is why, for loose grades, thickness is often increased beyond the calculated value even indoors.

Hard Rocks

Hard rocks include granite, basalt, quartzitic sandstones, gabbro and most dense marbles. Their high strength allows a smaller thickness for the same format.

ApplicationGranite, basaltMarble
Interiors, walls10–20 mm15–20 mm
Adhesive cladding (plinth, ground floor)20–30 mm20–30 mm
Rainscreen facade30–40 mm30–40 mm or more
Steps30–50 mm (tread)30–40 mm, preferably indoors
Paving, pedestrian / with vehicle access30–50 / 60–100 mmnot recommended

Flaming or bush-hammering removes the top millimetres of the stone, so slabs with extra thickness are used for these finishes. For marble on sunny facades, thickness is chosen with a margin because of the risk of thermal deformation of thin slabs.

Loads

Slab thickness is a balance between strength and weight. Every extra centimetre of stone with a density of about 2700 kg/m³ adds roughly 27 kg per square metre of cladding.

  • Load on the slab. A rainscreen slab works in bending under wind pressure and suction, as well as under local loads at the fixing points. The larger the format and the higher the floor, the thicker the slab or the more fixing points are needed.
  • Load on the substructure and wall. The weight of the cladding is transferred to brackets and anchors; for heavy stone, profile sections and the number of brackets are increased and the load-bearing capacity of the substrate is checked (especially aerated concrete and hollow brick).
  • Load on the adhesive layer. With the wet method, heavy slabs require additional mechanical fixing, and thickness and format are limited by height.
  • Stress concentration at fixings. A kerf for a clip or a hole for an anchor weakens the slab; minimum thickness is also determined by the need to leave enough stone around the fixing.

All these checks are carried out in the ventilated rainscreen facade design. TECHNOFORMA provides the full cycle: design, insulation, substructure installation and natural stone cladding.

Saving Money

You can save on thickness, but only where the calculation allows it.

  • Format. Reducing slab size allows the thickness to be reduced without loss of reliability.
  • Different thickness by zone. Thicker at the plinth and ground floor (mechanical loads), by wind calculation above.
  • Choice of rock. Strong stone allows a smaller thickness and lower weight, which reduces the cost of the substructure.
  • Combining materials. Natural stone in areas where its texture and durability matter; glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) or porcelain stoneware on the main surfaces. GFRC reproduces the look of stone at a lower weight.

Where you must not save: on the thickness of slabs for steps, parapets and cornices, and on large rainscreen facade formats. Cracks and falling cladding fragments are both a risk to people and an expensive repair at height.

How the fixing method affects the thickness of cladding slabs

The minimum thickness is often governed not by wind load but by the fixing detail: a kerf, a hole or an anchor seat has to be formed in the stone while leaving enough material around it. The same stone therefore requires a different thickness when bonded and when fixed on concealed fixings.

Fixing methodUsual slab thicknessWhat limits it
Adhesive on a continuous substratefrom 20 mmslab weight, adhesion, height of the cladding
Visible clips20–30 mmedge strength under the clip lug
Concealed fixing in an edge kerffrom 30 mmwall thickness on both sides of the kerf
Dowels (pins) in the edge30–40 mmdistance from the hole to the face
Anchors in the back of the slabfrom 30 mm, according to the anchor manufacturerembedment depth and residual thickness to the face

The values are indicative and apply to strong stones; for limestone, travertine and sandstone they are, as a rule, increased. A kerf or hole weakens the slab exactly where the greatest force acts, so the detail is verified by calculation and pull-out tests. More on fixing systems can be found in the article on the natural stone ventilated facade.

Bonded cladding above the ground floor is a separate case. Even where the adhesion calculation permits a thin slab, large and heavy elements are additionally secured with mechanical fixings, and these again call for sufficient thickness. The decision on where bonded cladding ends and the suspended system begins is taken in the design.

What stone slab thickness is needed for different formats

The larger the slab, the greater the bending moment that wind creates in it, and the thicker it must be for the same stone strength. As the format increases, thickness grows not in proportion to area but according to calculation: the span between the support points is decisive.

Slab formatGranite, basaltDense limestone, travertine, marble
up to 300×600 mm20 mm20–30 mm
600×600 – 600×900 mm30 mm30–40 mm
600×1200 mm and larger30–40 mm40 mm and more
Large-format slabsfrom 40 mm or additional fixing pointsby calculation, often impractical

The table shows the order of magnitude for suspended cladding at medium height; in corner zones and on upper storeys, where wind pressure is higher, the next thickness step is taken or the format is reduced. Narrow elongated slabs perform worse than square ones of the same area: they break more often in transport and installation. How loads are combined and bracket spacing is chosen is described in the piece on how a ventilated facade is calculated.

How much a square metre of stone cladding weighs at different thicknesses

The weight of cladding is directly proportional to the thickness and density of the stone, and it determines the subframe, the anchors, the logistics and the method of lifting onto the facade. Quotations with different thicknesses are conveniently compared in kilograms per square metre.

Stone (indicative density)20 mm30 mm40 mm
Granite, marble (about 2700 kg/m³)54 kg/m²81 kg/m²108 kg/m²
Dense limestone, travertine (about 2400 kg/m³)48 kg/m²72 kg/m²96 kg/m²
Shell limestone (about 1800 kg/m³, varies widely by grade)36 kg/m²54 kg/m²72 kg/m²

The subframe, insulation and fixings are added to the weight of the stone. Moving from 30 to 40 mm increases the mass of the cladding by a third, which already means a different bracket spacing and possibly a different rail material; the options are examined in the review of subframes for ventilated facades. For refurbishment and for walls of lightweight blocks, the weight of the cladding frequently becomes the main constraint when choosing thickness.

Weight also affects how installation is organised. A slab of up to 25–30 kg is set by two fitters by hand, while heavier elements require lifting gear and clamps, which means a different pace of work and a different cost. When choosing between a large format at 40 mm and a medium one at 30 mm, it is therefore not only the price of the stone that is compared but also the labour involved in setting it.

Tolerances and calibration: why actual thickness differs from nominal

A slab with a nominal thickness of 30 mm may measure 29 or 31.5 mm in a real batch: sawing the block produces a spread that is then partly removed by calibration. The thickness tolerance depends on the standard, the product class and the contract with the supplier; deviations of the order of 1–3 mm are usual.

For thin-bed bonding and for floors a calibrated slab is ordered — it is ground on the back to a uniform thickness. For a suspended facade with concealed fixing something else matters more: the kerf is cut at a set distance from the face so that the difference in thickness goes towards the wall and causes no steps in the plane.

  • The schedule states the nominal thickness, the tolerance and the face from which it is measured.
  • Flaming and bush-hammering are allowed for in advance: after texturing, the thickness must remain no less than the design value.
  • Slabs from different batches and different machines are best not mixed within one work section.

Variation in thickness is especially noticeable on exposed edges: at building corners, in reveals and on parapets. For such places slabs are selected from a single batch, or corner elements are ordered with a mitre — the edge cut at 45° so that the end is not visible at all. A mitre weakens the edge, so it is done with more confidence on strong stones than on porous ones.

Can the weight of stone be reduced without losing strength

Yes, if the idea that a slab must be solid across its whole area is abandoned. Several techniques are used for this, and each has its own conditions.

  • Thin stone on a load-bearing backing. A layer of stone a few millimetres thick is bonded to an aluminium honeycomb panel or another rigid backing. The weight is several times lower than that of a solid slab, but such panels are a factory product with their own fixing system and technical documents.
  • Reinforcement of the back. A mesh in epoxy is bonded to fragile grades of marble, travertine and onyx. It holds the fragments together if the slab cracks, but it does not replace the design strength of the slab.
  • Replacing stone on decorative elements. Cornices, window surrounds, pilasters and three-dimensional GFRC panels with a stone texture weigh noticeably less than stone elements of the same shape and are classed as non-combustible materials.

In practice a facade is often divided into zones: natural stone at the plinth and at eye level, lighter cladding above. Strong stones, for example granite for facades, make it possible to stay at 30 mm where limestone would require 40–50 mm.

How to check the thickness of cladding stone on acceptance

Thickness is checked before installation, at the warehouse or during unloading: once the stone is on the facade it can no longer be measured. The check takes little time and removes most disputes with the supplier.

  1. Compare the marking of crates and pallets with the schedule: stone type, format, thickness, finish.
  2. Measure a sample of slabs with a calliper at several points around the perimeter — not only at the corners.
  3. Check flatness: apply a straightedge to the face and along the diagonal.
  4. Inspect the edges and kerfs for chips, hairline cracks and displacement of the kerf towards the face.
  5. Request the batch test report with the flexural strength and compare it with the value adopted in the calculation.
  6. Set aside slabs with deviations and record them in a report before installation begins.

Elements made to individual dimensions deserve a separate check: sill slabs, parapet copings, corner pieces. An error in thickness is most noticeable on them because the edge remains in view.

It is useful to keep the results of the check together with the as-built documentation. If a crack appears on the facade some years later or a slab has to be replaced, the records of the batch, thickness and test reports make it possible to order stone with the same parameters and to understand whether the cause lay in the material or in the installation.

What to state in an enquiry so that slab thickness is selected correctly

A supplier can propose a thickness with good reason only when it is known where and how the stone will work. An enquiry such as granite 30 mm, 500 m² yields a material price but does not answer whether it suits the project.

  • purpose: facade, plinth, interior, steps, paving;
  • installation method: adhesive, or a suspended system with visible or concealed fixing;
  • building height, region of construction, wall material;
  • desired formats and layout, presence of corner and shaped elements;
  • finish of the face;
  • the design requirements for frost resistance and strength, if already defined.

From these data the designer selects the stone, format and thickness as a single solution rather than separately. The sequence of steps from sample to dispatch is described in the article on how to order stone for a project.

FAQ

How thick should facade slabs be?

For adhesive cladding usually 20–40 mm, for a rainscreen facade 30 mm or more. The exact value is determined by calculation based on the rock, format and wind load.

How thick should Dagestan stone be for facade cladding?

Dense limestones and dolomites are usually 20–30 mm on adhesive, porous shell limestones 30–50 mm. The batch characteristics should be the guide.

What is stone facade cladding with pronounced joints and a textured face called?

Rustication: treatment of a wall with stones that have emphasised joints and a textured face.

Can 20 mm granite be used on a rainscreen facade?

For small formats and low-rise buildings this is sometimes acceptable, but 30 mm is used more often. The decision is made only by calculation.

Why is a thicker slab used for flaming?

The treatment removes a few millimetres of the surface, so extra initial thickness is needed to maintain strength.

Is it true that the thicker the slab, the more reliable the facade?

Not always. A thick slab is stronger in bending but heavier: the load on brackets, anchors and the wall increases. Reliability is determined by the consistency of thickness, format, fixing and subframe, confirmed by calculation.

What thickness is needed for concealed fixing of stone?

As a rule, from 30 mm: a kerf or an anchor seat has to be formed in the slab while leaving sufficient wall thickness. For soft stones the value is increased; the exact figure comes from the calculation and the fixing manufacturer's recommendations.

Can slabs of different thickness be used on one facade?

Yes, this is common practice: thicker slabs are placed at the plinth and on the ground floor, and above that the thickness follows the wind calculation. The difference is taken up by adjusting the subframe so that the face plane remains uniform.
