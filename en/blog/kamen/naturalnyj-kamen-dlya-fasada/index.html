Natural Stone for Facades: Which Types Are Suitable

Natural stone for facades means slabs and architectural elements made of rock that can withstand outdoor conditions: temperature swings, freeze-thaw cycles, precipitation, UV and pollution. Not every stone type, and not every quarry of the same stone, is suitable for cladding, so the choice is not "granite" or "limestone" in general but a specific stone with confirmed properties.

The main selection criterion is the combination of frost resistance, water absorption and strength with the architectural brief and the climate. Hard stones (granite, basalt) are for plinths, steps and high loads; soft stones (limestone, sandstone, shell limestone, travertine) are for expressive wall surfaces and carved ornament in a suitable climate and with protection.

This article is for architects, developers and private homeowners. For an overview, see Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Requirements for facade stone

Cladding stone for exterior use is assessed on several properties. Their values depend on the quarry and even on the block, so exact figures are taken from test reports for the specific batch.

  • Frost resistance: the number of freeze-thaw cycles without failure or significant loss of strength. In the Russian climate this is the key parameter for facades; the required value depends on the construction region and the facade zone (the plinth gets wetter than the walls).
  • Water absorption: how much water the stone absorbs. The lower it is, the lower the risk of frost damage and the more slowly the stone gets dirty.
  • Compressive and flexural strength: determines the permissible slab thickness and fixing method, especially on rainscreen systems, where the slab works in bending.
  • Abrasion resistance: important for steps, landings and plinths.
  • Homogeneity: no cracks, cavities or weak veins along which the slab may split.
  • Colour stability: some stones change shade in sunlight or through oxidation of inclusions.

For rainscreen installation there is an additional requirement for geometry and strength at the fixing points: kerfs and anchor holes must not cause chipping.

Soft and hard stones

The division into soft and hard stones is practical: it determines how the stone is processed, where it is used and how long it lasts on a facade.

Hard stones are granite, basalt and, to a lesser extent, marble. They have high strength, low water absorption and good abrasion resistance. They are used where stone faces wear and wetting: plinths, steps, entrances, paving, and also for entire prestige facades. Processing is harder and more expensive, and carved ornament in granite is labour-intensive.

Soft stones are limestone, sandstone, shell limestone (coquina), dolomite and travertine. They are easier to cut and profile, allowing cornices, window surrounds, rustication and warm natural shades. However, many of them have higher porosity and water absorption, so frost resistance depends on the specific quarry, and on a facade they need water-repellent treatment and well-designed drainage details.

TECHNOFORMA's equipment processes both soft stones (sandstone, shell limestone, dolomite, limestone) and hard stones, so one project can combine, for example, a granite plinth with limestone walls.

Origin

The origin of stone is not just geography but also the predictability of its properties and supply. TECHNOFORMA imports stone from India, Turkey, China and Iran, keeps stock from these countries and supplies stone to order from anywhere in the world. A separate line is Dagestan stone, with which the company has extensive experience.

  • Turkey and Iran are known for travertines, marbles and limestones in light, warm tones.
  • India and China are major suppliers of granites in many colours and of sandstones.
  • Dagestan supplies limestones and other sedimentary stones traditionally used in the region's architecture.

For a large project it is important that the entire volume comes from one quarry and, where possible, from one batch: the shade of stone from different quarries and even different blocks varies noticeably. That is why the layout and order are done for the whole facade at once, and samples are approved before deliveries begin.

How to choose

How to choose stone for a facade:

  1. Define the climate and zones. Plinths, steps and horizontal surfaces work harder than walls and need denser, more frost-resistant stone.
  2. Choose the fixing system. On a rainscreen facade the requirements for flexural strength and thickness are higher than for adhesive fixing.
  3. Request test reports for the specific stone: frost resistance, water absorption, strength.
  4. Look at samples and photos of the blocks. Natural pattern and shade vary, and this shows on a facade.
  5. Order a layout. It allows the quantity to be calculated including offcuts and the slabs to be matched by pattern.
  6. Plan protection. Water-repellent treatment for porous stones, and sills and drips on horizontal elements for all stones.

TECHNOFORMA carries out measurements with its own surveyors, design, 3D visualisation and layout, followed by installation with designer supervision.

Which stone for which part of the facade: zoning the cladding

A facade is seldom clad in one stone from top to bottom: the conditions in which the stone works on the plinth, on the wall and on the cornice differ too much. It is more sensible to divide the building into zones and to select the stone type, finish and thickness for the loads of each.

In practice this often means granite or basalt at the bottom and a softer stone — limestone, travertine, sandstone or dolomite — on the walls. The boundary between them is conveniently aligned with an architectural division: a string course, rustication or a change of slab format.

Zoning is also useful for the budget: an expensive dense stone is used only where it is really needed, while a material that is easier to work is used over the main wall area. At the same time the number of stone types on one building is best limited to two or three, otherwise procurement, layout and subsequent repair become more complicated.

How do finish and colour change the look of a natural stone facade?

One and the same stone in different finishes looks like different materials: polishing makes the colour darker and richer, while bush-hammering or flaming lightens the stone and subdues the pattern. Samples for approval are therefore requested in exactly the finish that will go on the building.

  • Polished — maximum depth of colour and a mirror gloss; outdoors it suits granite, while on carbonate stones the gloss fades with time.
  • Honed — smooth and matt, calm, universal for walls.
  • Sawn — the natural matt surface of soft stones, the most economical.
  • Flamed and bush-hammered — rough, non-slip, for the plinth, steps and textured accents.
  • Split, rock-faced — bold relief for the plinth and rustication.
  • Antiqued — soft relief and eased edges, for classical work and restoration.

Combining two finishes of one stone is an inexpensive way to obtain a graphic facade without changing the stone type. The capabilities of the equipment and the limitations for different stones are described in the article on stone processing methods. Colour should be assessed outdoors, in both dry and wet condition: porous stones darken noticeably after rain.

Slab formats and thickness: what to allow for in the design

The slab format is determined at once by the architecture, the strength of the stone and the size of the blocks the quarry yields. The larger the slab, the more expensive the square metre and the higher the requirements for thickness, fixing and transport.

For walls, slabs with a long side of 600–1200 mm are used most often; larger ones are possible in strong stones and by calculation. Soft and porous stones are taken thicker than hard ones: whereas 30 mm is usual for granite in a ventilated system, for limestone, sandstone and shell limestone the thickness is generally greater. Sufficient material must remain around kerfs and anchor holes, otherwise the slab spalls at the fixing. Guidance by stone type and installation method is given in the article on the thickness of cladding slabs.

A modular grid saves stone: when the course heights and slab widths are multiples of the common sizes of blanks, there is less waste and supply is quicker. Non-standard elements — corner, curved, profiled — are placed in a separate schedule, since their production times are usually longer.

Ventilated facade or mortar-bedded cladding: how does the installation method affect the choice of stone?

The fixing method sets requirements for the stone no less than the climate does. In a ventilated system the slab works in bending between its points of support, so strength and uniformity matter; with mortar-bedded cladding the load is taken by the substrate, but the stone depends more on moisture and salts in the wall.

  • A ventilated system with an air gap — the principal solution for multi-storey buildings. Insulation is placed beneath the cladding, the stone dries from both sides, and an individual slab can be replaced. Calculation of the subframe and accurate slab geometry are required.
  • Cladding on mortar or adhesive with mechanical restraint — for plinths, low-rise buildings and small areas. It suits slabs of smaller format; compatibility of the mortar with the stone and the absence of soluble salts in it are important.
  • Massive elements — blocks, columns, profiled pieces — bear on the building structure and are restrained with anchors.

Laminated and fissured varieties are checked especially carefully for ventilated fixing. How the subframe and its details are arranged is shown on the page installation of ventilated facades.

The choice of fixing method also affects the programme: ventilated cladding can be installed over a wide range of temperatures, whereas mortar work is limited to the warm season or requires heated enclosures.

Facade stone by building type

The requirements for cladding differ not only with the climate but also with the purpose of the building: the developer of a residential complex, a hotel operator and the owner of a private house have different priorities.

  • Residential complexes. Stone is concentrated on the ground floors, entrance areas and podium, with lighter materials used above. Repeatable formats and a steady rhythm of deliveries to suit the installation programme are important.
  • Business centres. Large formats, a strict joint grid, granite and dense limestones predominate; stability of tone over large planes is valued.
  • Hotels and resort properties. Warm stones — travertine, limestone, sandstone; by the sea, resistance to salts and humidity is checked separately.
  • Public and administrative buildings. Durability and repairability come first, together with vandal resistance of the lower tier.
  • Private houses. Complex details over a small area: many corners, reveals and surrounds, so the share of special pieces is higher.

Examples of solutions for buildings of different purposes are gathered in the section completed projects.

For any type of building it is useful to decide in advance who is responsible for the result as a whole. When the selection of the stone, the working drawings of the cladding, supply and installation are divided among different contractors, mistakes at the interfaces between stages come to light only on the scaffolding. Single responsibility for design and installation reduces the number of such inconsistencies.

Stone and GFRC on one facade

Natural stone for a facade is often combined with GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete): the stone remains in the zone of close view and on the planes, while complex decoration and elements at height are made of a lighter, mouldable material. This is how, for example, the facade of the Dinastia club house in Kislovodsk is arranged, where granite is complemented by a large quantity of GFRC elements.

The logic of the division is simple. Cornices of large projection, capitals and architraves of complex profile in solid stone are heavy and require strengthened supports; thin-walled GFRC elements weigh several times less, do not burn and reproduce any shape. Stone, in turn, is irreplaceable where natural pattern, tactile quality and resistance to mechanical impact matter.

For the materials to read as a single whole, the colour and texture of the GFRC elements are matched to a sample of the chosen stone, and the joints and divisions are designed on a common grid. Samples of both materials are approved at the same time, in natural light.

What the cost depends on and what to prepare for an estimate

The cost of a stone facade is made up of the stone itself, its processing, the subframe with fixings, installation and logistics, and the share of the stone in the total is by no means always the largest. A detailed breakdown of the components is given in the article on what makes up the price of stone.

The budget is affected most by the stone type and its origin, the thickness and format of the slabs, the type of finish, the share of special and curved pieces, the height of the building and the difficulty of access. Savings on thickness or on fixings usually turn into costs in service, so it is better to optimise the layout and the range of formats.

The following is sufficient for a preliminary estimate:

  • elevations and plans with dimensions, in an editable format where possible;
  • the material and condition of the walls, and whether there is insulation;
  • preferences as to stone type, colour and finish, or reference images;
  • the zones where stone is required and the materials for the remaining area;
  • the region of construction, the number of storeys and the desired programme.

FAQ

Which natural stone is best for facade cladding?

Granite or basalt for plinths and steps. For walls, granite, dense limestone, dolomite, travertine or sandstone with confirmed frost resistance. The choice is made on the basis of test reports for the specific quarry.

Which volcanic stone is traditionally used for facade cladding?

Tuff, a volcanic rock traditionally used for cladding, especially in Armenia.

Which stone should be used to clad a foundation from outside?

Dense, frost-resistant stones with low water absorption: granite, basalt, dolomite. The plinth gets the wettest and takes the most knocks.

How does natural stone cladding differ from stone-effect panels?

Natural stone is a natural material with a unique pattern and a long service life, but it is heavier and more expensive. Stone-effect panels are lighter and cheaper but are an imitation; a highly mouldable alternative is glass fibre reinforced concrete with a stone texture.

Can I order a stone that is not in stock?

Yes. In addition to its stock from India, Turkey, China and Iran, TECHNOFORMA supplies stone to order from anywhere in the world.

Does natural stone fade in the sun?

Most facade stones keep their colour for decades: granite, basalt, light limestones and travertine are practically insensitive to ultraviolet light. Certain coloured varieties may change noticeably — some dark and green slates, marbles, and sandstones with organic or iron-bearing impurities. For such varieties it is worth looking at buildings where the stone has stood outdoors for several years.

How long does natural stone facade cladding last?

With a correctly chosen stone and competent installation, stone cladding lasts for many decades, and dense stones considerably longer. The service life of the facade as a whole is more often limited not by the stone but by the fixings, joints and junction details, so their condition is checked during regular inspections.

Can an existing building be clad in stone?

Yes, in refurbishment the stone is installed on the existing walls, most often on a ventilated subframe. The load-bearing capacity of the walls and foundations is surveyed beforehand and the anchors are pull-out tested: stone cladding is noticeably heavier than render or composite panels.
