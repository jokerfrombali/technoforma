What Determines the Price of Natural Stone

The price of natural stone is not a single figure per square metre of slab but the sum of several components: the cost of the stone at the quarry, sawing and processing, thickness, waste from cutting and pattern matching, delivery, substructure and installation. So the same granite costs differently on a warehouse price list and on a finished facade, and two stones with a similar slab price can produce very different final estimates.

The key principle of comparison is to calculate the cost per finished square metre of cladding on a specific project, not the price of the material. It is affected by slab format, facade complexity, building height, region and schedule.

The article will be useful to developers, estimators, architects and private homeowners planning a cladding budget. TECHNOFORMA determines the specific cost of works after a site inspection; the article contains no ready-made prices. An overview of stones is in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Stone type and origin

The first factor that determines the price is the stone type and the specific quarry.

  • Availability. Common grey and pink granites are quarried in huge volumes and cost noticeably less than rare colours: blue, green, deep black without inclusions.
  • Decorative value. White marbles with a clean background, onyx and marbles with a striking pattern are valued higher because of rarity and high extraction waste.
  • Yield from the block. Fractured quarries give fewer good slabs, so the cost of usable stone rises.
  • Grade and selection. Within one stone there are grades: those without spots and inclusions and with a uniform tone are more expensive.
  • Country and route. India, Turkey, China and Iran differ in the cost of extraction, processing and delivery route.

TECHNOFORMA works with stone from India, Turkey, China and Iran from its own warehouse, supplies items to order from anywhere in the world and has extensive experience with stones from Dagestan. The choice between a stock item and one ordered in affects both price and lead time.

Thickness and finish

Thickness determines the amount of stone per square metre and its resistance to loads. Interior walls use slabs of about 10–20 mm, floors and rainscreen facades more often 20–40 mm, steps 30 mm or more. A thicker slab means a higher material price, more weight, higher substructure requirements and more labour-intensive installation.

Non-standard elements such as balusters, cornices, columns and carving are priced separately and often cost an order of magnitude more per unit area than flat cladding. In such cases they are compared with a GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) option.

Waste

Waste is the part of the stone that is paid for but does not end up on the wall. Its sources:

  • Slab cutting. Slabs have uneven edges and a limited size; design-format pieces are cut from them, and offcuts go to small elements or waste.
  • Pattern matching. For book-matched marble and onyx, part of the slab is cut not in the most economical way but in the most beautiful one.
  • Rejection. Cracks, cavities and stains are cut out.
  • Cuts on site. At openings, corners and junctions.
  • Breakage in transport and installation.
  • Reserve. Spare stone from the same batch for replacements is a mandatory part of the order.

The share of waste depends on the stone, format and complexity of the facade. It is reduced by a layout based on real slabs, choosing a slab format that suits the slab size, and surveying before cutting. That is why layout and 3D visualisation pay off first and foremost on expensive stones.

Waste also matters when comparing supplier quotes. One quote may be based on the net facade area, another may include cutting, trimming and reserve. The first looks cheaper but leads to a top-up order on site, and a top-up from another batch risks a tone mismatch. So the correct comparison is between estimates calculated on the same layout, with the same reserve and the same assumptions about edge finishing.

Logistics

Stone is heavy and fragile, so logistics is a significant part of the price, especially for imported items. It includes:

  • sea or multimodal freight from the country of origin;
  • customs clearance;
  • packing: crates, slab racks, spacers;
  • delivery from the warehouse to the site, unloading with a crane truck;
  • storage on site until installation.

A warehouse in Russia removes the wait for sea freight from the schedule: items in stock ship immediately. TECHNOFORMA has warehouses from Novorossiysk and Makhachkala to Novosibirsk and Yekaterinburg, and its production base and stone warehouses are in the Moscow region. The closer the warehouse is to the site and the fewer transhipments, the lower the transport component and the risk of breakage.

Installation

On a facade the cost of installation is as significant as the cost of the stone. It is determined by:

  1. Fixing method. A ventilated rainscreen system with substructure, insulation and clips, or adhesive fixing to the substrate (for plinths and low-rise buildings).
  2. Substructure. For stone it is heavier than for porcelain stoneware because of the weight, with more closely spaced anchors.
  3. Height and access. Scaffolding, cradles, lifts, rigging for heavy slabs.
  4. Facade complexity. The number of corners, reveals, bay windows and curved sections.
  5. Slab format. Large slabs mean fewer joints but heavier installation.
  6. Season. Winter installation requires additional measures.

What goes into the cost of a square metre of finished cladding

The price of a finished square metre is made up of six or seven groups of costs, and the slab is only one of them. Depending on the stone and the facade type, the stone itself usually accounts for between one third and two thirds of the estimate; the rest goes on the subframe, fixings, labour and site arrangements.

Comparing quotations by the single line 'stone per square metre' is therefore pointless: a low material price is easily offset by an expensive subframe for a heavy slab or by a high share of trimming. Fixing methods are covered in more detail in the article on stone facade cladding.

How slab format and cutting affect the budget

The closer the slab size is to a multiple of the slab or strip dimensions, the less stone is lost as offcuts and the lower the price per square metre. A non-standard module chosen purely for the facade pattern typically raises material consumption by 10–25%; the exact figure depends on the stone and the size of the blanks.

Three decisions taken at design stage affect the cost:

  • Course height. Strip blanks normally have a fixed width; a cladding course equal to that width minus the joint is cut with almost no waste.
  • Slab length. Random lengths, where slabs of different length sit in one course, cost less than fixed lengths because the factory uses all of the material. A strict joint rhythm looks better on a regular facade but is paid for in offcuts.
  • Large format. Large slabs require defect-free areas of the raw slab, greater thickness and reinforced fixings. The price grows faster than the area, not in proportion to it.

The most economical approach is a stone layout before purchase: it gives the exact number of slabs of each size rather than an area with a notional factor. The layout also shows where a format can be changed by a few centimetres without harming the architecture and with a tangible saving of material.

Grade, batch and selection: what the client pays extra for

The same stone is sold in several grades, and the difference between them can be comparable to the difference between two separate stones. Grade is determined by the uniformity of the background, the number of veins and inclusions, the presence of filled cracks and voids, and the stability of tone.

For large facades a single batch matters more than the top grade. Stone from different blocks of the same quarry varies in shade, and buying the missing five per cent six months later is almost always visible on the wall. Two budgeting rules follow: the whole quantity, including spare stock, is bought in one order, and tone selection is a separate paid operation if the design calls for an even field without patches.

Saving on grade is reasonable where the surface is seen from a distance or is roughly finished: on upper storeys and in bush-hammered or flamed textures natural variation is less noticeable. At entrances and at ground-floor level, where stone is viewed at arm's length, selection is justified. How texture alters the perception of colour is described in the overview of stone finishes.

Which costs are often missing from the first estimate?

Preliminary calculations most often omit costs not directly related to the slab: special pieces, testing, protection and site organisation. Individually they are small, but together they can shift the budget noticeably.

  • Corner pieces, reveals, window sills, drips and parapet copings, which are counted in linear metres and units rather than in area.
  • Kerfs, slots and holes for concealed fixings, and mesh reinforcement on the back of slabs.
  • Pull-out tests of anchors and testing of stone samples where the design or the review authority requires it.
  • Water-repellent treatment of porous stones and protection of the installed cladding until handover.
  • Scaffolding, facade hoists, temporary shelters and storage on a restricted site.
  • Packaging and cargo insurance for long-distance transport.
  • A repair stock of slabs that stays with the building operator.

These items are identified most reliably at the working documentation stage: once there is a facade design with details and a specification, no 'as incurred' lines remain in the estimate.

How to compare quotations for natural stone

Quotations are comparable only when they are reduced to the same scope of work and the same specification. Before comparing, the same set of points should be checked for each supplier.

  1. The stone, the quarry and the grade are named specifically, not by a general trade name such as 'beige travertine'.
  2. Thickness, dimensional tolerances and the finish of the face and edges are stated.
  3. It is clear what has been calculated: facade area or the number of slabs from a layout, including waste.
  4. The subframe is described by material (galvanised steel, stainless steel, aluminium) and by fixing type.
  5. It is clear whether delivery to site, unloading, lifting to the floor and access equipment are included.
  6. It is defined who is responsible for breakage in transit and for tone matching on the wall.
  7. Samples are available from the same batch that will go to the site.

If supply and installation are split between different contractors, open questions appear where responsibilities meet: trimming on site, replacement of defective pieces, timing. Under a single contract for ventilated facade installation these risks rest with one contractor and are priced in from the start.

How the cost structure changes by building type

The balance of items in the estimate depends on the building type: on a high-rise residential complex the subframe and mechanisation play the main role, on a private house it is the stone itself and hand fitting. Understanding this structure helps to look for savings where they really exist rather than cutting the budget evenly across every line.

  • Residential complexes. Large repeating surfaces allow stone to be ordered in serial sizes and waste to be reduced. The subframe, insulation and lifting equipment take a noticeable share, and the cost of a layout error is multiplied by the number of sections and storeys.
  • Business centres and hotels. Requirements for tone selection and for entrances are higher, and large formats and concealed fixings are more common. The share of material and design work grows, and delivery dates are tied to the opening schedule.
  • Public buildings. There are many special pieces: portals, columns, cornices, stairs. Their cost is counted per unit, and it is these pieces, not the plain field, that determine the total.
  • Private houses. The quantity is small, so the minimum batch, delivery and the share of trimming at corners and reveals weigh more heavily. Stock items and standard formats are the better choice.

On buildings with pronounced relief, some stone details are often replaced with GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) elements: they are lighter, reproduce a complex profile and reduce the load on the subframe. Stone then remains where its texture and wear resistance matter, at the plinth, the entrances and ground-floor level.

For any building type it is useful to divide the estimate into the plain field and the accent zones. The plain field takes up most of the area, and this is where serial formats, random slab lengths and stock stone pay off. Accent zones are small in area, so a more expensive stone, pattern selection and complex edge finishing there hardly change the total, yet they noticeably affect how the facade is perceived at close range.

What to prepare for a cladding cost calculation

Architectural drawings and an idea of the stone are enough for an indicative calculation; an accurate estimate needs baseline data on the building. The more complete the set, the narrower the range in the quotation.

  • Elevations, plans and sections in an editable format, with levels.
  • The material and condition of the load-bearing walls, and the presence and thickness of insulation in the design.
  • The preferred stone or a reference sample, the finish and the preferred slab format.
  • A list of zones: plinth, main field, entrances, reveals, parapets.
  • The planned start date and site constraints.

FAQ

Why do natural stone prices vary so much?

Because of the rarity of the stone, grade, yield from the block, thickness, finish and logistics. Common granite and rare onyx can differ in price several times over.

Which costs more, the material or the installation?

It depends on the stone and the project. On a rainscreen facade the substructure and installation make up a significant part of the estimate, so the cost per finished square metre is compared.

How can the cost of stone cladding be reduced?

Choose a stock item, match the slab format to the slab size, prepare a layout before cutting, and combine stone with porcelain stoneware or GFRC.

Can I find out the price of a stone facade in advance?

An estimate is possible from the design and specification. TECHNOFORMA determines the exact cost of works after a site inspection.

How much extra stone should be ordered?

The reserve depends on the complexity of the facade and the stone; it is determined from the layout. It should be ordered from the same batch so that replacements match in colour.

Why does an estimate based on a layout differ from a calculation by area?

A calculation by area multiplies the square metres of the facade by a notional waste factor. A layout counts actual slabs: how many are whole, how many are trimmed, how many corner and make-up pieces there are. On simple facades the figures are close; on facades with many openings and projections a layout usually gives a larger quantity of stone, but it rules out topping up from a different batch.

Can the price of stone be fixed for the whole construction period?

The cost of imported stone depends on exchange rates and freight, so the only stable price is that of a batch already paid for and put into production. A practical way to protect the budget is to buy the whole quantity with spare stock in one order and keep it in a warehouse until installation begins. Specific terms are fixed in the contract.

Which is more economical: a thin slab on adhesive or a slab on a subframe?

Adhesive fixing is cheaper in materials and labour, but it is applicable only up to a limited height, on a sound substrate and not for every stone or format. A ventilated system costs more, yet it allows insulation, greater height and replacement of individual slabs. The decision is taken on the basis of the design, not on the price per square metre alone.
