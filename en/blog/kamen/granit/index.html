Granite for Facades and Plinths

Granite is the most reliable natural cladding stone for facades: an igneous rock of quartz, feldspars and mica with high strength, low water absorption, frost resistance and acid resistance. It lasts for decades without a noticeable loss of appearance, which is why it is traditionally used for plinths, entrances, steps and cladding of lower floors.

The key selection criterion is purpose and finish: plinths and steps need a rough surface (flamed, bush-hammered), while polished slabs also suit vertical surfaces. The second criterion is the colour and uniformity of the batch.

Granite is chosen by developers of residential complexes and public buildings, architects of business centres and private homeowners who need a durable plinth without regular repairs. More on other rocks in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Properties of Granite

Typical characteristics of facade cladding granite are given in the table. Specific values depend on the deposit; design uses the batch test report.

ParameterTypical rangeWhat it gives you
Densityabout 2600–2800 kg/m³heavy cladding, so fixings must be calculated
Compressive strengthapprox. 120–250 MPawithstands impacts and loads at plinth level
Water absorptionusually less than 0.5%does not absorb moisture or dirt
Mohs hardness6–7does not scratch, hardly wears
Frost resistancehighsuitable for any climate zone
Acid resistancehighpolish does not dull from rain

Thanks to these properties, granite cladding is the standard solution for a facade plinth: the zone by the apron is exposed to splashes, snow, de-icing salts and mechanical impacts, and granite withstands them better than other rocks.

Limitations include weight (the cladding is heavier than porcelain stoneware or glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC)), difficult processing (diamond tools are required) and, for some grades, natural background radiation. For residential buildings, stone with a confirmed radiation safety class stated in the batch documents is used.

Surface Finishes

The finish determines appearance, slip resistance and how granite ages.

FinishLookWhere to use
Polishedmirror shine, vivid patternvertical surfaces, portals, interiors
Honedsmooth matt surfacefacades, sills, wall cladding
Flamed (thermal)rough, lighter than the natural toneplinths, steps, landings, paving
Bush-hammereddotted texture, mattsteps, plinths, ramps
Split (rock-faced)rough reliefplinths, retaining walls, fences

Polished granite is not used for outdoor horizontal surfaces: it is slippery when wet and in frost. For plinths, flamed or bush-hammered finishes are usually chosen: they hide minor dirt and do not show streaks.

Applications

The main uses of granite on a building:

  • Plinth. The most common task: granite protects the lower wall from moisture and mechanical damage and visually “anchors” the building.
  • Entrances. Steps, landings, ramps, portal cladding.
  • Cladding of the ground floor and podium in residential and public buildings.
  • Full facade cladding on a rainscreen system, for prestige buildings.
  • Details: window sills, flashings, parapets, columns.
  • Landscaping: paving, kerbs, benches, retaining walls.

A granite plinth combines well with lighter materials on the main facade surface: GFRC, porcelain stoneware, render. This reduces the load on the structure and the budget, while the durable stone stays where it is really needed.

When choosing a colour, consider how granite looks with the rest of the finish. Grey and black granites are neutral and suit a light facade; red and pink ones are more assertive and need to be coordinated with the overall palette. The combination is best checked in a 3D visualisation and on large samples in daylight.

Installation

Granite is installed in three main ways:

  1. Ventilated rainscreen facade. Slabs, usually 30–40 mm thick, are fixed to a metal substructure with clips or concealed anchors. The method suits buildings of any height, allows the wall to be insulated and eliminates efflorescence.
  2. Adhesive fixing. For plinths and lower floors: 20–30 mm slabs on frost-resistant adhesive over a prepared substrate; large formats are additionally fixed mechanically.
  3. Mortar bedding with anchors: the traditional method for massive elements such as steps, parapets and blocks.

Key points when installing a granite plinth: a waterproofing barrier, a gap between the cladding and the apron or a sealed joint, movement joints, stainless fixings, and control of flatness and joints. The weight of the cladding is taken into account when designing the foundation and substructure.

Cost

The cost of granite cladding is made up of several parts:

  • stone: depends on the deposit, colour, thickness and finish; rare colours and large formats cost more;
  • processing: cutting to the layout, chamfers, kerfs for clips, complex elements;
  • substructure and fixings: a significant part of the budget for a rainscreen facade;
  • insulation: if carried out at the same time;
  • installation: depends on height, access and complexity of details;
  • logistics: delivery of heavy stone to site.

Quoting prices without input data would be misleading: at TECHNOFORMA the cost of works is determined after a site inspection. For an estimate you will need drawings or photos of the facade, the cladding area, and the desired colour and finish. You can save by limiting granite to the plinth and entrance and cladding the main surface with a lighter material.

Saving on thickness or fixings is not worth it: repairing a fallen or cracked slab at height costs more than the right design from the start.

Types of granite: colour, grain size, origin

Cladding granites are distinguished by colour, grain size and deposit, and these features determine both the appearance and the in-service behaviour of the stone. Related rocks — gabbro, diabase, syenite, labradorite — are also sold on the market under the trade name "granite": they are comparable in durability but differ in composition and pattern.

GroupFeaturesTypical use
Greythe most common group, even pattern, calm backgroundplinths, steps, paving, facades
Red and pinkpronounced grain, rich toneplinths, entrance areas, accent bands
Black (more often gabbro)dense fine-grained structure, deep colour when polishedplinths, portals, details
Yellow and beigewarm range, pattern may be non-uniformfacade walls, combination with light stone
Variegated and greenishdecorative pattern, noticeable variation between blocksinterior, accent inserts

By grain size, the rocks are divided into fine-, medium- and coarse-grained. Fine-grained stone is, as a rule, stronger and wears more evenly, so it is preferred for steps and paving. The current range of slabs and finishes is given on the granite for cladding page.

When choosing from a sample, it is worth remembering that a small tile does not convey the pattern of a coarse-grained or variegated stone. For such stones, several whole slabs or photographs of installed cladding are examined, and the acceptable variation in tone is recorded in the contract. Plain grey and black stones are more predictable: differences between batches are usually minimal, which is convenient for phased delivery to a large project.

Does the natural radioactivity of granite need to be checked?

It does need to be checked, but there is no reason to be wary of granite as a material: it is enough to make sure the batch has a document stating its radioactivity class. Granite is an igneous rock and contains small quantities of natural radionuclides. Their content depends on the deposit and is regulated for building materials.

Class I materials are used for residential and public buildings. Most cladding granites available on the market fall into this class, but this has to be confirmed by a test report or a sanitary and epidemiological certificate for stone from the specific quarry. Materials of higher classes are used only in road construction and outside built-up areas.

In practice the document is requested from the supplier together with the stone certificate. For a large project a copy is included in the as-built documentation: the question often arises at acceptance.

Shaped granite elements for the facade and plinth

Apart from flat slabs, granite cladding almost always includes shaped details: these are what shed water and close off the edges. The list is defined at the design stage, because made-to-measure production takes longer than the supply of standard tiles.

  • Plinth slabs and corner pieces, including chamfered or rusticated ones.
  • Weathering courses with a drip — the top band of the plinth that leads water away from the wall.
  • Copings for parapets, retaining walls and balustrades.
  • Steps, treads and risers for porches and entrance areas.
  • Sill slabs and opening surrounds.
  • Kerbs and setts for the adjoining grounds.

For steps, a slip-resistant finish is chosen and a fall for drainage is provided; more detail is given in the article on natural stone steps. The junctions with the perimeter paving and the construction options are examined in the article on stone plinth cladding.

Typical mistakes in granite cladding

Granite forgives a great deal in service, but not design and installation errors: because of its high weight and low water absorption it is demanding of the substrate, adhesive and fixings. The following miscalculations occur most often.

  • Adhesive chosen without regard to the weight and low absorbency of the stone. Dense granite bonds poorly with ordinary tile adhesives; adhesives for heavy low-porosity stone are required.
  • A polished surface on porches and steps. It is slippery in rain and frost, and this is hard to correct after laying.
  • No movement joints on large planes and at the junction with the perimeter paving.
  • Mild steel fixings in a ventilated system: rust streaks appear after a few seasons.
  • Slabs from different blocks on one plane, which makes the difference in tone and grain visible.
  • Thin large-format slabs fixed with adhesive at height without mechanical restraint.
  • Design of the substrate without allowing for the mass of the cladding, especially in refurbishment.

Most of these mistakes are built in before work begins. For this reason the details, slab thickness and fixing scheme are determined in the design rather than on site.

Granite on different types of building

The role of granite in a project depends on the purpose of the building. In residential complexes it is used to clad the plinth, ground floors and entrance areas — the zones with the heaviest footfall and risk of damage. In business centres and public buildings the stone is often carried up the full height of the main facade, and it is then installed as a ventilated facade with insulation.

In hotels and retail buildings granite is used in entrance zones, on staircases and in the paving in front of the building, where resistance to abrasion and de-icing agents is important. In a private house the volume is usually limited to the plinth, porch and sills, while the walls are finished in lighter stone or decorative panels.

The client of a similar project should define the boundary between granite and the other materials in advance: by plinth height, by ground-floor level or by an architectural band. Both the load on the walls and the volume of shaped elements depend on this.

Logistics is a separate matter. Granite is heavy, so unloading and storage areas, the lifting capacity of plant and the sequence for delivering slabs to each work section are planned on site in advance. Slabs are stored upright on spacers, and polished surfaces are protected from contact with metal and cement mortar. The delivery sequence is tied to the installation programme so that the stone does not lie on site for months.

How to maintain a granite facade and plinth

Granite requires minimal maintenance: periodic washing and inspection of the joints are sufficient. The stone is resistant to most everyday soiling, so complex products are rarely needed.

  • Washing with water and a neutral detergent; for the plinth, once a season or as it becomes soiled.
  • Inspection of joints and sealant at junctions, with damaged sections reinstated before winter.
  • Removal of oil and rust stains with special products for natural stone, after a trial on an inconspicuous area.
  • Water-repellent or protective impregnation for flamed and bush-hammered surfaces, where the pores are more open.

Strong acids and abrasive pastes should not be used on a polished finish: they will not destroy the gloss instantly, but they will make the surface uneven. A polished finish that has lost its appearance can be restored by re-honing — one of the practical advantages of natural stone.

FAQ

How can the plinth of a pile foundation be enclosed?

Options include facade panels on a frame, cladding on a substructure (granite, porcelain stoneware, fibre cement panels), or a brick or block skirt wall on a strip grillage followed by stone cladding. The choice depends on plinth height, soil and budget.

What is the right way to enclose a pile foundation plinth?

You need a frame or skirt wall not rigidly connected to the ground, vents to the underfloor space, a sloping apron and protection against frost heave. The cladding is fixed to the frame, not to the ground.

Which granite is best for a plinth?

Dense granite with low water absorption and a rough finish: flamed, bush-hammered or split. Thickness from 20–30 mm on adhesive and 30–40 mm on a rainscreen system.

Can the whole facade be clad in granite?

Yes, on a ventilated rainscreen system. The weight of the cladding must be accounted for when calculating the substructure and the load-bearing wall.

How much does granite facade cladding cost?

It depends on the stone, thickness, finish, area and installation method. The exact cost is determined after a site inspection and a project-based estimate.

Does granite fade in the sun?

Most granites retain their colour for decades: the colour comes from minerals, not pigments. Some dark and coloured stones may lighten slightly on south-facing facades, and certain grades with iron inclusions may yellow. For important facades, therefore, information is requested on how the stone has performed on completed buildings.

Does granite on a facade need impregnation?

Dense polished granite usually does without impregnation. For flamed, bush-hammered and split surfaces a protective product is useful: it reduces the absorption of dirt and makes washing easier. The product is selected for natural stone and tested on a sample, as some impregnators darken the tone.

Can a granite plinth be combined with a GFRC facade?

Yes. Granite takes on the zone of splashing, impact and de-icing agents, while panels and decorative elements of GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete), which are lighter and allow complex shapes, are installed above. It is important to think through the junction detail: a weathering course or band between the materials and coordination of the cladding thicknesses.
