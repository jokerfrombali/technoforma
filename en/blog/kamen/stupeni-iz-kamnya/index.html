Natural Stone Steps and Porches

Natural stone steps are the most durable option for stairs and porches: stone withstands constant foot traffic, frost and rain and needs no regular repairs. For outdoor porches granite and basalt are chosen most often; for indoor stairs, marble, travertine and dense limestone are also used.

The key selection criterion is safety and durability: a dense rock with low water absorption, a non-slip finish on the tread and sufficient thickness for the span and load.

Stone steps are chosen by private homeowners for porches and terraces, and by developers and architects for the entrances of residential complexes and public buildings. Other rocks and their properties are covered in the section Natural stone for facades and interiors.

Choosing the Rock

The rock determines how the steps will perform after years of use.

Granite steps are the standard choice for an outdoor porch in central Russia: the stone withstands freeze-thaw cycles, salts and mechanical loads better than other rocks. The characteristics of the specific batch are checked against the test report.

Indoors the rock is chosen primarily by style, but for high-traffic stairs (public buildings, lobbies) granite or hard grades of marble are preferable.

Slip Resistance

Slip resistance is the main safety issue for stone stairs, especially outdoors.

  • Tread finish. Outdoors: flamed, bush-hammered, sandblasted or natural split. A polished surface is unacceptable outdoors: when wet or frozen it becomes dangerous.
  • Anti-slip grooves: cuts or strips of rough finish along the tread nosing. Strips in a contrasting tone also mark the edge of the step, which matters for people with limited mobility.
  • Inserts: rubber, metal or abrasive strips set in a groove near the nosing.
  • Tread fall of 1–2% outwards, so that water does not stand and turn to ice.
  • A canopy over the porch reduces the amount of snow and ice on the steps.

For indoor stairs honed and polished surfaces are acceptable, but at entrances and where moisture is possible (from footwear, by a pool) it is better to use matt finishes or anti-slip strips.

Thickness

Step thickness depends on the construction.

ElementApproximate thickness
Tread on a concrete base (outdoors)30–50 mm
Tread on a concrete base (indoors)20–30 mm
Riser20–30 mm
Cantilevered step or step on stringersfrom 40–60 mm, by calculation
Solid block stepequal to step height, 150–180 mm
Porch landing30–50 mm on a concrete slab

The values are approximate. For cantilevered and self-supporting steps, thickness is determined by calculation based on the flexural strength of the stone, span and load. Flaming or bush-hammering removes a few millimetres of the surface, so the initial thickness is taken with a margin.

The overhang of the tread over the riser (nosing) is usually kept small, 20–30 mm; a large overhang on a thin slab can break off on impact.

Installation

Stone porch cladding is carried out on a concrete base.

  1. Base. A concrete porch on its own foundation, separated from the house foundation by a movement joint; otherwise differential settlement will cause cracks. Beneath the base, a non-frost-susceptible bed and drainage.
  2. Waterproofing of the base and drainage away from the building.
  3. Layout. The stone is cut to measurements: treads, risers, landing, side cladding (strings) and the porch plinth. Measurements are taken after concreting to account for the actual geometry.
  4. Installation on frost-resistant natural stone adhesive or on mortar with full bedding: voids under a slab lead to cracks. Risers first, then treads, from the bottom up.
  5. Joints are filled with frost-resistant grout or flexible sealant; movement joints are retained.
  6. Protection: water-repellent treatment for carbonate rocks and sandstone.

Installation at sub-zero temperatures without special measures is undesirable. TECHNOFORMA takes measurements with its own surveyors and provides layout and installation of natural stone; the cost is determined after a site inspection.

Care

Caring for stone steps:

  • regularly sweep away sand and small stones, which act as an abrasive and scratch the surface;
  • wash with water and pH-neutral stone cleaners; no acidic cleaners for marble, travertine and limestone;
  • in winter, use plastic shovels and sand or granite chippings; salt de-icers destroy joints and carbonate stone and leave stains;
  • renew the water-repellent impregnation on porous rocks as it loses effect;
  • repair joints promptly: through a failed joint water gets under the slab and lifts it when it freezes;
  • for indoor stairs, periodically restore the shine by honing and polishing.

It is worth inspecting the porch once a season: check for cracks, chipped nosings, voids under slabs (found by tapping) and failed sections of joints. A local repair, such as replacing one slab or regrouting a joint, costs far less than relaying the whole porch after several winters with water under the cladding.

If the steps have become slippery over time because the finish has worn, the surface can be renewed by re-flaming or bush-hammering on site, or anti-slip grooves can be cut.

What dimensions make stone steps comfortable and safe

A staircase is comfortable when the riser height and the tread depth are related to each other: the lower the step, the deeper the tread should be. For an outdoor porch the usual choice is a riser of 140–170 mm and a tread of 300–350 mm; indoors, slightly higher and narrower steps are acceptable.

The step formula is used as a check: twice the riser height plus the tread depth should come to roughly 600–640 mm. All steps in one flight are made the same height: a difference of even 5–10 mm is felt underfoot and causes falls. For this reason the stone is cut to the actual levels of the concrete base rather than to the design figures.

ParameterOutdoors, porchInterior
Riser height140–170 mm150–180 mm
Tread depth300–350 mm270–320 mm
Outward fall of the tread1–2 %not required
Depth of the landing in front of the dooras a rule, at least 1.2–1.5 mas designed

The values are indicative. For the entrances of residential complexes and public buildings the dimensions are set by the design with accessibility requirements in mind: the staircase becomes shallower, with handrails and a ramp alongside. The tread edge: which profile to choose for stone steps

The edge profile affects both the appearance of a step and its resistance to chipping. A sharp right angle is the first part of the stone to spall, so the front edge is always worked — at the very least a 2–5 mm chamfer is taken off.

  • Chamfer — a simple and inexpensive option, appropriate in contemporary architecture and on outdoor stairs with heavy foot traffic.
  • Quarter round — a rounded upper arris; this edge chips less and is safer in a fall.
  • Half round (full bullnose) — the classic profile for formal staircases; it requires a slab at least 30 mm thick.
  • Moulded profile with a cove — for interiors in a classical style, machined to a template.
  • Laminated edge — a strip of the same stone is bonded to the slab, so the step looks massive at a lower weight; outdoors this solution is less reliable because of the adhesive joint.

Exposed side ends of steps are worked in the same way as the front edge. The end is usually left honed or polished even where the tread is flamed, which brings out the colour of the stone. More on finishes can be found in the piece on types of stone finishing.

Drainage and joints: how to protect a natural stone porch from water

The service life of an outdoor staircase depends less on the stone type than on where the water goes. Treads and the landing are laid with a fall of 1–2 % away from the building so that no puddles remain on the stone and no ice forms in winter.

Water must not get beneath the cladding either. To achieve this, the slabs are laid on a continuous bed of adhesive, the joints are filled with a flexible frost-resistant grout, and a movement joint with sealant is left where the porch meets the wall and the plinth. On large landings such joints are also formed within the paved area — as a rule every few metres, as designed.

  • Drip groove — a saw cut on the underside of the tread overhang that stops water running onto the riser and leaving streaks.
  • Junction with the plinth — the landing slabs are not butted rigidly against the wall; the joint is covered with a stone skirting or sealed.
  • A canopy over the entrance reduces the amount of water and snow on the steps, but falls and joints are still needed.
  • Porous stones are treated with a protective compound after installation; there is a separate article on choosing water repellents and impregnations.

White streaks on the risers during the first seasons are most often efflorescence from the adhesive and the concrete base. They show that water is passing through the construction, and it is the cause that needs to be eliminated, not only the deposit on the surface.

Stone, porcelain stoneware, clinker or concrete: what to choose for steps

Natural stone steps outperform the alternatives in service life and repairability, but they require more precise cutting and a properly prepared base. The table compares the main features.

Clinker stepsslip-resistant surface, standard formatsmany joints, limited choice of sizes

Stone is justified where a porch serves for decades and forms part of the architecture: formal entrances, lobbies, terraces. Basalt slabs and granite for steps and plinths suit such areas. Porch railings do not have to be carved from the same stone: they are often made of GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) in the colour of the cladding, which reduces weight and simplifies installation — see GFRC balustrades.

Typical mistakes when cladding steps with stone

Most porch defects are built in before the first slab is laid — at the stage of the base and the cutting. The following mistakes are the most common.

  1. The stone is ordered from drawings rather than from a survey of the finished concrete: steps have to be trimmed on site and the heights end up different.
  2. The base has not gained strength or dried out: moisture escapes through the joints, and efflorescence and debonding appear.
  3. Laying on separate dabs instead of a continuous adhesive bed: water collects in the voids and the slab is lifted in winter.
  4. A polished or honed tread outdoors without anti-slip treatment.
  5. There is no fall, or it runs towards the door.
  6. Grey cement adhesive beneath light marble or limestone: stains show through on the face.
  7. A thin slab with a large overhang on a staircase with heavy traffic.

Such mistakes are difficult to correct after installation: as a rule the cladding is removed down to the base. Before ordering the stone it is therefore worth approving the layout, the junction details and the finishes on samples.

When and in what order natural stone steps are laid

A porch is clad in the warm season and after the facade works above the entrance have been completed. Adhesives and grouts are, as a rule, designed for a base and air temperature of at least +5 °C; at lower temperatures a heated enclosure is required, which complicates the work and makes it more expensive.

  1. Survey of the finished concrete base and a check of the levels against the door threshold.
  2. Approval of the layout and samples, cutting and finishing of the slabs at the factory.
  3. Preparation of the base: repair of defects, levelling, priming and, where necessary, waterproofing.
  4. Laying from the bottom upwards: first the riser, then the tread; the landing is laid last.
  5. Filling of the joints and sealing of the junctions once the adhesive has gained strength.
  6. Cleaning of the surface and protective treatment of the stone.

New concrete is allowed to gain strength and dry out — usually for at least three to four weeks. Until the remaining works on site are finished, the completed steps are covered with boards or heavy cardboard: edges chipped by scaffolding and trolleys are the most frequent damage to a new porch. The staircase may be loaded after the curing period stated by the adhesive manufacturer.

What to prepare for an estimate of a stone staircase and porch

A plan and a section of the porch with dimensions are enough for a preliminary estimate, while the exact schedule is drawn up after the site survey. The fuller the initial data, the smaller the gap between the preliminary and the final estimate.

  • drawings or photographs of the porch with dimensions: number of steps, length of the flight, size of the landing, presence of side walls;
  • condition of the base: new concrete, an existing porch to be refurbished, a steel frame;
  • the preferred stone, colour and finish, and the edge profile;
  • additional elements: skirting, cladding of side walls, coping slabs, railings;
  • the site address and the desired timescale.

The cost is made up of the stone, cutting and edge working, delivery, preparation of the base and installation; curved steps and non-standard formats affect it noticeably. If the porch is part of the landscaping of a plot, it is useful to coordinate the stone with paths and retaining walls straight away: this is covered in the article on stone in landscaping.

FAQ

Which stone is best for porch cladding?

Granite or basalt with a non-slip finish: they withstand frost, de-icers and constant foot traffic.

How thick should outdoor granite steps be?

A tread on a concrete base is usually 30–50 mm, a riser 20–30 mm. For cantilevered steps the thickness is determined by calculation.

Which finish should be chosen for granite steps?

Outdoors, flamed, bush-hammered or sandblasted; polished surfaces are used only indoors.

Can outdoor steps be made of marble?

In a warm climate and under a canopy, yes, but marble wears faster than granite and loses its polish outdoors.

Why do stone steps crack?

Most often because of voids under the slab, no movement joint between the porch and the house, frost penetrating the base, or water getting under the cladding through failed joints.

What fall is needed on the steps of an outdoor porch?

Usually 1–2 % away from the building — this is enough for water to run off the treads and the landing and not freeze on the stone. The fall is formed in the concrete base rather than built up with the thickness of the adhesive.

Can an old concrete porch be clad with stone?

Yes, provided the base is sound, has no through cracks and is not settling. Weak areas are removed and the geometry of the steps is levelled, after which the survey and cutting are carried out. If the porch has moved away from the house or settled, the cause is dealt with first, otherwise the cracks will reappear in the cladding.

Which edge should be chosen for granite steps outdoors?

A chamfer or a quarter round is the most practical: these edges chip less and are simpler to produce. A full bullnose is chosen for formal staircases and slabs from 30 mm thick.
