Restoring a Historic Facade: Decoration and Stone

Facade restoration means reinstating the historic appearance of a building while preserving as many original elements as possible: stone, ornamental plasterwork, cornices, window surrounds. Unlike repair, restoration does not replace everything worn with new; it conserves and consolidates the original and recreates only what has been lost — from casts, measured surveys and archive records.

The main criterion for choosing solutions is the status of the building. For a listed heritage site, the scope of work, materials and methods are defined by scientific design documentation approved by the heritage protection authority. For historic buildings without protected status there is more freedom, and lost decoration can be recreated with modern materials such as glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC).

The article is aimed at owners of historic buildings, architects and redevelopment developers. Related topics: Facade maintenance, standards and repair.

Survey

Restoration begins with a comprehensive study of the facade. The aim is to understand what original fabric survives, what condition it is in and what has been lost or distorted by later alterations.

  • Historical and archive research — old photographs, drawings, inventories; these are used to reconstruct the original appearance and identify later layers.
  • Measured survey — recording the geometry of the facade, profiles of cornices, string courses and ornament; today laser scanning and photogrammetry are widely used, giving an accurate 3D model.
  • Technical survey — condition of walls, lintels, balcony cantilevers, fixing of decoration, presence of cracks and delamination.
  • Materials research — composition of mortars, renders and paint layers, type of stone, degree of decay, salt contamination.

Based on the results, defect maps are drawn up and a method is chosen for each element: conservation, restoration, reconstruction or replacement.

Casts

Casts are needed to recreate lost or destroyed decoration exactly from a surviving example. If at least one capital, bracket or fragment of a frieze has survived on the facade, a mould is taken from it.

  1. Preparing the original — cleaning, consolidating loose areas, applying a release layer so that the moulding material does not damage the original.
  2. Taking the mould — with elastic compounds (silicones, polyurethanes) and a rigid jacket; for large ornament — in sections.
  3. Casting the model — a plaster model is cast from the mould, on which a sculptor restores lost details and symmetrically completes chips.
  4. Making the production mould — for the chosen replication material.

If no examples survive, the model is sculpted anew from photographs and analogues, or a digital model is built and milled. This route requires a sculptor and approval of the sketches by the conservation architect.

Replacing lost elements

Lost elements are reinstated in three ways:

  • In-situ reconstruction — building up chips and losses with restoration mortars using templates. Used for string courses, cornices, small losses in ornament and stone.
  • Indents — stone piecing-in: the damaged area is cut out and replaced with an insert of stone matched in colour and structure.
  • Full replacement — a lost or unsafe element is made anew and installed on new fixings.

In replacement, fixing and weight matter. Historic decoration is often held by wrought-iron pins that corrode over the years; the new element is fixed with stainless or galvanised fasteners to a calculation. Lightweight elements reduce the load on old masonry and cantilevers — this is one of the reasons why GFRC is used to recreate cornices and large ornament on historic buildings without protected status.

Original elements that can be kept are conserved: consolidated, desalinated, with cracks secured — replacing an original with a copy is always a second choice.

Materials

The choice of material depends on the status of the building, authenticity requirements and loads.

MaterialWhere it is usedFeatures
Natural stonePlinths, cladding, thresholds, indents, decoration on heritage sitesMatched by stone type, colour and texture; maximum authenticity
Lime and restoration mortarsRender, string courses, completing ornamentCompatible with historic masonry, vapour-permeable
Plaster (gypsum)Models, interior ornamentNot suitable for exterior use without protection
GFRCCornices, capitals, balusters, window surrounds, large ornamentLow weight with a thin wall, non-combustible, accurate reproduction of form
Polymer materialsLightweight decoration on low-rise buildingsLimitations on fire safety and ageing

The main requirement is compatibility: the new material must not be noticeably stronger, denser or less vapour-permeable than the adjacent historic material, otherwise the original will start to decay. Exact properties are taken from product data sheets and test reports.

Approvals

For listed heritage sites in Russia, work is carried out under Federal Law No. 73-FZ "On cultural heritage sites". The procedure in outline:

  1. obtaining a work brief from the heritage protection authority;
  2. preparation of scientific design documentation by an organisation licensed for heritage conservation;
  3. a historical and cultural expert review and approval of the documentation by the heritage authority;
  4. obtaining a permit for the work;
  5. execution of the work by a licensed contractor under scientific supervision and designer's supervision;
  6. acceptance with the participation of the heritage authority and reporting documentation.

For historic buildings without listed status but located in protection zones or development control zones, changes to the facade may require approval of the architectural and urban design solution by the local administration. Requirements vary by region, so they are clarified before design begins.

How does facade restoration differ from conservation, repair and reconstruction of lost elements?

These words are often used as synonyms, but they denote different kinds of work with different degrees of intervention in the original fabric. The kind chosen determines the scope of the design, the requirements for the contractor and the budget, so it is defined at the very start together with the conservation architect.

Type of workPurposeWhat happens to original elements
ConservationTo halt decay without changing the appearanceConsolidated and protected in their existing state
RepairTo maintain serviceable conditionRetained, with local defects made good
RestorationTo reveal and preserve historic and artistic valueLater accretions are removed, losses are made up
Reconstruction of lost elementsTo bring back a lost element or appearanceNo original survives; a copy is made from documents and analogues
AdaptationTo enable modern use of the buildingValuable elements are retained, the rest is altered within agreed limits

In practice one facade combines several types: the plinth stone is conserved, the run plaster mouldings are restored, the lost crowning cornice is recreated. If, however, the building has no protected status and the entire cladding is being changed, that is a different task, covered in the article on facade refurbishment.

Why historic decoration and stone cladding deteriorate

A historic facade is destroyed mainly by water, and most of the other causes merely open the way for it. Restoration that has not eliminated the source of moisture gives a result that lasts only a few seasons.

  • Defective roofing and drainage. Leaking eaves, missing cappings on cornices and string courses, failed downpipes.
  • Rising damp. A failed damp-proof course at the plinth and a pavement level raised over the decades lead to salt contamination of the lower part of the wall.
  • Corrosion of metal. Wrought-iron dowels, armatures in mouldings and balcony beams expand and burst the decoration from within.
  • Later repairs. Dense cement mortars and vapour-tight paints trap moisture in the masonry and come away together with the historic layer.
  • Air pollution. Gypsum crusts form on limestone and marble, beneath which the stone loses strength.
  • Vibration and settlement. Cracks at lintels and corners disrupt the fixing of mouldings and applied details.

For this reason work on the roof, rainwater goods and damp-proofing always comes first, and only then work on the face of the wall.

The causes are recorded on a defect map for each area of the facade. Losses that look the same beneath a cornice and at the plinth call for different solutions: in the first case the capping and eaves are repaired, in the second the damp-proofing and the drainage of water away from the wall.

How is natural stone restored on a historic facade?

Stone is restored on the principle of minimum intervention: first it is cleaned and consolidated, then losses are made up, and only as a last resort is a block replaced. The sequence is standard, while the materials are selected on the basis of laboratory analysis of the stone.

  1. Cleaning. Soiling, crusts and later paint layers are removed by gentle methods; the methods and limitations are described in the article on cleaning stone and concrete.
  2. Desalination. Salts are drawn out with poultices, applied several times until the salt content is reduced.
  3. Consolidation. The weakened surface is impregnated with stone consolidants that retain vapour permeability.
  4. Injection and bonding. Cracks are filled, and detached fragments are put back in place on adhesive and stainless steel pins.
  5. Making up losses. Small losses are built up with a mineral compound matched in colour and grain; large ones are closed with stone indents.
  6. Joints. Failed pointing is replaced with a mortar that is softer than the stone.
  7. Protection. Water-repellent treatment is used selectively, after trial areas.

Indents and replacement blocks require stone that is close in type, shade, density and water absorption. On historic facades limestone is the most common, with sandstone and plinth granite less frequent.

Recreating lost decoration in GFRC: when it is appropriate

Glass fibre reinforced concrete is appropriate where the original element has been lost and the requirements for the material of the copy are not restricted by the protected features. This means ordinary historic buildings, later extensions, buildings after redevelopment, and also upper levels, where low weight and reliable fixing matter.

The element is a thin-walled shell that follows the profile of the original and is fixed to the wall through cast-in parts and metal brackets. This is most often how crowning and intermediate cornices, window surrounds, brackets, capitals and balustrades are recreated. The load on the old masonry is lower than from a solid element, and the joints between elements are placed along the articulation of the decoration.

  • the load-bearing capacity of the masonry for anchors is checked by testing on site;
  • the model is made from a cast, a measured survey or a digital scan and is approved before the mould is made;
  • colour and texture are matched to a cleaned historic sample;
  • vapour-permeable mineral paints are used for painting.

How work on the model and moulds is organised for a one-off order is described in the article on bespoke decoration from a sketch.

Before the copies are installed, the remains of old fixings and the weakened masonry beneath them are removed, and the bearing areas are levelled with a repair compound. The top of projecting elements is covered with a metal capping with a drip: without it water quickly leaves runs on the new surface.

In what order restoration work on a facade is carried out

The general rule is from the top down and from structure to finishes. Breaking the sequence means that the finished surface is damaged by subsequent work.

  1. Erection of scaffolding with protective netting and weather protection.
  2. Emergency measures: removal or temporary securing of unstable elements.
  3. Repair of the roof, eaves, cornice cappings and rainwater system.
  4. Strengthening of structures — lintels, balcony slabs, masonry.
  5. Stripping and cleaning of surfaces, removal of later cement patches.
  6. Restoration of stone and render, reinstatement of run mouldings.
  7. Installation of recreated decoration and metal cappings.
  8. Painting, installation of joinery and wrought-iron items.

Wet processes are tied to the warm season, so it pays to carry out the survey, the casts and the manufacture of decoration in winter and to go onto the scaffolding in spring.

Typical mistakes in restoring facade decoration and stone

Restoration mistakes are dangerous because they do not show at once but after several winters, when the scaffolding has long been struck. The most common are as follows:

  • repairing the face of the wall without eliminating roof leaks and faulty rainwater goods;
  • making up losses with cement mortar that is stronger and denser than the historic render and stone;
  • painting a lime substrate with acrylic and other film-forming paints;
  • aggressive cleaning that removes the surface layer of the stone and the traces of original tooling along with the dirt;
  • fixing new decoration on mild steel without corrosion protection;
  • replicating an element from a single random sample distorted by later repairs and layers of paint;
  • simplifying profiles and replacing moulded details with standard items that do not match in proportion;
  • no photographic record or report, without which the next restoration will have to start from scratch.

Most of these mistakes are prevented by designer's supervision: the architect checks the models before replication, and the trial areas of cleaning and painting before work moves to the whole area.

What the owner should prepare before facade restoration begins

Preparation determines both the approval period and the accuracy of the estimate. Before approaching designers it is useful to gather the initial information:

  • information on the status of the building: whether it is a cultural heritage site, an identified site, or located in a protection zone;
  • the protection obligation and the description of the protected features, if they have been issued;
  • archive photographs, drawings, records of earlier measured surveys;
  • details of previous repairs: when, with what materials, what was replaced;
  • a list of visible defects and unsafe areas;
  • access conditions: pavements, neighbouring buildings, the possibility of erecting scaffolding;
  • the expected funding arrangement — as a whole or elevation by elevation.

A brief is then drawn up and facade design is carried out, with the layout of the decoration and the fixing details. For cultural heritage sites the research and design documentation is developed by a licensed organisation, and the manufacturer of the decoration works from its drawings and templates.

FAQ

What are facade decoration and mouldings made of?

Natural stone, restoration mortars, GFRC and polymers. For exterior decoration on historic buildings, stone, mortars and GFRC are chosen most often; plaster is not used outdoors without protection.

How is facade ornament restored?

Survey and measurement, cleaning and consolidation of the original, in-situ completion of losses or casting from a surviving element, making copies and installing them on new fixings.

Can lost decoration be recreated if no examples survive?

Yes, from archive photographs and analogues: a sculptor models it or a digital model is built, approved by the architect and then replicated.

Does facade restoration require approval?

For a listed heritage site in Russia — yes, under Federal Law 73-FZ through the heritage protection authority. Buildings in protection zones may need local approval of their appearance.

Why is GFRC used to reinstate decoration?

Thin-walled GFRC elements are lighter than solid ones, non-combustible and reproduce the form accurately, which reduces the load on old walls and cantilevers.

What is a historic facade painted with after restoration?

With vapour-permeable mineral paints — lime or silicate — which do not form a dense film and do not trap moisture in the wall. The colour is selected from the results of paint investigations; for cultural heritage sites it is approved as part of the design.

Can a historic facade be insulated externally?

On cultural heritage sites external insulation is, as a rule, not permitted: it conceals the decoration and alters the proportions. On buildings without protected status the matter is decided by the design, often in favour of internal insulation or replacement of windows and doors.

At what time of year is facade restoration carried out?

Render, stone and painting work is carried out at stable above-zero temperatures. The survey, design, taking of casts and workshop manufacture of decoration are almost independent of the season, so they are planned for the cold months.
