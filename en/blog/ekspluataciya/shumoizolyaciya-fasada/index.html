Sound Insulation Through the Facade

Facade sound insulation is the ability of the external wall together with its windows to reduce street noise inside the rooms. The main contribution comes from wall mass and window quality: a massive wall insulates well, while a window, a ventilation inlet or a gap at a junction becomes the "weak link" through which most of the sound passes.

The main design criterion is the sound insulation of the weakest element of the facade. Thickening the wall is almost useless if the windows are not designed for highway noise. The cladding and insulation of a rainscreen facade add to the effect but do not replace the calculation of windows and junction details.

The article is useful for developers of housing and hotels near major roads, designers and owners. Related topics: Facade maintenance, standards and repair.

Noise sources

External noise that the facade protects against is divided by source and spectrum:

SourceNature of the noiseProtection notes
Road trafficContinuous, with pronounced low frequencies from lorriesThe main design source for urban buildings
Railway, tramIntermittent, with peaks and vibrationMaximum levels matter, not just averages
AviationPeak levels, broad spectrumAll envelope elements, including the roof, provide protection
Industrial and utility facilitiesContinuous, often tonalLow frequencies are poorly damped by lightweight constructions
Urban environmentPeople, construction sites, restaurantsVariable, important at night

To design noise protection, noise levels at the facade are determined — from a noise map, on-site measurements or a calculation based on traffic flow — and compared with permissible levels in the rooms.

Role of the facade

The airborne sound insulation of a wall increases with its mass per unit area: the heavier the construction, the less it vibrates under a sound wave. Cast-in-place reinforced concrete or dense brickwork provide high sound insulation on their own; lightweight framed walls provide considerably less.

What a rainscreen facade adds:

  • Cladding — an additional screen with an air gap behind it. Heavy materials (stone, GFRC, porcelain stoneware) work better than light ones.
  • Mineral wool insulation — the fibrous material absorbs sound in the cavity and reduces resonances.
  • Multi-layer effect — a "mass–spring–mass" construction insulates better than a homogeneous wall of the same mass, especially at mid and high frequencies.

However, the open joints of a ventilated facade and the air gap limit this effect: for a wall with high mass of its own, the gain in sound insulation from a rainscreen facade is usually moderate. For lightweight walls the contribution of the facade is more noticeable. The exact effect is determined by calculation or by testing the specific construction.

Windows

Windows are the main weak link in facade sound insulation. Even a good insulated glass unit insulates worse than a massive wall, and an open sash or a supply air inlet reduces insulation many times over.

  • Glass unit — sound insulation is improved by different glass thicknesses (so that resonant frequencies do not coincide), laminated glass with an acoustic interlayer and a wider cavity.
  • Frame and seals — tight closure around the whole perimeter, several seal lines.
  • Installation joint — filled without voids and sealed; a gap around the frame lets more sound through than the glass itself.
  • Ventilation — acoustically attenuated supply inlets or mechanical ventilation that allows the windows to stay closed.
  • Glazed area — with full-height glazing, requirements for glass units rise sharply.

The junction of rainscreen cladding with the window unit also matters: reveals in the cladding material with insulation behind them and sealed joints reduce flanking sound paths.

Materials

The sound insulating properties of facade materials are largely determined by mass and structure. Guidelines for comparison (exact values come from calculation and tests of the specific construction):

  • Natural stone and porcelain stoneware — dense, heavy materials, a good screen as part of the cladding.
  • GFRC — a dense mineral material; the elements are thin-walled, so the contribution depends on the thickness and configuration of the element.
  • Mineral wool — absorbs sound well in cavities but does not insulate on its own: without massive layers the effect is small.
  • Foam plastics — rigid closed-cell materials absorb sound poorly; of little use for acoustics.
  • Metal cassettes and thin panels — light, can resonate and "drum" in rain; need anti-vibration measures.

For walls along major roads it is more effective to combine a massive substrate, mineral wool insulation and heavy cladding with sealed details than to try to make up the insulation with a single "soundproofing" material.

Standards

Noise protection requirements are set by the Russian code SP 51.13330 "Protection against noise". It defines:

  • permissible sound levels in rooms of residential and public buildings — separately for day and night;
  • required airborne sound insulation indices for building envelope elements;
  • sound insulation requirements for external envelopes taking traffic noise into account — through the required traffic noise insulation of windows and walls;
  • methods for calculating noise protection.

Sanitary limits for permissible noise in rooms are also set by sanitary rules and standards. For buildings near major roads and railways, an acoustic calculation is carried out in the design: the required sound insulation of windows and walls is determined from the noise level at the facade. Compliance is confirmed by on-site measurements after construction.

The practical conclusion for the client: facade noise requirements are set at the building design stage, not when choosing the cladding. If the building stands next to a major road, it is worth commissioning an acoustic calculation before choosing windows and the facade system — this is cheaper than replacing glass units after complaints from residents or tenants.

How an acoustic calculation for the facade is carried out

An acoustic calculation answers one question: what sound insulation the windows and walls must provide so that noise inside the room does not exceed the permitted level. It is carried out at the building design stage, before the glazing units and the facade system are selected, and repeated whenever the glazing changes significantly.

  1. The design noise level at the facade is determined separately for day and night, for each side of the building and over its height. Upper storeys are often noisier than lower ones, which are screened by neighbouring buildings and greenery.
  2. Permitted levels are set for rooms according to their use: bedrooms, hotel rooms, wards and classrooms are subject to stricter limits than offices and retail areas.
  3. The required insulation of the external envelope is calculated, taking into account the window area, the area of the solid part of the wall and the volume of the room.
  4. Windows, curtain walling and air inlets with confirmed performance are selected, and the composite wall-plus-window construction is checked.
  5. The requirements are fixed in the design documentation and specifications so that the windows are not replaced with simpler ones at procurement.

The outcome is not a single figure for the whole building but a set of requirements by elevation and storey. This makes it possible to install upgraded glazing only where it is really needed and to avoid overspending on courtyard elevations.

Which facade sound insulation indicators should the client know?

Documents for windows and envelope constructions contain several acoustic indicators, and products can only be compared like for like. For protection against street noise, the most important indicator is the one that reflects the spectrum of traffic flow rather than the general laboratory index.

IndicatorWhat it meansWhere it is used
Airborne sound insulation index Rw, dBA single-number rating of the sound insulation of a construction obtained in a laboratoryWalls, partitions, comparison of constructions with one another
Traffic noise insulation RAtran, dBAReduction of urban traffic noise with allowance for its low-frequency spectrumWindows, curtain walling, external envelopes near main roads
Sound level in the room, dBAThe final result that is regulated and measuredBuilding handover, investigation of complaints
Equivalent and maximum levelsThe average level over a period and the peak noise levelRoad traffic is assessed by the average; trains and aircraft by the peaks as well

Two windows with the same Rw may behave differently next to a main road: a construction that cuts off medium and high frequencies well sometimes copes poorly with the rumble of lorries. The supplier is therefore asked for a test report specifically for traffic noise and for the glazing configuration that will actually be delivered. Laboratory values relate to a product installed without defects; the actual result on site is, as a rule, lower because of installation joints and junctions.

Through which junctions sound bypasses the wall

Flanking paths are gaps, cavities and openings through which noise enters the room, bypassing the mass of the wall and the glazing unit. In practice, they are what explains the difference between the calculated and the actual sound insulation of a facade.

  • Window reveals and the sill zone. Voids under the sill flashing and behind the surround act as a sound channel; they are filled with mineral wool and sealed on both sides of the joint.
  • Service penetrations. Openings for air-conditioning lines, cables and ventilation ducts are sealed through the full thickness of the wall after installation.
  • Boxes and recesses. Roller shutter boxes and recesses for outdoor units reduce the wall thickness at its most vulnerable point, next to the opening.
  • Joints between curtain walling and the floor slab. An unfilled gap between the slab edge and the glazed construction also transmits sound between storeys.
  • The air gap behind the cladding. The cavity can carry sound along the facade; horizontal cavity barriers and dense filling around openings reduce this effect.

How the cladding is joined to windows, the parapet and the plinth is discussed in the article on junction details of a ventilated facade. When ventilated facades are installed, these points are checked before they are covered by the cladding: later they can only be corrected by opening it up.

Facade noise protection for housing, hotels and offices: what is the difference?

Noise protection requirements depend on the use of the rooms, so a building beside a main road receives different solutions depending on its function. The general principle is the same: first the layout and the placing of rooms, then windows and ventilation, and only after that the build-up of the wall and the cladding.

Building typeWhat is criticalTypical solutions
Residential complexNight-time noise in bedroomsBedrooms facing the courtyard, glazed loggias as a buffer, acoustic windows with inlets
HotelQuiet guest rooms, peak noise at nightFixed or restricted-opening glazing, mechanical ventilation
Business centreLarge glazed area, daytime occupancyCurtain walling with laminated glass, sealed junctions with floor slabs
Schools, hospitalsStrict limits for classrooms and wardsSeparation of noisy and quiet rooms by elevation, upgraded windows

Layout measures often achieve more than the most expensive glazing units: staircases, corridors and kitchens facing the road act as a buffer in themselves. Cladding and glazing features by building type are described in the articles on hotel facades and on the business centre facade.

Does the cladding generate noise of its own: rain, wind, vibration?

Cladding not only screens street sound but can also be a source of it. Rain noise, wind hum in the cavity and the rattling of poorly fixed elements are among the complaints that an acoustic calculation of the windows does not take into account.

  • Rain and hail. Thin metal sheets, sill flashings and parapet cappings sound noticeably louder than massive slabs of stone or concrete. Flashings above bedrooms are fitted with a damping underlay.
  • Wind. Whistling arises in open profile ends, perforations and narrow gaps; it is eliminated with end caps and by adjusting the joint width.
  • Rattling. Loosened fixings and missing pads between the slab and the clip cause knocking in gusts of wind and under vibration from traffic.

Heavy claddings are less prone to such effects, but only when the fixings are in good order. Unusual sounds from the facade are therefore a reason not for additional sound insulation of the room but for an inspection of the subframe and fixings.

Typical mistakes and what to check before choosing a facade system

Most noise complaints are linked not to the cladding material but to decisions taken without a calculation or altered at procurement. The same miscalculations recur most often.

  • Windows are chosen on thermal performance alone: an energy-efficient glazing unit does not necessarily insulate sound well.
  • At tender stage, the glazing units are replaced with an 'equivalent' of a different configuration without checking the acoustics.
  • Ordinary air inlets without sound attenuation are fitted into acoustic windows.
  • Thin metal cassettes without damping are used on an elevation facing bedrooms, which produces rain and wind noise.
  • Insulation is expected to solve the noise problem although the windows remain the weak link.

What the client should clarify before signing a contract:

  • whether an acoustic calculation exists and what requirements it sets for each elevation;
  • which products the test reports cover and whether they match those being purchased;
  • how ventilation is provided with the windows closed;
  • who checks the filling of installation joints and penetrations, and at what stage;
  • whether control noise measurements in the rooms are planned before handover.

The mineral wool layer installed during facade insulation is also useful acoustically, but it does not replace the window calculation. An overview of the current requirements for external walls is given in the article on standards and codes of practice for facades.

FAQ

How can the sound insulation of external walls be improved?

Increase the mass of the construction or add a multi-layer system with mineral wool infill, but first of all replace the windows with sound-insulating ones and seal the installation joints.

Does a ventilated facade help against noise?

It adds to the effect through the cladding and mineral wool, especially for lightweight walls. For massive walls the gain is moderate, and windows play the decisive role.

Which standard governs noise protection?

The Russian code SP 51.13330 "Protection against noise", as well as sanitary limits for permissible noise in rooms.

Why can I hear the street in my flat if the walls are thick?

Sound passes through the weakest link — windows, supply air inlets and gaps in installation joints.

Which cladding material dampens noise better?

Heavy, dense materials — stone, porcelain stoneware, concrete elements — combined with mineral wool insulation. The exact effect is determined by calculating the construction.

On which storeys is noise from a main road stronger?

Not necessarily on the lower ones. Ground and first floors are often screened by neighbouring buildings, fences and greenery, while middle and upper storeys have a direct line of sight to the road. The noise level at the facade is therefore determined over the height of the building rather than as a single figure.

Does a glazed loggia reduce noise in the room?

Yes, loggia glazing forms an additional barrier and an air buffer in front of the room window. The effect is noticeable as long as the sashes are closed, so airing is dealt with separately, using inlets with sound attenuation or mechanical ventilation.

Should noise be measured after construction is completed?

Control measurements in the rooms are useful on sites near main roads and railways: they confirm that the windows and junctions have been installed in line with the calculation. If the level is higher than expected, the cause is usually found in installation joints, air inlets or penetrations.
