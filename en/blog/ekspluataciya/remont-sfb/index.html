Repairing Chips and Cracks in GFRC

Repairing glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) means restoring an element in place: filling chips, blowholes and cracks with repair compounds compatible with GFRC in strength, deformability and colour. It is justified when the defect is local and does not affect the fixing or the load-bearing capacity of the element; otherwise the element is replaced.

The main criterion for choosing between repair and replacement is the cause of the damage. A chip from a scaffold impact can be closed in an hour, whereas a crack caused by a misaligned substructure will reappear after repair unless the fixing itself is corrected. That is why every repair starts with an assessment.

The article is written for maintenance teams, technical supervision and general contractors who accept or maintain facades with GFRC decoration. Related topics: Facade maintenance, standards and repair.

Causes of damage

Damage to GFRC elements is almost always related not to the material itself but to the conditions it works in. Typical causes:

  • Mechanical impact — knocks during transport and installation, contact with scaffolding and gondolas, vandalism on lower tiers, hail.
  • Restrained thermal movement — if an element is fixed rigidly with no freedom to move, or a movement joint is filled with mortar, stresses and cracks develop as it heats and cools.
  • Substructure errors — misaligned rails, too few fixing points, settlement of brackets; the element works in bending it was not designed for.
  • Corrosion of cast-in anchors — corrosion products expand and split the concrete around the fixing.
  • Wetting and frost damage — water entering through cracks and open pores destroys the surface layer through repeated freezing.
  • Shrinkage cracks — a fine network on the surface formed during curing; as a rule, shallow.

The same visible crack can have different causes, so repairs are not prescribed from a photo.

Assessment

The assessment answers three questions: how serious the defect is, whether it is developing and what caused it. The answer determines whether a cosmetic repair is enough.

For the assessment, the crack width is measured with a feeler gauge or crack gauge, and the crack ends are marked with the date — after a few weeks it is clear whether it is growing. Tapping reveals delamination of the face layer (a hollow sound). Fixings are inspected with access to the element; if necessary, an adjacent element is removed.

Repair materials

The repair compound must work together with the element: have similar strength and modulus of elasticity, good adhesion to the substrate and low shrinkage. A material that is too stiff and strong detaches from the GFRC along its edges; one that is too weak crumbles quickly.

  • Non-shrink cement-based repair mortars with polymer additives — the main material for chips and blowholes. They come in thin-layer (for levelling) and thick-layer (for rebuilding volume) versions.
  • Injection compounds — for filling cracks; the choice between rigid and elastic ones depends on whether the crack is stable or keeps "breathing".
  • Elastic sealants — for movement joints and junctions, not for cracks in the body of the element.
  • Pigments and finishing compounds — for matching colour and texture; an exact colour match is most often achieved by painting or glazing.

Epoxy fillers are used sparingly: they are strong but not vapour-permeable and differ noticeably in colour and ageing. The specific system is chosen from the mortar manufacturer's technical data sheets, taking into account the layer thickness and service conditions.

Technique

A typical sequence for repairing a chip or blowhole:

  1. Preparation. Remove unsound concrete down to a dense substrate, cut the edges to a step rather than feathering them to zero, remove dust.
  2. Dampening or priming — as required by the mortar manufacturer, so that the substrate does not draw water out of the mortar.
  3. Filling. The mortar is applied in layers of permissible thickness; at large depths, with intermediate strength gain. Templates are used to restore the profile of the decoration, and complex relief is moulded from a cast of an intact element.
  4. Curing. Protection from sun, wind and rain, moist curing during the first day.
  5. Finishing. Sanding, texture matching, painting with vapour-permeable paint and water-repellent treatment.

Cracks are first chased out or prepared for injection, then filled. Work is carried out at above-zero temperatures within the limits given in the materials' data sheets. The main mistake is repairing the surface without removing the cause: if the crack was caused by an over-tightened fixing or a misaligned rail, the fixing is corrected first.

When to replace an element

Replacing an element is economically and technically justified when repair will not restore its performance or safety:

  • a through crack or the element split into pieces;
  • damage in the zone of cast-in parts and fixings;
  • corrosion of a cast-in anchor with destruction of the concrete around it;
  • delamination of the face layer over a large area;
  • deformation of the element so that it no longer lines up with its neighbours in plane and joints;
  • damage over such an area that the repair would be visible and cost more than a new element.

When replacing, it is important that the new element matches the others in geometry, texture and colour. This is easier if the moulds and working drawings have been kept. On a ventilated rainscreen facade, one element can usually be removed without dismantling its neighbours, but work at height always requires fencing off the danger zone below.

Repairing glass fibre reinforced concrete before installation and on the facade: what is the difference

A defect found before an element is installed is easier and more reliable to put right than at height: the element lies flat, it is accessible from all sides, and curing conditions can be controlled. A significant share of future repairs is therefore eliminated by incoming inspection on site.

On unloading, each consignment is inspected before the delivery note is signed. The edges and corners, the face, the areas around cast-in fixings and the match between the marking and the installation layout are checked. Damage is photographed together with the element marking and the packaging, so that it is clear where it occurred — in transit, during unloading or in storage.

  • Minor chips to edges and corners — made good on site before lifting; the element is kept until the repair material has gained strength.
  • Blowholes and pores on the face — filled with a thin-layer compound and levelled to match the texture.
  • Cracks, damage at cast-in fixings, distortion — the element is not installed; the manufacturer decides on repair or replacement.

On an installed element the same chip calls for a cradle or scaffolding, protection from rain and work in an awkward position. Hence the rule: whatever can be put right on the ground is put right before lifting, and elements are stored on spacers, in their design position and without bearing on decorative projections.

How cracks in GFRC are repaired: the sequence of work

A crack is made good only once it has been established that it is stable or that its cause has been removed. Otherwise the repair will open up along the same line in the very first season.

  1. Monitoring. Tell-tales or dated marks are placed on the crack, and its width is measured several times at intervals, covering both cold and warm weather.
  2. Correcting the fixing. If the crack is related to the fixing, over-tightened fasteners are eased, freedom of movement at the connection is restored, and a clogged movement joint is cleared.
  3. Preparation. A stable crack is cut out to a shallow depth, dust and loose particles are removed, and the edges are primed where necessary.
  4. Filling. Narrow cracks are impregnated or injected with a low-viscosity compound; cut-out cracks are filled with repair mortar and compacted.
  5. Finishing. The surface is levelled to match the texture and, once strength has developed, tinted and protected with an impregnation.
  6. Checking. The repaired location is marked on the facade layout and checked at subsequent inspections.

Cracks that continue to move with temperature are not filled with a rigid compound: they are either turned into a formed movement joint with flexible filling, or the element is replaced. Such a decision is agreed with the designer, since it changes the joint pattern on the facade.

How can colour and texture be restored after a GFRC repair?

Matching colour and texture is the hardest part of a repair: a technically sound patch often remains visible. The result depends on how the colour of the element was produced and what kind of surface it has.

Trial samples are made on a fragment of the same element or on an inconspicuous area. The shade is compared in daylight and in a dry state: damp concrete is always darker. Repairing joints between elements and fixing points

Joints and fixings are damaged less often than edges, but it is on them that the durability of the decoration depends. Failed sealant lets water through to the cast-in parts, and a loosened fixing changes the way the element works structurally.

  • Replacing sealant. The old material is removed completely, the edges are cleaned, a backer rod that sets the depth of fill is placed in the joint, and a new sealant compatible with concrete and suitable for painting is applied.
  • Restoring joint width. Mortar and debris that have entered a movement joint are cleaned out: a clogged joint becomes a rigid link between elements.
  • Fasteners. Loosened connections are tightened to the design torque; corroded fasteners are replaced with items of at least the original resistance class.
  • Flashings and cappings. On horizontal surfaces the aprons and falls are checked, because standing water quickly ruins both the joint and the surface.

Such work is most often needed on strongly projecting elements, above all on GFRC cornices, which receive more rainfall. An additional fixing point drilled through the body of an element is added only on the designer's decision.

Typical mistakes when repairing GFRC decorative elements

An unsuccessful GFRC repair is usually explained not by the quality of the mix but by a breach of simple rules. The main mistakes are:

  • patching with ordinary sand-cement mortar or tile adhesive, which shrink and debond around the perimeter;
  • feathering the compound to nothing at the edges — the thin edge is the first to break away;
  • working on a dry, dusty or frozen substrate;
  • repairing in sun and wind without curing — the compound dries out and cracks;
  • filling a crack without checking the fixings and joints;
  • filling a movement joint with rigid mortar for the sake of neatness;
  • painting with a film-forming paint beneath which moisture is trapped;
  • no record of the repair — at the next inspection it is impossible to tell whether a defect is new or old.

A separate group of mistakes relates to access. Resting ladders and staging on the decoration, attaching fall-arrest systems to projecting details and moving a cradle hard against the facade often add new chips to those that were to be repaired.

What to prepare when ordering repair or replacement of elements

The more complete the initial information, the more accurately the scope of work is determined and the fewer the repeat visits. The following set is sufficient for an assessment:

  • general and close-up photographs of the defects, indicating the storey, grid line and elevation;
  • the marks of the damaged elements from the layout or working drawings; what these documents are is explained in the article on shop drawings;
  • information on the manufacturer, the year of installation and whether the moulds have been kept;
  • the type of surface finish and the name of the paint, if the facade is painted;
  • the height of the defects and the possible means of access;
  • the history: when the damage was noticed and whether it is changing over time.

When elements are replaced and fixings reinstated, the work is carried out as part of construction and installation works on the facade, with the area beneath the workplace cordoned off. After the repair the facade returns to the normal maintenance regime described in the article on caring for GFRC elements.

What should be done after a repair so that GFRC damage does not recur?

A repair deals with the consequences, while recurring damage is prevented by ordinary maintenance: control of water, joints and mechanical impact. In the first year or two, repaired elements are inspected more closely than the rest.

  • in spring and autumn the repair locations, adjacent joints and the condition of the paint are checked;
  • flashings, cornice cappings and rainwater goods are monitored — water must not run down the decoration or stand on horizontal ledges;
  • the water-repellent impregnation is renewed when water stops beading and begins to soak in;
  • on the lower levels bollards are installed or vehicle access is restricted if the chips were caused by impact;
  • during any work from cradles and scaffolding, projecting elements are protected with padding;
  • all repairs and replacements are entered in the maintenance log with before-and-after photographs.

If defects of the same type appear on many elements of one level or one side of the building, this calls not for yet another local repair but for a survey of the subframe and fixings over the whole area.

FAQ

Are cracks in GFRC dangerous?

A network of hairline surface cracks is usually not dangerous and is closed with impregnation. Through cracks, cracks near fixings and growing cracks are dangerous — they are investigated before repair.

How is a chip in facade decoration repaired?

Remove unsound concrete, dedust and dampen the substrate, fill with non-shrink repair mortar using a template, and after it has gained strength match the texture and colour.

Can a GFRC element be repaired in winter?

Cement-based repair compounds are applied at above-zero temperatures within the limits set by the manufacturer. In winter, temporary measures and repairs inside a heated enclosure are possible.

When is it easier to replace a GFRC element than to repair it?

With a through crack, a split element, damage in the zone of cast-in parts, corrosion of fixings, deformation of the element or a large damaged area.

Will the repaired area differ in colour?

The repair compound is tinted, but an exact match is usually achieved by painting or glazing the repaired area together with the surrounding surface.

Can a chip in GFRC be made good with ordinary cement mortar?

It is not advisable. Ordinary mortar shrinks, bonds less well to the substrate and differs in strength and deformability, so the patch debonds around its perimeter. Non-shrink repair mortars with polymer additives are used.

How long does it take to repair a chip on a facade element?

Filling a small chip takes little time, but the full cycle is longer: the compound has to gain strength, after which the texture is matched and painting and impregnation are carried out. The timescale depends on the material, the layer thickness and the weather.

Does the whole element need to be painted after a local repair?

As a rule, it is not a patch that is painted but the area up to the nearest architectural boundary — a joint, an arris or a corner. This makes the difference in shade between fresh and old paint unnoticeable.
