Falling Facade Cladding: Causes and Prevention

If tiles fall off a facade, it is almost always due to one of three causes: loss of bond between adhesive-fixed cladding and the substrate, failure of a fixing in a rainscreen system, or deterioration of the material itself from water and frost. A falling element is not a cosmetic defect but a threat to people's lives, so the first action is to fence off the danger zone, the second is to find the cause.

The key factor in cladding reliability is a correctly chosen and installed fixing system: for heavy and large-format materials at height, adhesive fixing is risky, and ventilated rainscreen facades with calculated mechanical fixing of every element are used instead.

The article is written for owners, property managers, developers and technical supervision. Related topics: Facade maintenance, standards and repair.

Typical causes

The causes of cladding falling off differ for "wet" and rainscreen systems.

SystemTypical causes of failure
Tiles or stone on adhesiveInsufficient adhesive bond, unprepared substrate, voids under the tiles, water getting behind the cladding and freezing, thermal movement without movement joints, heavy slabs without mechanical back-up fixing
Ventilated rainscreen facadeInsufficient anchor capacity in the substrate, corrosion of brackets and fasteners, missing rivets, incorrectly calculated fixing spacing, panel failure at the fixing point, wind load above the design value
Rendered facade with decorationRender delamination, failure of fixings for string courses and ornament, corrosion of old pins

The common factor for all systems is water. It reduces bond, causes corrosion and splits the material when it freezes, so leaks and faulty drainage accelerate any of the failures listed.

Installation errors

Most cladding failures are related not to the material but to installation. Typical errors:

  • Anchors without testing the substrate. In hollow brick, aerated concrete or old masonry, the anchor capacity can be several times lower than the rated value for concrete. A control pull-out test on site is mandatory.
  • Deviation from the design — increased bracket spacing, substituting cheaper fasteners, missing rivets.
  • Incorrect sliding and fixed points — a rail fixed rigidly at every point presses on the fasteners when heated and loosens them.
  • Contact between dissimilar metals without isolation — accelerated galvanic corrosion.
  • Spot-bonded adhesive cladding instead of full coverage — voids where water collects.
  • No movement joints and joints filled with rigid mortar.
  • Panels damaged during installation — cracks at fixing holes that show up later.

These errors are identified at acceptance through records of concealed work and spot checks; once covered by cladding they are much harder to detect.

Corrosion

Corrosion is a slow but common cause of failure in rainscreen systems and decoration fixings.

  • Galvanised steel brackets and rails lose protection where the zinc is damaged — at cut edges, drilled holes, scratches. Aluminium systems are more resistant but vulnerable in contact with steel and copper.
  • Anchors and plugs — with constant wetting of the substrate, the expansion part corrodes and capacity falls.
  • Cast-in parts in concrete and stone elements — corrosion products expand and split the surrounding material.
  • Aggressive environments — marine climate, industrial emissions, de-icing salts at the plinth accelerate the process.

Signs: orange streaks from under panels and at anchors, blistered paint, spalling around fixing points. Fastener material is chosen at design stage according to the corrosivity of the environment; in repairs, replacements are made with elements of at least the original resistance class.

Prevention

Prevention is made up of decisions at design stage and regular maintenance.

  1. Design — calculating the substructure for wind and dead loads, choosing fasteners for the substrate and environment, mechanical fixing of heavy cladding.
  2. Testing — control pull-out tests of anchors in the substrate before installation begins.
  3. Installation control — records of concealed work on anchoring and substructure, spot checks of tightening and rivets.
  4. Drainage — sound sills, parapets and downpipes, sealed junctions.
  5. Inspections — in spring and autumn and after severe weather; selective opening-up of rainscreen cladding.
  6. Timely repair — replacing cracked panels and corroded fasteners.
  7. Protecting people — as soon as suspicious elements are found, the zone is fenced off and nets are installed.

For adhesive-fixed cladding on existing buildings, periodic tap testing is useful: a hollow sound indicates delamination, and such tiles are removed or additionally secured mechanically before they fall. For rainscreen systems, the main preventive tool is selective opening-up with checks of anchors and rivets, since a fixing failure is usually invisible from outside until the last moment.

Liability

In Russia, responsibility for facade safety during operation lies with the building owner or the organisation to which management has been transferred (in an apartment building — the management company or homeowners' association). It is their duty to organise inspections, routine repairs and urgent measures.

If a defect is caused by construction faults, claims can be made against the developer or contractor within the warranty period; for construction contracts, the Russian Civil Code (Art. 756) sets a five-year period for discovering defective work on buildings and structures, unless otherwise provided by law or contract. Liability for harm caused to third parties or their property by falling cladding usually rests with the owner or management organisation, which can then seek recourse from the contractor at fault.

That is why documents — the inspection log, records, as-built documentation — are not a formality: they show that the building was operated properly and help identify who is at fault.

What signs warn of falling cladding in advance?

Cladding rarely falls without warning: months before failure, signs appear on the facade that are visible on careful inspection from the ground, from windows or from a lift. The task of the maintenance team is to know these signs and not to postpone the response until scheduled repairs.

SignWhat it usually meansHow to respond
A panel projects from the plane or is skewedA loosened clip, rivet or bracketCordon off the area, inspect the fixing from an access platform
Joints have opened unevenly or closed upDisplacement of the subframe, restrained thermal movementCheck the rails and the sliding fixings
A hollow sound when tappedVoids behind the tile, debonding from the adhesiveMark out the area, remove or fix mechanically
Cracks from corners and holesOver-tightened fixings, bending of the panelReplace the panel, correct the fixing
Rattling and knocking in the windPlay in the fixing, missing componentsUnscheduled inspection of the area
Efflorescence and damp patches along jointsWater behind the claddingFind and eliminate the source of the leak
Fragments and fixings on the paving belowFailure has already begunUrgent cordoning and survey

One sign in one area does not yet mean the whole facade is unsafe, but it indicates where to open up. If the same defects recur in different sections, the cause is systemic and a full check is needed.

It is useful to supplement inspection from the ground with binoculars or drone photography: high-resolution images show displaced panels and empty rivet holes on the upper storeys. The results are entered in a log with reference to grid lines and storeys, so that the condition of the same areas can be compared at the next inspection.

Which facade zones are the most dangerous

Loads and wetting are distributed unevenly across a facade, so failures are concentrated in a few zones. These receive the main attention during inspections and selective opening-up.

  • Building corners and the parapet zone. Wind suction here is noticeably higher than in the general zone, and the design fixing spacing is usually closer. If the installers kept the general spacing, the safety margin is reduced.
  • Upper storeys of tall buildings. Wind pressure increases with height, and inspection is difficult.
  • Plinth and ground floor. Splashing, de-icing agents, impacts from trolleys and vehicles, vandalism.
  • Zones beneath parapets, window sills and balconies. With faulty flashings, water gets behind the cladding and destroys the fixings.
  • Reveals and opening surrounds. Narrow make-up pieces with few fixing points.
  • Soffits and inclined surfaces. Cladding on canopies and arches works in tension, and adhesive fixing is particularly unreliable here.
  • Areas at the building's movement joints. Movement of the substrate is transferred to the cladding if the joint is not repeated in the facade system.

What lies beneath the dangerous zone is considered separately. Above entrances, pavements and playgrounds any defect is remedied first, and protective canopies are installed until the repair is done.

What to do after cladding has fallen from a facade

After a fragment has fallen, action runs in two directions: keeping people out of the danger zone and gathering evidence of the cause. Repair without an established cause usually ends in a repeat failure in the adjacent area.

  1. Cordoning. The potential fall zone is closed off with a margin in width; entrances beneath it are relocated or protected with canopies.
  2. Recording. The failure location, the fallen fragments and the fixings are photographed; the debris itself is kept — the fracture surface and traces of adhesive or corrosion reveal the failure mechanism.
  3. Emergency measures. Unstable elements are removed and catch netting is hung over the area.
  4. Survey. Specialists open up the cladding in the failed area and in control areas and check the anchors, the subframe and the adhesive layer; the procedure is described in the article on facade surveys.
  5. Decision on the scope of repair. Local replacement, strengthening of an area or recladding of the whole facade.
  6. Design and execution. Strengthening and replacement are carried out to a design solution, with records of concealed works.
  7. Monitoring. In the first seasons after the repair, inspections are carried out more often than usual.

During the investigation the facade is not left unmonitored. After strong wind, a thaw or heavy rain the failed and adjacent areas are inspected again: it is in such periods that weakened elements most often lose their support.

How is cladding that is losing its fixing strengthened?

The strengthening method depends on the system and on how widespread the defect is. For isolated failures local repair is enough; with widespread problems it is more reliable to change the fixing principle itself.

  • Mechanical back-up for adhesive-fixed cladding. Slabs are additionally secured with anchors or plugs through the body of the slab, or with concealed cramps in the joints. The method is applicable where the substrate is sound and debonding is not yet widespread.
  • Re-bedding of areas. Debonded tiles are removed, the substrate is cleaned back to a sound layer and the tiles are relaid on a continuous adhesive bed with movement joints.
  • Replacing fixings in a ventilated system. Corroded anchors, rivets and clips are replaced with components of a more resistant material, and brackets are added where necessary.
  • Additional anchoring. If the load-bearing capacity of the substrate is below the design value, the bracket spacing is reduced or chemical anchors are used; the solution is confirmed by anchor pull-out testing.
  • Conversion to a ventilated system. When adhesive-fixed cladding is debonding over the whole area, it is removed and a ventilated facade is installed with mechanical fixing of every element.

Strengthening by eye, without calculation and a check of the substrate, creates a false sense of safety. The number, type and position of additional fixings are specified by the designer.

Weak points in fixing stone, porcelain stoneware and GFRC

Each cladding material has its own failure pattern, and different details are examined during checks. The general rule is that a panel must be fixed so that it does not fall out entirely if one point fails.

MaterialWhere failure occursWhat is checked
Natural stoneKerfs and holes for concealed fixings, natural cracks and veinsSlab thickness, kerf depth, edge distance
Porcelain stonewareClips and rivets; on impact the tile shatters into fragmentsClip grip, presence of a safety mesh on the back
Glass fibre reinforced concreteCast-in fixings, rigidly tightened connections with no allowance for movementCondition of cast-in parts, freedom of movement at fixings, joint width
Adhesive-fixed tile and stoneThe adhesive bed and the substrate surfaceContinuity of bedding, movement joints

For stone the safety margin is directly linked to the section: a slab that is too thin breaks at the fixing under wind suction. How the section is chosen for the format and fixing method is covered in the article on stone slab thickness.

For GFRC elements and large stone slabs the separation of fixing functions also matters: load-bearing fixings carry the weight, while restraint fixings take the wind load and do not hinder thermal movement. When all points are tightened rigidly, stresses build up in the element itself and in time lead to cracks at the cast-in parts.

What to require from the design and the contractor so that cladding does not fall

Fixing reliability is established by documents and verified at acceptance. It is enough for the client to require a limited set of materials in order to see that the system has been calculated and installed to the design.

  • a structural calculation of the subframe and the cladding fixings, taking the building's wind zones into account;
  • anchor test reports for the actual substrate;
  • documents confirming the suitability of the facade system used and its fire safety;
  • working drawings with details for corners, parapets, openings and movement joints;
  • records of concealed works with photographs for each section;
  • an as-built layout and a facade maintenance manual.

The calculation and details are developed at the facade design stage, and they must not be replaced by the subframe supplier's standard solutions without reference to the specific building. What exactly to check at handover is listed in the article on facade acceptance.

FAQ

What should be done if facade cladding has fallen off?

Immediately fence off the danger zone, install nets and remove unstable elements if necessary, notify the owner or property manager and arrange a survey to find the cause.

Why do tiles fall off a facade?

Because of loss of adhesive bond with the substrate, voids and water freezing behind the cladding, anchor failure or corrosion of fixings in a rainscreen system, and installation errors.

Who is responsible when cladding falls?

Towards third parties — usually the owner or management organisation. If the cause is a construction defect, claims can be made against the developer or contractor within the warranty period.

How can cladding be prevented from falling?

A design with calculated fixings, anchor testing, installation control, sound drainage, regular inspections and timely replacement of damaged elements.

Is a rainscreen facade more reliable than tiles on adhesive?

For heavy and large-format cladding at height — yes: each element is mechanically fixed to a calculation, and the cladding is separated from wall movement. Provided it is installed and maintained correctly.

Can a fallen slab be put back in its former place?

Only if the slab is intact and the cause of the fall has been found and eliminated. New fixings are used, and the detail is checked on the adjacent slabs. A slab with cracks or chips at the fixing points is replaced.

Can the condition of the fixings be assessed without opening up the cladding?

Partly. Tapping, endoscope inspection through the joints and thermal imaging reveal suspect areas, but the condition of the anchors and subframe is reliably confirmed only by selective opening-up.

Does the whole facade need to be netted if one slab has fallen?

Not always. Netting and canopies are installed over the failed area and the zones above walkways, and the scope of further measures is determined by the survey results. If the defect proves to be systemic, protection is extended to the whole facade until the repair is complete.
