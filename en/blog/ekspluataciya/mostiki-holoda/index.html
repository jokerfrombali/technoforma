Thermal Bridges in Ventilated Facades

Thermal bridges in a facade are areas of the building envelope through which heat escapes faster than through the rest of the wall. In a ventilated rainscreen facade the main thermal bridges are the metal substructure brackets, which pass through the insulation layer and connect the cold cladding to the warm wall.

The key to a correct solution is to reduce heat flow at fixing points and keep the insulation continuous. To do this, thermal break pads are placed under the brackets, the number of fixing points is optimised, and reveals and junctions are carefully insulated.

This article is for designers, technical supervision, installation contractors and clients of rainscreen facades. Related topics are in Facade operation, codes and repair.

Where they come from

Thermal bridges occur where a continuous insulation layer is interrupted or crossed by a more conductive material. Sources in a rainscreen facade:

  • Brackets and anchors — metal conducts heat hundreds of times better than mineral wool; each bracket is a point thermal bridge.
  • Slab edges and in-situ ring beams — if the wall is masonry and the slab reaches the facade uninsulated, a linear bridge runs around the whole floor perimeter.
  • Window and door reveals — hard to insulate, especially with a narrow reveal and the frame set towards the inner face of the wall.
  • Balcony slabs and cantilevers — reinforced concrete elements passing through the envelope.
  • Joints and gaps in the insulation — boards not fitted tightly to each other and around brackets.
  • Parapets, corners, plinth — geometric bridges where the external surface area exceeds the internal one.

The effect of each inclusion is accounted for when calculating the effective thermal resistance: the more bridges, the lower the wall's real thermal performance compared with the "clear wall" figure.

Thermal bridges are dangerous not only because of heat loss. The inner wall surface at a bridge is colder, and if its temperature drops below the dew point of the indoor air, condensation forms on it: damp patches, mould and peeling finishes appear. So details with thermal bridges are checked not only for effective thermal resistance but also for the minimum inner surface temperature. Requirements for thermal protection of buildings are set by the Russian standard SP 50.13330 "Thermal performance of buildings"; specific values depend on the climate zone and building use.

Brackets

The bracket is the main point thermal bridge in a rainscreen facade. Its heat transfer depends on:

FactorEffect
MaterialAluminium conducts heat much better than steel; stainless steel worse than galvanised. So aluminium systems especially need thermal breaks
Section and foot areaThe larger the contact area with the wall, the greater the heat flow
Number per m²Depends on cladding weight and wind load; heavy cladding needs more points
ProjectionA long bracket for thick insulation transfers heat over a longer length
Thermal breakA pad under the foot reduces contact heat transfer to the wall

A bracket thermal break is a pad between the bracket foot and the substrate, sometimes with an insulating sleeve for the anchor. It breaks direct metal contact and reduces losses at each fixing point. Bracket spacing and type are set by the substructure load calculation, so reducing their number for thermal reasons is permissible only within limits confirmed by that calculation.

Substructure accessories deserve a separate note. Bracket extensions are used when the wall is badly out of true or insulation thickness has increased: each extension is an extra metal section through the insulation and an extra connection, so their number and permitted length are limited by the system design. Rail connectors do not affect thermal performance directly, but if a profile joint is made without a thermal gap, the substructure distorts, the insulation gets crushed and gaps appear. Fixings for cladding and ornament that reach the structural wall are point bridges just like brackets and are included in the calculation together with the main substructure.

Thermal breaks

Thermal break pads for ventilated facades are made from materials with low thermal conductivity and enough compressive strength not to be crushed under the anchor load. Used are:

  • Paronite — a traditional pad material, resistant to load and ageing.
  • Polymer pads — polyamide, polypropylene, foamed polymers, sometimes reinforced.
  • Combined solutions — a pad under the foot plus a thermal sleeve on the anchor that prevents contact between the anchor metal and the bracket.

Pad requirements: no smaller than the bracket foot, keeps its thickness under load, resistant to moisture and temperature cycling, compatible with the system's fire requirements. The pad must be part of a tested facade system kit, not chosen by installers on site. Installation quality also matters: a pad that has shifted or is missing under some brackets cancels the effect.

Thermal imaging

A thermal imaging camera is the main tool for finding thermal bridges in a completed building. It shows the surface temperature distribution: over thermal bridges the outer surface is warmer and the inner surface colder than the surrounding wall.

  • When to survey. In the heating season with a stable indoor–outdoor temperature difference; in overcast weather or at night, with no direct sun on the facade, no precipitation and no strong wind.
  • Outside or inside. In a rainscreen facade the cladding screens the wall, so bridges are less visible from outside; an internal survey is often more informative, showing cold corners, reveals and slab edges.
  • Method. The procedure for thermographic inspection of building envelopes is set by the Russian standard GOST 26629 "Buildings and structures. Method of thermovision control of enclosing structures thermal insulation quality".
  • Interpretation. A thermogram shows anomalies; the cause is confirmed by opening up or comparison with the design.

A thermographic survey is useful when accepting a new facade and when there are complaints about cold walls in an occupied building.

Signs of thermal bridges noticeable without a camera: wall areas, corners and reveals that feel cold to the touch in the heating season; dark streaks and patches on wallpaper and plaster in corners, above windows and along the ceiling; condensation on reveals and at the bottom of the wall by the floor; mould behind furniture on external walls. You can go over the inner surface with a contact or infrared thermometer, comparing temperatures at different points. This is rough diagnosis: it shows where to look but not why.

Three typical mistakes when searching for thermal bridges: surveying on a sunny day or right after sunset while the cladding is still releasing heat; trying to find bridges in a rainscreen facade only from outside, where the cladding and cavity mask anomalies; drawing conclusions from a single thermogram without comparing with the design and opening up.

Remedies

Remedying thermal bridges depends on their type and the project stage.

  1. At design stage — choosing a system with thermal breaks, calculating optimal bracket spacing, details for insulating reveals, slab edges and balconies. This is the cheapest stage.
  2. During installation — checking pads under every bracket, cutting insulation tightly around brackets, staggering boards, insulating reveals before cladding. Recorded in concealed works records.
  3. On an existing facade — partial removal of cladding in problem areas, extra insulation of reveals and gaps, fitting pads when replacing brackets, restoring the wind barrier.
  4. From inside — as a temporary measure for individual cold corners; internal insulation moves the dew point into the wall and requires a vapour barrier.

After remedial work, the effect is confirmed by a repeat thermographic survey in the heating season.

If cold walls come with damp and mould, the insulation is checked at the same time: a layer wetted by leaks or condensation loses its thermal performance, and the bridge then forms not in the metal but across a whole zone of the wall. Such insulation is replaced and the source of moisture eliminated.

Details to prioritise in the design:

  • slab edges — insulation is carried over the edge without breaks, especially with infill masonry set back from the slab;
  • window reveals — the window is moved into the insulation plane or the insulation is returned onto the frame, and the reveal cladding is installed after insulating;
  • parapets — insulated on the outside and top, with a sealed parapet capping;
  • plinth — plinth insulation is butted to the facade insulation without a gap;
  • balcony slabs — in new construction, structural thermal breaks are used; in existing buildings, the cantilever is insulated on all sides.

How are thermal bridges accounted for in the thermal calculation of a ventilated facade?

In the calculation, thermal bridges are accounted for through the reduced thermal resistance: the losses through each linear and point element are added to the losses through the plain wall. The resulting figure shows how the wall actually performs, and it is this figure that is compared with the required value under SP 50.13330.

Specific heat losses are determined for every non-uniformity. For linear elements (slab edge, reveal, parapet) these are losses per linear metre; for point elements (bracket, insulation anchor) they are losses per item. The values are obtained by calculating the temperature fields of the detail or taken from reference tables; the characteristics of typical non-uniformities are collected in SP 230.1325800.

The ratio of the reduced resistance to the nominal one is called the thermal uniformity coefficient. For ventilated facades it is, as a rule, noticeably below one and depends on the sub-frame material, the number of brackets per square metre and the quality of the junction details. The heavier the cladding and the more window openings there are, the stronger the influence of non-uniformities.

The designer needs the following input data for the calculation:

  • climatic parameters of the construction region and the design indoor air temperature;
  • the wall build-up layer by layer with the thermal conductivity of the materials;
  • the number and type of brackets per square metre, their material and cross-section;
  • the number of insulation anchors;
  • the length of reveals, slab edges, parapets and plinth junctions;
  • the presence of balcony slabs and other penetrating elements.

The temperature field calculation is also needed for a second check — the hygiene one. The standards require the temperature of the internal wall surface at a non-uniformity to be above the dew point of the indoor air. A detail may pass on average heat loss yet fail this condition, in which case it is reworked regardless of the overall result.

The practical conclusion for the client: comparing facade systems by insulation thickness alone is incorrect. Two walls with the same 150 mm of mineral wool may differ noticeably in actual thermal performance if one has aluminium brackets without pads and the other has stainless steel brackets with a thermal break. The design brief should therefore explicitly require a calculation that covers all heat-conducting inclusions, not just the main layer.

Thermal conductivity of sub-frame materials: a comparison table

The thermal bridge at a bracket is stronger the higher the conductivity of the metal and the larger the cross-section passing through the insulation. The order of magnitude is easy to compare in a table; the values are indicative and depend on the alloy grade and material density.

MaterialThermal conductivity, W/(m·K), indicativeWhat it means for the facade
Aluminium alloys160–220The highest losses through the bracket; a thermal break is essential and the cross-section is kept as small as possible
Galvanised steel45–58Losses several times lower than aluminium at the same cross-section; corrosion protection matters
Stainless steel15–20The lowest losses among metals; used for heavy cladding and aggressive environments
Reinforced concrete1.7–2.0Slab edges and balcony slabs act as linear bridges
Compressed fibre gasket material, polyamide0.2–0.4Materials for thermal break pads
Mineral wool0.035–0.045The main thermal insulation layer

Geometry has to be compared as well as material. A thin-walled steel bracket with perforation in the insulation zone and a small base plate transfers less heat than a massive aluminium profile of the same load-bearing capacity. Some systems use brackets with a polymer insert or composite designs in which the metal is interrupted within the insulation; the suitability of such solutions is confirmed by strength and fire tests of the system.

The table shows why changing the sub-frame material sometimes has a greater effect than extra centimetres of insulation. The choice of metal is not driven by thermal performance alone, however: cladding weight, fire requirements, corrosion resistance and cost are all taken into account. The design and types of supporting frames are covered in the article on the ventilated facade sub-frame.

Thermal bridges in facades with heavy GFRC and stone cladding

Heavy cladding calls for stronger brackets and anchors, so thermal bridges in stone and glass fibre reinforced concrete facades are designed with particular care. The aim is to transfer the load to the structure through the minimum number of metal cross-sections.

  • Fixing into floor slabs. Load-bearing brackets are placed at the floor slabs, where the substrate is stronger, and only wind restraints of smaller cross-section are left on the infill wall.
  • Large format. GFRC panels with cast-in fixings are hung on a limited number of points, so there are fewer penetrating elements per square metre of facade than with small-format cladding.
  • Separate bracket functions. Load-bearing brackets take the weight, restraint brackets take only wind; the cross-section of the latter can be smaller.
  • Stainless fixings. Stone slabs on concealed fixings use stainless steel components, which conduct heat less than galvanised ones.

Architectural decoration — cornices, string courses, window surrounds — is fixed with its own cast-in parts and brackets. Each such detail is checked as a separate non-uniformity: a cantilevered cornice with a large projection needs a strong support, and the insulation around it must be laid without voids. Where the calculation allows, decoration supports are aligned with slab edges so as not to create new penetrations in the infill wall.

For the thermal calculation of such facades the designer needs input data from the cladding manufacturer: weight per square metre, the layout of cast-in fixings and the permissible spacing between support points. The earlier this information appears in the design, the lower the chance that the number of brackets will have to be increased on site.

Stone cladding bedded in mortar, without an air cavity, presents a different picture: there are no brackets, but there is usually no external insulation layer either, so thermal performance is determined by the wall itself. When moving to a ventilated arrangement with insulation, losses through the wall fall, but a sub-frame appears and its influence is accounted for under the general rules.

Which insulation installation errors create thermal bridges?

A significant share of losses arises not in the metal but in the insulation layer itself — because of gaps, voids and incorrect fixing of the slabs. These defects are hidden by the cladding, so they are checked before the facade is closed.

  • Unfilled gaps between slabs and around brackets. A gap a few millimetres wide already acts as a channel for cold air; it is filled with offcuts of the same insulation.
  • Single-layer installation without staggered joints. With two layers, the joints of the second layer are offset from the first, leaving no through joints.
  • Poor contact with the substrate. On an uneven wall an air pocket forms behind the slab, and air moves through it.
  • Too many or too few insulation anchors. An anchor with a metal expansion pin is also a point bridge, so products with a thermally insulated head are used and installed in the quantity set by the design.
  • Tears in the wind barrier membrane, where the system provides for one. Air movement through fibrous insulation reduces its effectiveness.
  • Wetting during installation. Insulation left unprotected in the rain takes a long time to dry and retains heat less well in the meantime.

Installation is inspected section by section: before the rails and cladding are fitted, the site supervisor examines the layer, spot-checks thickness and contact, and records the result in a certificate. Correcting a defect at this stage takes hours; after the facade is closed it means removing cladding.

The requirements for density, thickness and fixing pattern of the slabs are set out in detail in the article on ventilated facade insulation.

Checklist: controlling thermal bridges at design and installation stage

Thermal bridges are cheapest to eliminate before installation begins, so it is convenient to split the check into two stages — design review and acceptance of concealed works.

At design stage

  1. the thermal calculation takes account of brackets, anchors, reveals and slab edges rather than the plain wall only;
  2. the specification lists thermal break pads and their material;
  3. the bracket spacing is justified by a load calculation and is not inflated as a safety margin;
  4. insulation details are developed for window reveals, the parapet, the plinth and balcony slabs;
  5. separate details exist for corner zones and decoration.

During installation

  1. pads are fitted under every bracket and are not displaced;
  2. extensions and additional brackets are used only with the designer's approval;
  3. the insulation is laid without gaps and the cut-outs for brackets are tight;
  4. the concealed works certificate for insulation is signed before the cladding is installed, with photographic records;
  5. after the first heating season a thermal imaging survey of the problem zones is carried out.

The inspection results are recorded so that they can be used later: photographs of details referenced to grid lines and floors, marks on the as-built drawing, and a list of deviations with the decisions taken on them. If complaints about cold walls arise, these records make it possible to see quickly whether the area was built to the design and to narrow the search without unnecessary opening-up.

TECHNOFORMA takes these requirements into account in facade design: thermal and structural calculations are carried out together, so that a decision on one parameter does not worsen another.

Which is more cost-effective: thicker insulation or removing thermal bridges?

It is usually more effective to reduce losses through non-uniformities first and only then add insulation. An extra layer of wool increases the cladding stand-off, the bracket length and the load on the anchors, and so partly reinforces the bridges itself.

The rational sequence is as follows. First the sub-frame layout is optimised: material, spacing, bracket type. Then the linear details are worked through — reveals and slab edges, which in buildings with a large glazed area may account for a substantial share of losses. Only after that is the insulation thickness adjusted to the required value of reduced resistance.

Window details are assessed separately. A window set in the plane of the load-bearing wall leaves a reveal with a thin layer of insulation, and the linear losses around the opening become comparable to the losses through dozens of brackets. Moving the window into the insulation zone or insulating the rebate reduces these losses without changing the thickness of the main layer.

When comparing options, the consequences for the structure are considered as well as material cost. Thicker insulation needs long brackets that are more heavily loaded in bending; reveals and sills become wider and the consumption of trim elements rises. Thermal breaks and spacing optimisation have no such side effects, which is why they are considered first.

On a building in use the choice is narrower: opening up the whole facade for the sake of pads is not practical, so work is confined to local cold zones. If the system as a whole is worn out, the matter is resolved as part of a facade refurbishment with a new thermal calculation.

How does the regional climate affect the requirements for ventilated facade details?

The colder and longer the heating season, the more each thermal bridge costs and the stricter the requirements for details. The same sub-frame solution may be acceptable in the south and inadequate in Siberia.

In regions with severe winters the required thermal resistance rises, insulation thickness reaches 200 mm and more, and brackets become longer. In these conditions steel and stainless sub-frames, two-layer insulation and temperature field calculations for every non-standard detail are used more often. The internal surface temperature at corners and reveals becomes critical: it is checked against condensation at the design outdoor air temperature.

A humid maritime climate adds another factor — corrosion. In coastal cities the sub-frame is selected for enhanced resistance, and stainless steel is advantageous here for two reasons at once: it lasts longer and conducts heat less than other metals.

In southern regions the heating season is shorter, but summer overheating matters. The ventilated cavity and massive cladding reduce heat gain into the rooms, yet heat-conducting inclusions keep working in the opposite direction — they increase the cooling load. For the seismic regions of the Caucasus, reinforced fixing is additionally taken into account, which raises the number of metal elements. The link between climatic conditions and the choice of construction is examined in the article on facade and climate.

The general approach does not change: first a calculation for the specific region and building, then the choice of sub-frame and details, and only then the materials specification.

FAQ

Why is a paronite pad used in a rainscreen facade?

It is placed under the bracket foot as a thermal break: it breaks direct metal contact between bracket and wall and reduces heat loss at the fixing point.

What is a bracket thermal break?

A pad of low-conductivity material between the bracket and substrate, sometimes with a thermal sleeve on the anchor. It is part of the facade system kit.

How are thermal bridges found on a facade?

By thermographic survey in the heating season with an indoor–outdoor temperature difference; for rainscreen facades an internal survey is also informative. The method is set by the Russian standard GOST 26629.

Can the number of brackets be reduced for better thermal performance?

Only within limits confirmed by the substructure calculation for cladding self-weight and wind loads.

Which thermal bridges matter most in a rainscreen facade?

Brackets, slab edges, window reveals and balcony slabs; their effect is accounted for through the effective thermal resistance.

Why does mould appear at thermal bridges?

At a bridge the inner wall surface is colder. If its temperature falls below the dew point of the indoor air, moisture condenses and mould grows on the damp area. The solution is to eliminate the bridge itself, not just treat the surface.

Is a thermal break pad needed under a steel bracket?

Yes. Steel conducts heat less than aluminium but many times better than insulation, so direct contact between the bracket and the wall remains a thermal bridge. The composition of the detail is defined by the facade system's album of technical solutions.

Do insulation anchors affect the thermal performance of the wall?

They do, though less than brackets. An anchor with a metal pin and no insulated head is a point bridge, and at several per square metre the total losses become noticeable. Anchors with a thermally insulating head are therefore used and installed in the design quantity.

Can thermal bridges be eliminated by insulating from the inside?

As a rule this is undesirable: internal insulation shifts the condensation zone into the wall and may lead to moisture build-up in the structure. It is more reliable to remove the cause from the outside — restore the insulation and rework the details.
