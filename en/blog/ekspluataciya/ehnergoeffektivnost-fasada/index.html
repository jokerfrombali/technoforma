Facade Energy Efficiency and Building Heat Loss

Facade energy efficiency is the ability of external walls to retain heat in winter and not overheat in summer with minimal energy spent on heating and cooling. It is determined by the reduced thermal resistance of the wall: the thickness and quality of insulation, the number of thermally conductive elements and the airtightness of the construction.

The main criterion when choosing a solution is not maximum insulation thickness but the absence of weak spots: thermal bridges, gaps in the insulation, draughty details. A well-designed ventilated rainscreen facade with continuous insulation and thermal breaks in the fixings noticeably reduces heat loss through the walls.

The article is written for developers, designers, owners and property managers. Related topics: Facade maintenance, standards and repair.

Heat loss

A building's heat loss is distributed between several paths: through walls, windows, the roof, the floor above the basement or on the ground, and with ventilation and air infiltration. The share of each path depends on the architecture, glazed area, number of storeys and condition of the structures. In multi-storey buildings walls are one of the major sources of loss, so a building's thermal protection is unthinkable without a good facade.

Heat loss through walls depends on:

  • thermal resistance — the higher it is, the lower the heat flow for the same temperature difference;
  • wall area and the difference between indoor and outdoor temperatures;
  • thermally conductive elements — brackets, floor slabs extending to the facade, balcony slabs, reveals;
  • moisture in the insulation — wet insulation conducts heat much better than dry;
  • air permeability — wind passing through fibrous insulation without a wind barrier carries heat away.

Actual wall losses are almost always higher than those calculated for the plain wall area, so the codes require the reduced thermal resistance to be used, adjusted for non-uniformities.

Standards

Thermal protection requirements are set by the Russian code SP 50.13330 "Thermal performance of buildings". The logic of the code:

  • Element requirements — the required thermal resistance of walls, windows and roofs. It depends on the heating degree-days (the climate of the area) and the building's use: requirements for Moscow are higher than for southern regions.
  • Overall requirement — the specific thermal performance characteristic of the building as a whole.
  • Sanitary requirement — the internal wall surface temperature must not drop below the dew point, otherwise condensation and mould appear.
  • Protection from excess moisture — moisture must not accumulate in the construction from year to year.

In addition, Federal Law No. 261-FZ "On energy saving" and related requirements define energy efficiency classes for buildings. For new construction and major repairs, compliance is confirmed by an energy passport as part of the design.

Insulation

The main way to improve wall thermal protection is external insulation. Placing insulation on the outside is better than on the inside: the wall stays in the warm zone, the dew point moves into the insulation, and floor slabs do not become thermal bridges.

In a rainscreen facade the insulation is fixed with disc-head plugs, boards are laid staggered, without gaps and tight to the brackets. The thickness is determined by a thermal calculation to SP 50.13330 for the specific region. For taller buildings non-combustible insulation is used in line with fire safety requirements.

Thermal bridges

Thermal bridges are areas through which heat escapes faster than through the main wall area. In facades these are:

  • metal substructure brackets passing through the insulation;
  • edges of floor slabs and balcony slabs reaching the facade;
  • window reveals and frame junctions;
  • gaps in the insulation, loose joints between boards;
  • metal anchors and plugs.

At thermal bridges not only does heat loss increase, but the internal surface temperature also falls — condensation and mould appear in corners and at reveals. Remedies: thermal break pads under brackets, brackets made of less conductive materials, optimising the number of fixing points, insulating reveals and slab edges, balcony connections with thermal breaks. A detailed analysis is in a separate article on thermal bridges in rainscreen facades.

The effect of thermal bridges is easy to underestimate: with many brackets without thermal breaks and uninsulated reveals, the actual reduced thermal resistance of the wall can be noticeably lower than that calculated for the main area. That is why rainscreen systems are compared not only by insulation thickness but also by the specific heat losses through fixing points, which are given in the system's technical documentation.

Economics

The economic effect of facade insulation has several components:

  • Lower heating costs — in proportion to the reduction of heat loss through the walls; depends on the climate, tariffs and the initial condition of the building.
  • Lower load on building services — in new construction, smaller heating and cooling capacity can be designed.
  • Durability — a wall in the warm zone is less exposed to freeze–thaw cycles.
  • Comfort and no mould — fewer complaints from residents and tenants, higher attractiveness of the property.
  • Energy efficiency class — for the developer, it affects compliance and marketing.

The payback period of insulation varies greatly by region and building, so it is calculated for the specific building: calculated heat losses before and after, the cost of heat and the cost of works are compared. In reconstruction it usually pays to combine insulation with replacing the cladding — scaffolding, access and removal costs are incurred only once.

How the reduced thermal resistance of a wall is calculated

The reduced thermal resistance is obtained in two steps: first the nominal resistance of the plain wall is calculated, then it is lowered to account for all thermally conductive inclusions. The nominal value is the sum of the layer resistances, each equal to the layer thickness divided by the thermal conductivity of the material. The insulation makes the main contribution: mineral wool boards usually have a conductivity within 0.035–0.045 W/(m·°C), whereas that of concrete and brick is tens of times higher.

The transition from the nominal to the reduced value is expressed by the thermal homogeneity coefficient. For ventilated facades it generally lies in the range of 0.6–0.9 and depends on the bracket material, the number of brackets per square metre, the presence of thermal breaks and the quality of reveal insulation. In other words, a wall with a calculated plain-area resistance may lose between a tenth and a third of its thermal protection at the junctions.

Does the cladding affect the energy efficiency of a facade?

Directly, hardly at all: in a rainscreen system the cladding is separated from the insulation by a ventilated cavity and is usually not included in the thermal calculation of the wall. Its influence is indirect and comes through weight and format, which determine the number and section of the brackets.

  • Cladding weight. The heavier the panel, the stronger the subframe and the more metal passes through the insulation. Thin-shell GFRC panels and porcelain stoneware load the wall less than thick stone slabs.
  • Format. Large panels are fixed at fewer points per square metre than small-format tiles, but each point carries more.
  • Decor. Cornices, string courses and surrounds require additional brackets; they should be included in the thermal calculation rather than added afterwards.
  • Protection of the insulation. The cladding shields the insulation from rain and sun, so joint filling and water drainage affect the moisture content and hence the conductivity of the boards.

For this reason it is rational to design the subframe and the cladding together: the panel layout sets the spacing of the rails, and that determines the number of thermal bridges. In the installation of ventilated facades this is resolved at the detailed design stage, before the brackets are purchased.

Summer overheating: how a facade reduces the cooling load

The energy efficiency of a facade is assessed not only over the heating season. In southern regions and in buildings with a large glazed area, cooling costs are comparable with heating costs and sometimes exceed them.

In summer a ventilated facade acts as a solar screen. The cladding receives direct radiation and heats up, but the heat is carried away by the rising air flow in the cavity and only part of it reaches the wall. The insulation and the massive load-bearing wall additionally smooth out daily temperature fluctuations.

  • light-coloured cladding reflects more solar radiation and heats up less than dark cladding;
  • a continuous cavity with an open inlet at the bottom and outlet at the top provides draught; a blocked cavity stops removing heat;
  • louvres, projecting canopies and deep reveals shade the glazing, through which most summer heat gains enter;
  • relief panels and deep modelling cast their own shadow on the wall plane.

When choosing solutions for a hot climate it is useful to consider the wall and the glazing together: reducing heat gains through the windows usually has a greater effect than extra centimetres of insulation.

Which installation mistakes reduce the thermal protection of a wall?

The design thermal resistance is achieved only with careful installation. Most losses are caused by defects that are covered by the cladding and are no longer visible afterwards.

  1. Gaps between insulation boards. A gap of a few millimetres around a bracket or at a board joint acts as a channel for cold air. Boards are trimmed to fit and voids are filled with the same material.
  2. A single layer without staggered joints. With two-layer insulation the joints of the outer layer are offset from those of the inner layer; with a single layer especially tight laying is required.
  3. Too few dowels. Over time the boards come away from the wall and a draughty void forms behind them.
  4. Wetting during installation. Insulation left in the rain without cladding or protection takes a long time to dry and retains heat less well until it does.
  5. Missing or torn wind barrier. Where the design provides for it, the wind barrier membrane must be laid with overlaps and without sagging.
  6. Uninsulated reveals. The window junction is left for later and closed with metal without insulation; the correct solutions are described in the article on ventilated facade junction details.

All the works listed are concealed works, so they are accepted under inspection certificates before the cladding is installed, with photographic records for each work section.

How to check a building's heat loss after construction

Actual thermal protection is checked by thermal imaging and selective opening-up. The survey is carried out during the heating season with a stable temperature difference between inside and outside, usually at least 10–15 °C, and in the absence of direct sun, precipitation and strong wind.

  • From outside. A thermogram of a ventilated facade shows mainly the temperature of the cladding, so an external survey reveals only gross defects and areas where warm air escapes.
  • From inside. A survey of internal surfaces is more informative: cold corners, reveals, areas near floor slabs and fixing points are visible.
  • Opening-up. In suspect locations one or two panels are removed and the tightness of laying, the moisture content of the insulation and the condition of the fixings are checked.
  • Comparison with the design. The measured internal surface temperatures are compared with the calculated values and with the dew point for the room.

Such a check is included in the programme at handover or carried out later if complaints about cold walls and condensation arise. The procedure and the contents of the report are described in the article on facade surveys. If defects are confirmed, the scope of repair is determined from the results of the opening-up: from local addition of insulation to rebuilding a section with renewed facade insulation.

What to request from the contractor to compare systems by thermal protection

To compare proposals by energy efficiency rather than only by price per square metre, the same set of data is requested from each bidder. Differences in insulation thickness and in the number of brackets then become explainable.

  • a thermal calculation of the wall with the reduced thermal resistance for the specific subframe and construction region;
  • the number of brackets per square metre, their material and the presence of thermal break pads;
  • the grade, density and design conductivity of the insulation, the number of layers and the fixing method;
  • solutions for reveals, the parapet, the plinth and slab edges in the form of details rather than general statements;
  • technical documentation for the system with data on specific losses through the fixing elements;
  • the procedure for accepting concealed works and the conditions for thermal imaging checks after installation.

An option with thinner boards but with thermal breaks and well-developed details often proves warmer than one with thick insulation on a dense steel subframe. The final comparison is therefore made by the reduced value and by cost of ownership: installation costs plus heating and cooling costs over the design service period of the building.

FAQ

How can heat loss through walls be reduced?

Install external insulation of the calculated thickness, eliminate thermal bridges with thermal breaks and insulated reveals, and protect the insulation from wind-washing and wetting.

What insulation thickness does a facade need?

It is determined by a thermal calculation to the Russian code SP 50.13330 for the specific region and building use, taking into account the thermally conductive elements of the substructure.

What does a ventilated facade do for energy efficiency?

It provides a continuous insulation layer on the outside of the wall, removes moisture through the ventilated cavity and allows thermal bridges to be eliminated with thermal breaks. The insulation stays dry and keeps its properties.

Does facade insulation pay for itself?

The payback period depends on climate, tariffs and the initial condition of the building; it is calculated individually. It is most cost-effective to combine insulation with replacing the cladding.

Which standards define a building's thermal protection?

The Russian code SP 50.13330 "Thermal performance of buildings" and energy saving legislation, including energy efficiency classes.

Does the cladding material affect the thermal protection of the wall?

Directly, hardly at all: the cladding is behind the ventilated cavity and is usually not included in the thermal resistance calculation. Indirectly its weight matters, since it determines the number and section of the brackets passing through the insulation.

When should a thermal imaging survey of a facade be carried out?

During the heating season, with a stable temperature difference between inside and outside, without direct sun or precipitation. For ventilated facades a survey from inside the rooms is usually supplemented by selective opening-up of the cladding.

Can the energy efficiency of a facade be improved without replacing the cladding?

Partly. Reveals and junctions can be insulated, the sealing of window joints restored, and local defects remedied by removing individual panels. Increasing the insulation thickness over the whole area is not possible without dismantling the cladding.
