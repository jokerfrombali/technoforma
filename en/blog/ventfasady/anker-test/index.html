Anchor Pull-Out Testing Before Facade Installation

An anchor pull-out test is an on-site check of the load-bearing capacity of anchor fixings in the wall of a specific building. The anchor is installed in the substrate exactly as during installation and loaded axially with a portable tester up to a set load or to failure, and the maximum value is recorded.

The key criterion is that tests are carried out before the rainscreen facade working drawings are issued, at a sufficient number of points and in different wall zones, and the results are documented in a report. Without them, the substructure calculation relies on the anchor's published data, which may be unreliable for a real wall, especially a hollow or old one. The procedure is needed by general contractors, designers and technical supervisors in new construction and refurbishment.

The role of anchors in the overall system is in the Ventilated rainscreen facades section.

Why test

The anchor transfers the entire load from the cladding and wind to the wall. If it pulls out or breaks the substrate, the failure affects the bracket, the rail and the cladding elements above it.

  • The anchor's data sheet gives values for a reference substrate — concrete of a certain class or brick of a certain grade. The real wall may differ.
  • Masonry is non-uniform: voids, joints, low-strength mortar, aerated concrete of varying density.
  • In refurbishment the wall's properties are often completely unknown.
  • The substructure calculation requires the anchor's design load-bearing capacity — it is determined from test results with reduction factors.

Tests make it possible to choose the anchor type, embedment depth and bracket spacing on a sound basis. The economics look like this: an extra anchor on every bracket costs more than a day of testing, and an error in the other direction costs more than the whole facade.

Tests are especially important for heavy claddings — natural stone and large GFRC elements, where the vertical load on the anchor is high — and on tall buildings with high wind suction.

Test method

The test method is determined by regulatory documents and the anchor manufacturer's recommendations; the procedure is set out in a test programme.

  1. Choosing locations. Test points are distributed across facades, floors and substrate types: typical masonry, zones near openings, floor slabs, areas with visible defects.
  2. Installing anchors. The same anchors, drill bit, depth and drilling mode as in installation. For hollow materials — drilling without hammer action.
  3. Loading. A tester with a support that does not restrict failure of the substrate around the anchor applies axial force in steps or continuously.
  4. Recording the result. Maximum force and failure mode: anchor pull-out, cone failure of the substrate, steel failure, slippage.
  5. Processing. The mean value, scatter and design load-bearing capacity are determined for the series.

The number of tests depends on the facade area and the non-uniformity of the substrate; each substrate type requires a separate series.

Test report

The report is the document on which the designer bases decisions. It must contain:

  • the project, date, tester, equipment used and its calibration details;
  • anchor type and size, drilling diameter and depth;
  • substrate type and a layout of test points;
  • the result for each point: force, failure mode;
  • statistical processing and the design load-bearing capacity;
  • conclusions and recommendations on anchor use.

The report is attached to the substructure calculation and to the as-built documentation. At acceptance, technical supervision checks that exactly the tested anchors have been used on site.

In addition to pre-design tests, on large projects proof tests are carried out during installation: selected installed anchors are loaded to a proof load without failure. This checks installation quality — correct drilling, hole cleaning, setting depth — and its results are also documented in a report and attached to the concealed works records for the brackets.

Substrate types

Results depend heavily on the substrate.

SubstrateCharacteristicsTypical solutions
Cast-in-situ reinforced concreteHigh and consistent load-bearing capacityExpansion and chemical anchors
Solid brickGood load-bearing capacity; mortar quality mattersExpansion anchors, checking that they land in the brick body
Hollow brick, ceramic blocksLow and inconsistent valuesAnchors for hollow materials, chemical anchors with mesh sleeves, fixing to floor slabs

Old masonryNon-uniformity, weathered mortarExtended test programme, sometimes strengthening

A single building often has several substrates: a concrete frame with block infill, brick sections, floor slabs. Tests are carried out for each of them, and the design specifies which anchor is used in which material. The installer on the scaffolding needs this information in a clear form — on the bracket layout drawing with zones marked — otherwise an anchor meant for concrete will end up in a hollow block.

If values are low

Low results are not a death sentence for the facade but input data for the design.

  • Change the anchor type — chemical instead of expansion, or a special anchor for hollow materials.
  • Increase embedment depth if the wall thickness allows.
  • Add anchors per bracket or reduce bracket spacing.
  • Move load-bearing brackets to floor slabs and concrete elements, leaving only support brackets on the wall.
  • Use a slab-to-slab scheme — a substructure bearing on the floor slabs.
  • Use lighter cladding if other measures do not work.

After the solution is changed, the chosen anchor is retested. All solutions are agreed with the designer and reflected in the working drawings. The earlier the results are obtained, the cheaper the correction: at the design stage it is a recalculation, at the installation stage it is idle crews and re-ordering fasteners.

Which anchors are used in ventilated facades and how they differ

Four main groups of anchors are used to fix ventilated facade brackets: metal expansion anchors, facade plugs with a polymer sleeve, chemical (bonded) anchors and concrete screw anchors. The choice depends on the wall material, the load on the bracket and the requirements of the technical approval for the facade system. A pull-out test of the anchors shows which group actually performs in the wall in question.

Anchor typeHow it holds in the substrateWhere it is usedPoints to consider
Metal expansion anchor (wedge, sleeve)Friction and expansion against the hole wallsIn-situ concrete, high-grade solid brickSensitive to edge distances and cracks; does not work in hollow materials
Facade plug with a polyamide sleeveExpansion of the sleeve and interlock behind the webs of the voidsHollow brick, clay blocks, lightweight concreteDrilling without hammer action, expansion zone length matched to the specific unit
Chemical (injection) anchorBond between the resin and the hole walls; in voids, through a mesh sleeveWeak and non-uniform masonry, aerated concrete, zones near edgesHole cleaning, substrate temperature, curing time
Concrete screw anchorCuts a thread in the hole wallsIn-situ concreteExact drill bit diameter, single installation only

For load-bearing brackets, anchors are normally required to have corrosion resistance matching the service life of the facade: hot-dip galvanised or stainless steel. The anchor material is coordinated with the bracket material to rule out contact corrosion. For more on the load-bearing frame, see the article on the ventilated facade subframe.

Two types of fixing are often used on one facade: expansion anchors in concrete for the load-bearing brackets at the edges of the floor slabs, and facade plugs or chemical anchors for the restraint brackets in the block infill. Each combination of anchor and substrate is tested as a separate series, and the results are not mixed in the report.

What an anchor pull-out test programme includes

The test programme is a short document drawn up before going to site: it records what is tested, where and under what load. It is prepared by the facade designer or the testing contractor and agreed by the client or the technical supervisor. Without a programme the results are difficult to tie to the calculation, and disputed points cannot be justified.

  1. A list of the substrate types identified during the survey of the building walls and the number of test points for each type.
  2. A layout of the test points on the facade elevations, referenced to grid lines and levels.
  3. Brands and sizes of the anchors to be tested, embedment depth, drilling diameter and drilling mode.
  4. The required design load on the anchor from the designer — it sets the level to which the anchor is loaded.
  5. The loading method: to a proof load without failure, or to failure.
  6. Failure criteria: pull-out, slip beyond the permitted displacement, failure of the substrate.
  7. Requirements for the test device and the form of the report.

Tests to failure provide more information for design but leave damaged areas of wall. Non-destructive proof tests are used on anchors already installed during the works: the anchor is loaded to a specified value, held and checked for the absence of displacement.

On site the work requires access to the wall at different levels — scaffolding, a lifting platform or a cradle — as well as a power supply. A series on a medium-sized building is usually completed in one or two working days; for chemical anchors the curing time of the resin is added.

How is the failure load converted into the design value?

The design resistance of an anchor is always substantially lower than the force at which it came out of the wall. The values of the series are processed statistically and then divided by a safety factor that depends on the substrate material and the anchor type. For concrete the margin is usually smaller; for masonry and cellular concrete it is larger, because the scatter of results is higher there.

The outcome is governed not by the mean value but by the lower bound of the series and the scatter. Ten points with similar results give a higher design value than ten points with the same mean but two very low readings. For this reason unsuccessful points are not removed from the report: they are the ones that reveal the real weak spots in the masonry.

The resulting value is compared with the anchor force from the structural calculation of the facade, which takes into account the weight of the cladding, wind pressure and suction and, in corner zones, increased peak loads. If the margin is insufficient, the solution is changed and the series repeated.

What the failure mode indicates

  • Pull-out of the anchor without damage to the wall — the limit is bond or expansion; a greater embedment depth or a different anchor type helps.
  • A breakout cone in the substrate — the strength of the wall material is exhausted; a stronger anchor will not improve the result, and the fixing layout must be changed.
  • Rupture of the anchor shank — the substrate is stronger than the fixing; the result is limited by the steel, and the wall has reserve capacity.
  • Gradual slip under sustained load — an unreliable fixing; such a point is treated as a failure even if the anchor has not formally come out.

Typical mistakes in anchor testing

Most unreliable reports are explained not by the quality of the anchor but by the way the tests were organised. Mistakes more often overstate the result, and the subframe calculation is given a margin that the wall does not have.

  • Points are chosen only in convenient places — on the ground floor and in the concrete bands — while the block infill on the upper floors is left unchecked.
  • The supports of the device stand too close to the anchor and clamp the substrate, preventing a breakout cone from forming.
  • One anchor is tested but another is specified in the design — of a different length, diameter or manufacturer.
  • Holes are drilled with hammer action in hollow brick although hammer action is prohibited during installation, or the other way round.
  • A chemical anchor is loaded before the resin has fully cured, or is installed in a hole that has not been blown out.
  • The layer of render or old finishes is ignored: the embedment depth in the load-bearing material turns out to be less than nominal.
  • The device has no valid calibration, and the report contains no photographic record or layout of points.

Some of these mistakes come to light only at handover, when changing the solution is already costly. It is more dependable when a representative of the designer or the technical supervisor attends the tests and the points are marked on the facade jointly.

A separate mistake is testing the anchors too early or too late. If the infill masonry has not yet gained its strength, the results will be understated and will not reflect the working condition of the wall. If the series is carried out after the fixings and subframe have been purchased, any unsuccessful result turns into a dispute over who pays for replacing the materials.

How anchor installation is checked during facade works

A good report does not guarantee a reliable fixing if the anchors are installed on site differently from the way they were tested. The installation parameters from the report are therefore transferred to the method statement and checked during the installation of the ventilated facade.

  • Drill bit diameter and wear of its cutting edge, hole depth with an allowance for drilling dust.
  • Cleaning of the hole by blowing out and brushing — mandatory for bonded anchors.
  • Minimum distances to the edge of the wall, to an opening and to the adjacent anchor.
  • Tightening torque applied with a torque wrench in accordance with the manufacturer’s data.
  • Placement of the anchor in the body of the masonry unit rather than in a mortar joint, where the design specifies this.
  • Substrate and air temperature for chemical resins.

The results are recorded in concealed works certificates with photographs: once the insulation is in place the anchors are no longer visible. For heavy cladding, such as a ventilated facade in natural stone, random proof loading during installation is usually written into the contract in advance. Breaches at this stage are one of the main reasons why cladding elements fall after years in service.

FAQ

Why carry out anchor pull-out tests?

To determine the actual load-bearing capacity of anchors in the wall of a specific building and calculate the rainscreen substructure on that basis.

When are anchor tests carried out?

Before the working drawings are issued — at the survey stage. They are repeated if the anchor or substrate changes.

How many test points are needed?

It depends on the wall area and its non-uniformity; a separate series is carried out for each substrate type. The number is set by the test programme.

What should be done if an anchor in aerated concrete shows low pull-out values?

Change the anchor type, increase the number of anchors or move load-bearing brackets to the floor slabs, and repeat the tests.

Who can carry out anchor tests?

The anchor manufacturer, a laboratory or an installation company with calibrated equipment; the result is documented in a report.

Can test results from a neighbouring block be used?

Only as a guide. Even with an identical design, the masonry of different blocks differs in block batches, mortar and workmanship, so a separate series of tests is carried out for each building.

Do anchors in in-situ concrete need to be tested?

Yes, although the scatter of results there is usually smaller. The tests confirm the actual strength of the concrete, reveal zones with concreting defects and show how the anchor performs near the edge of a floor slab, where load-bearing brackets are often fixed.

How do proof tests differ from tests to failure?

In a test to failure the anchor is loaded until it fails, giving the ultimate value for the calculation. In a proof test the anchor is loaded to a specified value and held: if there is no displacement, the fixing is considered fit for use and remains in service.
