Rainscreen Substructures: Aluminium, Galvanised and Stainless Steel

A rainscreen substructure is a metal framework fixed to the load-bearing wall that holds the cladding away from the insulation, forming the ventilated gap. It consists of brackets, rails, anchors and cladding fixings and is made of aluminium alloys, coated galvanised steel or stainless steel.

The main selection criterion is calculation: the substructure must carry the weight of the cladding and the wind load of the particular building with a margin, must not corrode in service, and must have confirmed compliance with the requirements for facade systems. The choice depends on the cladding, building height, climate and substrate. All articles on the topic are in the section Ventilated rainscreen facades.

Types of substructure

Rainscreen substructures are classified by several features.

By rail layout:

  • vertical — brackets and vertical rails; the most common layout for porcelain stoneware and panels;
  • horizontal — rails run horizontally; used for certain cladding types;
  • cross (slab-to-slab) — vertical and horizontal profiles; lets the system bear on the floor slabs when the wall between them is weak (for example, aerated concrete infill in a frame building).

By cladding fixing: visible (clips, rivets) and concealed (agraffes, undercut anchors, cast-in fixings in panels, adhesive systems covered by a technical certificate).

By material: aluminium, galvanised steel with polymer coating, stainless steel, and combinations.

The market offers systems from various manufacturers — among the well-known brands people often search for are Grand Line, Metall Profil, Hilti and others. Each system has its own range of brackets, profiles and fixings and its own scope of application confirmed by documents. Elements of different systems must not be mixed: calculations and tests are carried out for a specific kit. Angles, brackets and fixings are selected only from the range of the chosen system.

A porcelain stoneware ventilated facade without a substructure does not exist: cladding glued directly to the wall or insulation is a different construction, with no ventilated gap and with its own limitations.

Materials

The substructure material determines weight, corrosion resistance and cost.

MaterialProsLimitations
Aluminium alloysLow weight, resistance to atmospheric corrosion, precise profile geometryGreater thermal movement, lower melting point — important for fire safety; thermal expansion must be accommodated
Galvanised steel with polymer coatingHigh strength, lower cost, better behaviour when heatedCorrosion where the coating is damaged, at cuts and holes; heavier
Stainless steelHigh strength and corrosion resistance, fire resistanceHighest cost, harder to work

Combined solutions are common: stainless steel brackets and fixings with aluminium or galvanised rails. Stainless steel washers, rivets and anchors reduce the risk of corrosion at the most heavily loaded points.

Contact between dissimilar metals without isolating pads must be avoided — it causes galvanic corrosion. Exact profile properties and permissible loads are given in the system manufacturer's documentation.

Load calculation

A substructure is selected by calculation, not by analogy with the building next door. The calculation is performed by the designer based on data about the building and the system.

What is taken into account:

  • self-weight of the cladding — porcelain stoneware, GFRC and natural stone give different mass per square metre;
  • wind load — depends on the wind zone, building height and terrain type; it is higher at corners and parapets, so bracket spacing is reduced there;
  • ice and seismic loads — in the relevant zones;
  • thermal movement — especially for aluminium, accommodated by sliding fixings and breaks in the rails;
  • substrate capacity — anchors are selected for the wall material and verified by on-site pull-out tests.

The result of the calculation is the spacing of brackets and rails, the type and number of anchors, and profile sections for each facade zone. Design loads are defined by the current Russian code of practice on loads and actions.

Certification

Facade systems are structures on which people's safety depends, so the use of a substructure must be supported by documents. A system usually has:

  • a technical certificate or equivalent document confirming the system's suitability for use in construction, with its scope and limitations;
  • fire test results for the complete facade system with specific cladding and insulation, with the fire hazard class of the construction;
  • a technical solutions manual — details, schemes, component range;
  • certificates and data sheets for profiles, fixings and anchors.

Importantly, the documents cover the system as a whole: replacing the cladding, insulation or substructure elements with ones not covered by the documents can take the facade outside its confirmed scope of application. At the selection stage it is worth checking that the technical certificate is valid and covers the required cladding type, building height and fire hazard class of the building.

Choosing by cladding

Matching the substructure to the cladding is the main task of rainscreen facade design.

CladdingTypical substructureFeatures
Porcelain stonewareVertical, aluminium or galvanised steel, clips or concealed fixingLow weight, standard solutions
Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC)Reinforced, fixed via the panels' cast-in fixingsLarge and three-dimensional panels, custom details for decor
Natural stoneReinforced, often steel or with stainless steel clipsHeavy, reduced bracket spacing
Fibre cement, metal cassettesLight vertical or horizontalMinimal load

For GFRC panels that combine cladding and decor (cornices, pilasters, window surrounds), the substructure is designed together with the division of the facade into products: the positions of the cast-in fixings in the panels and of the brackets must coincide. What elements make up a ventilated facade subframe and what is each responsible for?

A ventilated facade subframe works as a chain: the anchor transfers the load to the wall, the bracket sets the stand-off, the rail forms the plane, and the fixing element holds the cladding. A weak link anywhere determines the load-bearing capacity of the whole structure, so the elements are selected as a set, not individually.

ElementFunctionWhat to look at
Load-bearing bracketTakes the weight of the cladding and the wind loadRigid connection to the rail, usually one per profile within a storey
Restraint bracketTakes the wind load onlySliding connection that does not hinder lengthening of the profile
Thermal break padReduces heat transfer through the bracket and separates metal from wallMaterial and thickness as per the system documentation
Bracket extensionTakes up wall unevenness and increases the stand-offAdjustment range, number of rivets in the connection
RailForms the plane and carries the cladding fixingsSection, length between thermal breaks
Cladding fixingsClips, hangers, sliders, connections for embedded partsSuitability for the cladding material and thickness

The division of brackets into load-bearing and restraint types is fundamental. If all connections on a rail are fixed rigidly, the profile cannot lengthen when heated and begins to bend, pushing the cladding out. If all are made sliding, there is nothing to carry the weight of the cladding.

Every bracket passes through the insulation and acts as a thermal bridge. The more brackets per square metre and the heavier their section, the thicker the insulation has to be; this is discussed in more detail in the article thermal bridges in a ventilated facade.

Anchors and substrate: how the subframe is fixed to different walls

The load-bearing capacity of a subframe is limited not by the metal of the profiles but by how the anchor holds in the wall. The same bracket carries a different load on in-situ concrete and on aerated concrete, so the anchor type is chosen according to the substrate material and confirmed by tests on site.

SubstrateTypical solutionPoints to note
In-situ reinforced concreteMetal expansion or chemical anchorsThe most reliable substrate; edge distance and reinforcement are taken into account
Solid brickExpansion anchors and facade plugsDrilled into the body of the brick, not into the joint
Hollow brick and blocksExtended facade plugs, chemical anchors with a mesh sleeveDrilling without hammer action so as not to break the webs
Aerated and other cellular concretesSpecial anchors for cellular concrete, or support on the floor slabsLow load-bearing capacity; a slab-to-slab arrangement is often used

Pull-out tests are carried out before mass installation begins, on several areas of the facade including zones with the poorest masonry. On the basis of the results the designer confirms or corrects the bracket spacing and anchor type. The procedure is described in the article anchor pull-out testing.

In framed buildings with lightweight block infill, the load is usually transferred to the slab edges and columns, and only restraint points are placed in the infill. This decision is taken at the design stage because it changes the rail section and the amount of metal.

Aluminium, galvanised or stainless steel: how does the environment affect the choice of material?

The subframe material is chosen with regard to the atmosphere in which the building will stand for decades. The frame is hidden behind the cladding and difficult to inspect without dismantling, so a wrong choice shows up late and is expensive to put right.

  • Ordinary urban environment. All three materials are applicable; the decision is driven by cladding mass, fire requirements and budget.
  • Coastal areas. Airborne salts accelerate corrosion of galvanised steel at cuts and holes. Stainless steel or aluminium alloys with stainless fixings are chosen more often.
  • Industrial zones and areas beside main roads. Aggressive emissions and de-icing agents stress the coating; the requirements for its thickness and for the steel grade are higher.
  • Plinth and lowest tier. A splash and snow zone where, even on an ordinary project, fixings and brackets are best specified in stainless steel.

With galvanised steel the vulnerable points arise during installation: cutting with an angle grinder burns off the zinc around the edge, and drilling in situ leaves an unprotected end. Cutting is therefore done with shears or a cold-cut saw, and reworking of connections is kept to a minimum. For aluminium the main risk is contact with steel and with the alkaline environment of fresh mortar.

How the climate of a region affects the facade structure as a whole is considered in the article choosing facade materials for the climate.

Typical mistakes in subframe installation

Frame defects are dangerous because they are not visible once the cladding has been hung. Most of them are due to departures from the designed spacing and simplification of connections on site.

  • Bracket spacing increased. Often without recalculation, by analogy with the ordinary zone, although the load is higher at corners and the parapet.
  • Anchor placed in a masonry joint or near a slab edge. The load-bearing capacity of such a fixing is markedly below the calculated value.
  • No thermal break pads. Heat loss increases, and an aluminium bracket is in direct contact with concrete.
  • All connections rigid. Rails several storeys long without breaks and sliding connections deform in summer.
  • Rivets and screws of the wrong steel. Ordinary galvanised fixings in an aluminium or stainless frame become a seat of corrosion.
  • Extensions at the limit of adjustment. A connection on one rivet instead of two works as a hinge.
  • Frame set out without a geodetic survey. An error in the plane is later corrected with packing under the cladding.

The frame is accepted before it is covered by the cladding: the spacing, type and tightening of the anchors, the number of fixings in the connections, plumb and gaps between rails are checked. The result is recorded in a concealed works certificate and an as-built drawing. The place of this check in the overall sequence of works is shown in the article stages of ventilated facade installation.

What does the cost of a subframe depend on and where must savings not be made?

The cost of a subframe is determined by the amount of metal per square metre, which depends on the stand-off, the cladding mass and the wind load. Comparing offers by price per metre without identical input data is meaningless: the same figure may conceal a different bracket spacing and a different profile thickness.

  • Stand-off. The thicker the insulation and the greater the wall unevenness, the longer and heavier the brackets.
  • Cladding. Stone and GFRC panels need more metal than porcelain stoneware or cassettes.
  • Material. Stainless steel is more expensive than aluminium and galvanised steel, but in some conditions there is no alternative.
  • Substrate. Weak walls require a slab-to-slab arrangement with reinforced rails.
  • Building geometry. Many corners, openings and projections mean more non-standard connections.

Optimisation is acceptable through calculation: zoning of the spacing, selection of sections, reduction of the stand-off by evening out the layout. It is not acceptable to save by changing the steel grade, profile thickness, or number of anchors and fixings relative to the design. What to state in the enquiry so that offers are comparable

For quotations from different contractors to be comparable, the enquiry sets the same conditions for all bidders and asks them to disclose the make-up of the frame, not just the total price.

  • type and mass of the cladding, and its fixing method;
  • insulation thickness and required stand-off;
  • wall material by area, number of storeys, location;
  • material of the frame and fixings, profile thickness;
  • bracket spacing by zone and anchor consumption per square metre;
  • name of the facade system and the current documents for it;
  • whether design, anchor tests, scaffolding and trim elements are included.

An offer in which the metal consumption and spacing are not disclosed cannot be assessed on price: the difference is almost always explained by precisely these parameters.

FAQ

What is a rainscreen substructure?

A metal framework of brackets, rails, anchors and fixings that holds the rainscreen cladding away from the insulation.

What substructure is needed for porcelain stoneware?

Usually a vertical aluminium or galvanised steel system with clips or concealed fixing; the spacing is determined by calculation.

Can a porcelain stoneware ventilated facade be built without a substructure?

No. Without a substructure there is no ventilated gap — it would be a different type of facade with its own limitations.

Which is better: an aluminium or a steel substructure?

Aluminium is lighter and does not rust; steel is stronger and behaves better when heated. The choice is made by calculation, cladding and fire safety requirements.

Can elements of different systems be combined?

No, the documents and tests apply to a specific system kit.

How many brackets are needed per square metre of facade?

There is no single figure: the quantity is determined by calculation and depends on the cladding mass, wind load, building height and anchor load-bearing capacity. In corner zones and at the parapet more brackets are installed than in ordinary areas, and this is reflected in the frame layout.

Can a subframe be fixed to aerated concrete?

Yes, but only with anchors intended for cellular concrete and after pull-out tests. With heavy cladding the load is usually transferred to the floor slabs using a slab-to-slab arrangement, and only restraint points are placed in the aerated concrete.

What is the service life of a ventilated facade subframe?

It depends on the frame material, the service environment and the quality of installation. An indicative service life is stated in the documents for the specific system; in practice it is shortened by damage to the zinc coating, contact between dissimilar metals and unsuitable fixings.
