Ventilated Rainscreen Facade: Structure and How It Works

A ventilated rainscreen facade is an external cladding system in which the finishing material is fixed to the wall via a metal substructure, set away from the insulation. A ventilated air gap remains between the insulation and the cladding, through which moisture escapes from the wall and insulation. Cladding options include porcelain stoneware, glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC), natural stone, fibre cement and metal panels.

The main criterion for choosing a system is that the substructure, insulation and cladding are compatible with each other and meet the fire safety and load requirements for the particular building. Rainscreen facades are used on multi-storey residential buildings, office and public buildings, in refurbishment, and on private houses. All articles on the topic are in the section Ventilated rainscreen facades.

Layers of a rainscreen facade

A typical ventilated rainscreen facade consists of several layers, working outward from the wall:

LayerMade ofFunction
Load-bearing substrateCast-in-situ concrete, brick, blocksCarries the load from the system
BracketsAluminium alloys, galvanised or stainless steelAnchored to the wall, set the projection
Thermal insulationNon-combustible mineral wool boardsReduces heat loss
Wind and moisture barrier (as designed)Membranes or faced insulationProtects the insulation from fibre wash-out and moisture
RailsVertical and/or horizontal profilesCarry the cladding
Ventilated gapAirRemoves moisture, evens out temperature
CladdingPorcelain stoneware, GFRC, stone, panelsProtects the system, shapes the appearance

Besides the main layers, the system includes anchors, thermal isolation pads under the brackets, cladding fixings (clips, rivets, concealed fixings), fire barriers, opening surrounds, flashings and junctions with the roof and plinth. A ventilated rainscreen facade is fixed to the load-bearing wall: it carries the entire load from the cladding and wind, so anchors are selected for the substrate material and verified by pull-out tests.

Why the air gap is needed

The ventilated gap lies between the insulation (or wind barrier membrane) and the cladding. This is the working principle that distinguishes a rainscreen facade from 'wet' render systems.

Why the air gap is needed:

  • Moisture removal. Water vapour passing from inside the building through the wall and insulation is carried away by the airflow and does not condense in the insulation. Dry insulation keeps its thermal performance.
  • Weather protection. The cladding takes rain and snow; water that gets behind the cladding through joints runs down the gap and is not absorbed by the insulation.
  • Temperature balancing. In summer the cladding heats up and the gap reduces heat transfer to the wall; in winter thermal stresses in the wall are reduced.
  • Independent cladding. The cladding is not rigidly connected to the wall and does not transfer its thermal movement to it.

The effect comes from natural stack ventilation: air enters at the bottom, at the plinth, warms up and exits at the top, at the parapet. So the gap must have open inlet and outlet openings protected by grilles, and its width must match the system design and the system developer's recommendations. A gap that is too narrow impairs ventilation, and one blocked by sills and barriers without calculation disrupts it.

Cladding

The cladding of a rainscreen facade determines the building's appearance, the system's weight and the requirements for the substructure.

  • Porcelain stoneware. The most common cladding: a wide choice of formats and textures, moderate weight, flat tiles. Fixed with visible clips or concealed fixings.
  • GFRC. Large-format flat and three-dimensional panels, decor, relief. Allows cladding and architectural decor to be combined in one system. According to TECHNOFORMA, GFRC is rated non-combustible (Russian class NG).
  • Natural stone. Granite, marble, limestone, travertine slabs. Heavy, needs a reinforced substructure and calculation.
  • Fibre cement panels — light flat boards with a painted or textured surface.
  • Metal cassettes and composite panels — light, with precise geometry; for composite panels the fire performance of the core is critical.
  • Terracotta and ceramic panels, including extruded ones such as FRONTEK.

One facade often combines several claddings — for example, GFRC for sculpting and decor and porcelain stoneware for plain areas.

Where it is used

Ventilated rainscreen facades are used:

  • in new residential construction — multi-storey buildings that need durable insulated cladding;
  • on office and retail centres — large panels, complex geometry, integrated signage and lighting;
  • on public buildings — schools, universities, hospitals, sports facilities;
  • in refurbishment — insulating and renewing old buildings without wet processes on the wall;
  • on private houses — including aerated concrete, timber and timber-frame houses, where a rainscreen lets the wall 'breathe'.

Limitations relate to the wall's load-bearing capacity (anchors must hold the load), building height (fire safety requirements become stricter for tall buildings) and climate (wind zones, seismicity). Requirements for the design, testing and use of facade systems are set by the current Russian codes of practice on fire safety and loads and by the technical certificates for specific systems.

How a ventilated facade is built up in terms of loads

The build-up of a ventilated facade serves one purpose: to transfer the weight of the cladding and the wind forces to the wall without preventing the components from lengthening and shortening freely as the temperature changes. The subframe is therefore not a rigid frame but a set of components with fixed and sliding connections.

The vertical load from the weight of the cladding and the profiles is taken by load-bearing brackets; these are usually placed at the floor slabs, where the substrate is strongest. Restraint brackets hold the rail against wind and allow it to move along its axis. Wind acts in both directions: it presses on the cladding on the windward side and pulls it away on the leeward side, and suction near the corners and the parapet is noticeably higher than in the middle of the wall.

  • The rails are divided over the height into lengths, as a rule within one storey, with an expansion gap left between them.
  • The cladding is fixed so that each panel has fixed points and free sliding points.
  • The movement joints of the building are repeated in the subframe and in the cladding: neither a profile nor a panel may bridge the joint.
  • In seismic regions the connections are additionally checked for horizontal actions.

The bracket spacing and the profile sections are determined by the calculation of the facade system for the specific building: its height, wind zone, and the weight and format of the cladding.

Visible and concealed fixing of cladding: what is the difference

The fixing method determines the appearance of the facade, the design of the rails and the cost of the system. With visible fixing the fixing elements remain on the face; with concealed fixing they are hidden behind the panel or in its edge.

MethodHow it is doneFor which claddingsFeatures
Visible, on clipsThe lugs of the clip grip the edges of the tilePorcelain stoneware, fibre cementSimple installation and replacement of a tile; the fixing is noticeable at close range and is painted to match the cladding
Visible, on rivets or screwsThe panel is fixed to the profile through holesFibre cement and composite panelsFixed and sliding points are needed, otherwise the panel warps
Concealed mechanicalAnchors in the back of the panel or kerfs in the edges, hung on horizontal profilesStone, large-format porcelain stoneware, ceramicsRequires a thicker panel and precise factory machining
Concealed, on cast-in fixingsEmbedded parts are cast into the body of the element during productionGFRC panels and decorative elementsThe position of the embedded parts is set in the design before the moulds are made

For thin panels, concealed fixing is carried out only using solutions confirmed by tests of the system. Adhesive fixing without mechanical back-up is, as a rule, not used on high-rise buildings. In GFRC elements the embedded parts are positioned with regard to the mass of the element and its projection from the wall: a cornice and a flat panel load the subframe differently.

What should the air gap be, and what stops it working?

The gap works only when air enters freely at the bottom, travels the full height and leaves at the top. Its width is set by the design and by the technical approval of the system; in practice it is usually a few centimetres — most often from 40 to 100 mm. The minimum value is checked at the narrowest point, allowing for unevenness of the wall and the actual thickness of the insulation.

Typical reasons why ventilation does not work:

  • the insulation is laid loosely or has come away from the wall and blocked the cavity;
  • the inlet openings at the plinth are closed by paving, a sill flashing or a solid profile;
  • the air outlet under the parapet is sealed by a coping with no vents;
  • the membrane is poorly fixed and is pressed against the cladding by the wind;
  • reveals, flashings and cavity barriers are made solid where the design requires air flow.

A separate subject is fire barriers in the cavity. They are installed over the height of the facade and around openings to limit the spread of flame; the barrier must perform this function without cutting off ventilation in normal conditions. Inlet and outlet openings are closed with perforated profiles or meshes against birds and rodents.

Insulation in a ventilated facade: thickness, layers, fixing

The thickness of the thermal insulation is determined by a thermal calculation for the local climate and the use of the building, not by the habits of the installers. For central Russia it is usually within 100–200 mm, greater in northern regions and less in southern ones.

The slabs are placed tightly against each other and against the wall, with staggered joints; in a two-layer arrangement the outer layer covers the joints of the inner one and has a higher density. Each slab is secured with disc fixings, the number of which is specified in the design. Around the brackets the insulation is cut to fit without voids — this is where gaps through which heat escapes are most often left. The subject is covered in detail in the article on insulating a ventilated facade.

The bracket itself is a thermal bridge: metal passes through the entire insulation layer. Losses are reduced by thermal break pads, by using stainless steel with its lower thermal conductivity and by a sensible bracket spacing. In the thermal calculation this is taken into account by the thermal uniformity factor.

Junctions on which the durability of a ventilated facade depends

The plain wall area rarely causes problems — failures occur at the junctions. Water gets behind the cladding through them, and mistakes in these areas become visible after just one or two winters.

What mistakes are made most often when building a ventilated facade?

Most defects in ventilated facades are connected not with the materials but with departures from the design and substitutions during procurement. The most frequent ones recur from project to project.

  • Replacing system components with ‘equivalents’ without recalculation: a different alloy, a different profile thickness, a different anchor. The technical approval covers only the set of components described in it.
  • Installation without an as-built survey of the walls: the bracket projection does not cover the actual deviations, and the gap disappears in places.
  • Rigid fixing of rails and panels without expansion gaps — the cladding bows or cracks in the very first hot season.
  • Insulation left exposed for weeks: it gets wet and, under wind, loses fibres from its surface.
  • A combination of dissimilar metals without isolating pads, which causes corrosion to develop at the points of contact.

What a ventilated facade design requires and the sequence of works

The design of a ventilated system begins with the initial data on the building. The more complete the data, the fewer changes will have to be made on site.

  • Architectural drawings of the facades, plans and sections, information on the wall material floor by floor.
  • A geodetic survey of the actual deviations of the walls from the vertical.
  • The selected cladding, its format, weight and fixing method.
  • The construction region, the height of the building, the functional fire hazard class.
  • The results of anchor pull-out tests.

On this basis the facade design is carried out: the cladding layout, the subframe calculation, details and schedules. Then follow the setting-out of the axes, installation of brackets, insulation, and fitting of rails, cladding and trims; after each stage the concealed works are accepted under a certificate. The TECHNOFORMA Group runs these works as a single chain: a design team, in-house production of GFRC and stone cladding, and ventilated facade installation by its own crews.

This arrangement reduces the number of interfaces between contractors. The designers coordinate the panel layout with production capabilities from the outset, and the installers receive elements with cast-in fixings for the specific subframe. Comments from site return to the design directly, without correspondence between several organisations.

FAQ

What is a ventilated rainscreen facade?

A system in which the cladding is fixed to the wall via a metal substructure, with a ventilated air gap between the insulation and the cladding.

Between which layers is the ventilated gap?

Between the insulation (or wind barrier membrane) and the cladding.

What is a ventilated rainscreen facade fixed to?

The load-bearing wall: the substructure brackets are anchored to it, the rails are fixed to the brackets, and the cladding to the rails.

What types of ventilated facades are there?

They are distinguished by cladding (porcelain stoneware, GFRC, stone, fibre cement, metal, composite, ceramics) and by substructure (aluminium, galvanised or stainless steel).

Can a rainscreen facade be clad in porcelain stoneware?

Yes, this is the most common cladding option for a ventilated rainscreen facade.

Which regulations govern rainscreen facades?

The current Russian codes of practice on fire safety and loads, as well as the technical certificates for specific facade systems.

How long does a ventilated facade last?

The service life depends on the materials of the subframe and the cladding and on how aggressive the environment is; the design values are stated in the technical approval for the system. With a corrosion-resistant subframe and durable cladding it is a matter of decades, provided the facade is inspected regularly and defects are remedied in good time.

Can a ventilated facade be installed in winter?

Yes, installation is a dry process and is not tied to above-zero temperatures. The restrictions relate to strong wind, icing of the scaffolding, the installation conditions for chemical anchors and the fact that insulation must not be left exposed to precipitation.

Is a wind barrier membrane always necessary?

No. The need for a membrane is determined by the design and the technical approval of the system: with a dense outer insulation layer it may be omitted. Where a membrane is specified, a material with confirmed fire performance is used.
