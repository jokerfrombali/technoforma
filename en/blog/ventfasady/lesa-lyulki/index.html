Scaffolding or Suspended Platforms: How High-Rise Facades Are Installed

Facade scaffolding is a modular structure of standards, ledgers and decks that stands on the ground along the wall and gives installers a continuous working platform on every storey. The alternative is suspended facade cradles (gondolas): hanging platforms raised and lowered on wire ropes by hoists fixed to outriggers on the roof. There is also a third class: mast climbing work platforms and self-propelled aerial work platforms.

The main selection criterion is not height alone but the combination of height, the volume of work on one section of the wall and the weight of the cladding elements. Scaffolding wins when several crews work on the wall at once and material has to be stored on the working level; cradles win when the work front is narrow and vertical and the building is tall.

This article is for general contractors, site managers and technical supervisors planning the installation of a rainscreen facade on a multi-storey building. For background on the systems themselves, see Ventilated rainscreen facades.

Facade access options

In practice a high-rise facade is installed using one of four methods, and on large projects they are combined section by section.

MethodHow it worksStrengthsLimitations
Frame and wedge-lock scaffoldingStands on the ground or on brackets, tied back to the wall with anchorsContinuous work front, space for material, simultaneous work on many levelsSlow to erect and dismantle, anchors in the wall, height limits without structural calculation
Suspended facade cradlesPlatform on wire ropes, hoists and outriggers on the roofQuick start, no footprint on the ground, suitable for great heightsNarrow work section, wind-dependent, little room for material
Mast climbing work platformsPlatform travels along a mast tied to the buildingWide platform, high load capacity, room for several workersNeeds level ground at the base and ties to the building frame
Truck-mounted lifts and self-propelled platformsMobile machinery with a basketSpot repairs, lower floors, plinth, entrance areasLimited height and reach, vehicle access required

The choice is recorded in the method statement for the works (known in Russia as PPR): it specifies the type of access equipment, tie-in schemes, storage zones and danger zones.

Working at height

Installing a ventilated rainscreen facade at height is a sequence of operations: setting out and fixing brackets, insulation and wind barrier, substructure rails, then the cladding. Each operation puts different demands on the access equipment.

  • Brackets and anchors involve drilling and a lot of horizontal movement. This is easier from scaffolding or the wide platform of a mast climber.
  • Insulation is light but bulky; on a cradle it has to be delivered in batches.
  • Cladding is where element weight decides. Porcelain stoneware and light cassettes are installed from cradles. Large glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) panels and natural stone slabs are heavier; they are often lifted by crane or hoist, while installers stand on scaffolding or a platform and guide the element into its design position.

Decorative elements are a separate topic: cornices, string courses, window surrounds, columns. They are fixed to embedded plates and brackets using project-specific details, and the installer needs stable footing and both hands free. That is why, even when the main front is done from cradles, areas of complex ornament are often scaffolded.

Safety

In Russia, work at height is governed by occupational safety rules for working at height approved by the Ministry of Labour, plus the method statement for the specific site and the equipment certificates. Key points checked by technical supervision:

  • scaffolding is erected according to its certificate or a structural calculation, tied to the wall as specified, with guardrails and toe boards; after erection it is accepted by a signed record and inspected regularly;
  • cradles and hoists undergo technical inspection and operate with fall arrest devices and a safety rope separate from the working rope; workers are clipped to an independent anchor line;
  • when wind rises above the permissible value for the equipment, work in the cradle stops; this is stated in the certificate and the method statement;
  • a danger zone for falling objects is marked at ground level, and protective canopies are installed over entrances;
  • only trained workers with the appropriate safety qualification and a work permit are allowed to work at height.

For rainscreen facades with heavy cladding, the slinging and lifting scheme is checked separately: a panel or slab must not swing against the wall, and an installer should not receive it while standing on the edge of a narrow platform.

Cost

There is no universal price per square metre for access equipment: the cost is made up of rental, delivery, erection and dismantling, and for cradles also roof fixings and repositioning between sections.

  • Scaffolding: costs grow in proportion to facade area and rental period; a significant share goes on erection and dismantling.
  • Cradles: lower one-off set-up costs, but every move along the perimeter has to be planned; productivity is lower when the cladding is heavy.
  • Mast climbers: more expensive to rent, but on large areas they can pay off through faster progress.

The right way to compare options is by the total cost of the whole work cycle, not by the rental rate: slow installation from cheap access equipment extends the construction schedule. How to choose

A short checklist to help decide at the method statement stage:

  1. Assess height and geometry: a continuous flat wall argues for cradles or mast climbers; lots of projections, bay windows and balconies argue for scaffolding.
  2. Look at the weight and format of the cladding: light porcelain stoneware suits a cradle; large GFRC panels and stone call for scaffolding or platforms with a crane.
  3. Check the ground: is there room for scaffold bases and a mast foundation, and will landscaping, neighbouring buildings or driveways get in the way?
  4. Consider the schedule: if several trades work on the wall in parallel, scaffolding gives every crew a work front at once.
  5. Consider climate and season: windy sites reduce the number of working days in a cradle.

A combination is often best: scaffolding on the lower floors and in areas of complex ornament, cradles or mast climbers on typical floors.

How are scaffolding and cradles attached to the building and what does this mean for the facade?

Any means of access transfers load to the building, and the points of this transfer lie right in the body of the future facade. How they are resolved determines how many unclosed areas remain after dismantling and whether patches appear in the finished wall.

  • Scaffolding. It is tied to the wall with anchors at the spacing given in its data sheet — usually every few metres horizontally and vertically in a staggered pattern. The ties pass through the insulation and the cladding plane, so the layout identifies panels that are installed after the scaffolding has been struck, or fixing into window openings with reveal ties is planned in advance.
  • Cradles. Outriggers with counterweights stand on the roof or bear on the parapet. The load-bearing capacity of the roof and parapet must be checked, the roof waterproofing under the supports protected, and the parapet cladding not started until the outriggers have been removed.
  • Mast climbers. The mast stands on a prepared base and is tied to the frame every few metres in height. A vertical strip without cladding remains along the mast and is closed from top to bottom during dismantling.

After dismantling, the holes from the scaffold anchors are made good, the insulation is reinstated and only then is the panel installed. This operation is often done in a hurry, from a boom lift or a cradle, so it is worth including it in the programme and in the list of concealed works. The sequence of the main operations is described in the article on the stages of ventilated facade installation.

When choosing the spacing and type of scaffold anchors, the substrate is taken into account in the same way as for facade brackets: fixing into the reinforced concrete frame is more reliable than into block infill. If the wall does not allow the required number of points, the scaffolding is restrained through window openings or supported on the floor slabs, and such a solution is documented by calculation rather than left to the discretion of the team.

Getting materials up to height: crane, hoist, winch

A means of access gives the installer a workplace but does not solve the question of how the brackets, insulation and cladding will get there. On a high-rise project the vertical transport of materials often limits the rate more than the number of installers does.

Method of supplyWhat it suppliesParticular features
Tower cranePallets of cladding, large panels, bundles of railsBusy with general construction work; crane time for the facade is booked in the programme
Goods and passenger hoistInsulation, fixings, small-format panels, peopleMaterial is distributed to the storeys from inside and passed onto the scaffolding through openings
Electric hoist or winch on the roofIndividual panels and decorative elementsA calculated outrigger and guy lines are needed so that the load does not swing
Mobile crane, lorry loaderLower-storey elements, columns, portalsRequires access and a hardstanding for the outriggers
Mast climber platformMaterial together with the teamLifting capacity is limited by the data sheet, loading must be even

The most frequent planning mistake is to count on a tower crane that has already been dismantled by the time cladding begins. If the facade starts after the crane has gone, the scheme for supplying heavy elements is thought through separately: winches on the roof, a mobile crane, or installation in pre-assembled panels before the crane is removed.

It is useful to calculate the output of a team together with logistics. If the cradle spends half the shift travelling down for material, the actual rate will be far below the calculated one; on high-rise sections intermediate stores are therefore set up on the storeys and panels are passed to the installers through window openings.

Scaffolding or cradles: which means of access to choose for different heights and cladding?

There is no hard and fast rule, but typical combinations of building height, cladding type and means of access have become established in practice. They help with orientation before the method statement is prepared; the final decision is confirmed by calculation and the equipment data sheets.

BuildingCladdingWhat is usually used
Up to 5–6 storeysAny, including stone and decorationFrame scaffolding to full height, a boom lift for spot work
Medium rise, complex facade reliefGFRC, stone, much decorationWedge-lock or tube-and-fitting scaffolding, supply by crane
High-rise, flat wallsPorcelain stoneware, cassettes, fibre cementCradles or mast climbers
High-rise, heavy claddingLarge GFRC panels, stone slabsMast climbers with crane supply; scaffolding in decorated zones and on the lower tiers
Podium, entrance groupsStone, portals, columnsScaffolding and a mobile crane regardless of the height of the main volume

The weight of the element is the parameter checked first. A thin-walled panel of glass fibre reinforced concrete is noticeably lighter than a stone slab of the same area, and this widens the choice of means of access; design values are given in the article on how much GFRC weighs. For stone cladding at height the requirements for supplying and receiving slabs are stricter — see the article on the natural stone ventilated facade.

Rope access: where it is appropriate and where it is not

Rope access work is appropriate where the volume is small and erecting scaffolding or a cradle is not worthwhile. As the main method of installing a ventilated facade, rope access is unsuitable: the worker has no stable support, the weight of tools and material is limited, and accurate alignment of the subframe from a rope is difficult.

  • Appropriate: inspection and photographic recording, sealing of individual joints, washing, replacement of a single small-format panel, closing of scaffold tie positions.
  • Not appropriate: large volumes of drilling for anchors, installation of rails, hanging of heavy panels and decoration.

During operation of the building, rope access technicians and cradles remain the main way of reaching the wall. Anchor points on the roof are therefore included in the design from the outset; how the maintenance schedule is drawn up is described in the article on ventilated facade maintenance.

Constrained conditions: street, pavement and neighbouring buildings

In dense urban development the choice of means of access is often determined not by the building but by what surrounds it. A pavement, a carriageway, a neighbouring building a few metres away or a shop entrance in use limit the space for supports and the zone where objects might fall.

  • a protective gallery with decking and lighting is erected over the pavement, and the scaffolding is covered with netting;
  • if the scaffolding cannot bear on the ground, cantilever arrangements supported on the floor slabs or suspended platforms are used;
  • occupation of public land is agreed in advance, and the time needed to obtain permits is included in the programme;
  • unloading of materials is planned for the hours of least traffic, and the storage area is moved into the courtyard.

In such conditions products with a high degree of factory readiness are more advantageous: fewer operations at the wall and less time during which the pavement is occupied.

Wind is assessed separately. Between closely spaced tall buildings the air flow accelerates, and the number of days on which work in a cradle is prohibited by its data sheet may be greater than on an open site. In such places mast climbers and scaffolding give a more predictable programme.

Typical mistakes in organising access to the facade

Most problems with means of access are built in not on site but at the planning stage — when the method of access is chosen by the hire rate without reference to the cladding layout and the programme.

  • The distance from the scaffolding to the wall is chosen without regard to the thickness of the facade build-up: once the insulation and rails are in place the installer has nowhere to work or, on the contrary, the gap is too large and requires additional guarding.
  • The scaffold lifts are not coordinated with the horizontal joints and string courses: standards and ledgers fall where cornices are to be installed.
  • Scaffold anchors are set in lightweight block infill without testing.
  • Cradles are chosen for heavy panels which, by weight and size, cannot be received safely from a suspended platform.
  • No storage space is provided: pallets of cladding stand on the scaffold lifts in excess of the permissible deck load.
  • Dismantling of the scaffolding is scheduled before the cladding defects have been remedied, and they have to be corrected from a boom lift.
  • Adjacent works are not taken into account: landscaping and utilities begin while the supports still stand at the wall.

Each of these mistakes results in downtime, and downtime of hired equipment is paid for in the same way as work.

What to allow for in the design of a high-rise facade to make installation easier

It is more convenient to choose the means of access at the same time as the cladding is designed rather than afterwards: panel size, fixing arrangement and the sequence of work sections depend directly on what they will be installed from and with.

  • The dimensions and weight of the elements are matched to the lifting capacity of the crane at working radius and to the capabilities of the platforms.
  • Lifting loops or embedded fixings for slinging are provided in the panels, and the fixing detail is designed so that the panel is hung from above and adjusted without access from the rear.
  • The layout is coordinated with the tie points of scaffolding and masts, identifying in advance the panels to be installed last.
  • Product marking and the order of dispatch are tied to the work sections so that pallets do not have to be sorted on site.

FAQ

Which is better for a high-rise facade: scaffolding or suspended cradles?

It depends on the volume of work and the cladding. Cradles are quicker to set up and suit tall flat walls with light cladding. Scaffolding provides a continuous front for several crews and is more convenient for heavy panels and complex ornament. On large projects the methods are combined.

Can GFRC panels be installed from facade cradles?

Small elements, yes. Large panels are usually lifted by crane or hoist, and installers receive them from scaffolding or a wide platform. The decision is recorded in the method statement, taking into account element weight and the slinging scheme.

Do you need permits to put up scaffolding against a facade?

Scaffolding is erected according to its certificate or a calculation and accepted by a signed record. If it occupies a pavement or public land, approval from the local authority and a works permit are required; the procedure depends on the city.

At what wind speed is working in a cradle not allowed?

The maximum wind speed is stated in the certificate of the specific cradle and in the method statement. If it is exceeded, work stops and the cradle is lowered and secured.

How much does facade installation cost including scaffolding?

The cost depends on the area, height, cladding type and the method of access to the wall. TECHNOFORMA calculates it after inspecting the site.

Can facade panels be supplied by tower crane?

Yes, if the crane is still on site and its time has been allocated in the programme. In practice the crane has often been dismantled by the time cladding begins, so the scheme for supplying heavy elements — by winches from the roof, a mobile crane or a mast climber — is determined in advance in the method statement.

What is done with the scaffold tie positions after dismantling?

The anchor is removed, the hole is made good, the insulation and membrane are reinstated, and then the cladding panel that was left out is installed. The work is accepted as concealed work, and panels for these areas are reserved from the same batch.

Is rope access suitable for installing a ventilated facade?

Not as the main method: from a rope it is difficult to drill, align the subframe and receive heavy elements. Rope access technicians are engaged for inspections, sealing, washing and replacement of individual small panels.
