Architectural Lighting for Facades with Ornament

Architectural facade lighting is a lighting system that reveals the form of a building after dark: it emphasises volume, the rhythm of windows, cornices, columns and other decorative elements. Unlike functional site lighting, its purpose is the image of the building, not lighting the paths.

Well-designed lighting can make a relief facade more expressive at night than by day. The main rule of successful lighting is that it is designed together with the facade, not after it. Then the cables are hidden in the ventilated cavity, the luminaires are built into the ornament, and the light works with the relief of the cladding instead of glaring into windows.

This article is for architects, lighting designers and clients of commercial and public buildings. More on rainscreen systems in Ventilated rainscreen facades.

Why light a facade

Facade lighting serves several purposes:

  • Image and recognisability. At night a building is perceived differently than by day; lighting lets you pick out what matters: the silhouette, the portico, the crowning cornice.
  • Commercial value. For business centres, hotels and retail buildings, a lit facade works as a sign and increases visibility.
  • Urban environment. In many cities, lighting of buildings on main streets is part of architectural lighting programmes and is regulated by local requirements.
  • Safety. Lit entrances, colonnades and galleries are more comfortable for visitors.

Column lighting is one of the most expressive techniques: a luminaire at the base or in the capital draws the shaft upwards with light, while the flutes and the profiles of bases and capitals get deep shadows. For classical facades in GFRC or stone, this is often the main night-time accent.

The main techniques of architectural lighting:

Good lighting usually combines two or three techniques: for example, an outline along the crowning cornice, grazing light across a rusticated plinth and accents on the entrance columns.

Concealed cabling

A ventilated rainscreen facade has the advantage of space behind the cladding for cables. But it has to be used by the rules:

  • cable routes, cable exit points and luminaire fixing points are marked on the cladding layout at the design stage;
  • cables run in non-combustible or flame-retardant ducts and conduits fixed to the substructure, not lying on the insulation;
  • penetrations through the cladding are sealed so that water does not enter the cavity;
  • drivers and junction boxes are placed in accessible locations, such as on the roof or in service recesses, not behind fixed panels;
  • embedded fixings are provided for luminaires in the ornament: drilling finished cladding on site to mount them is risky and looks bad.

If lighting is added after the facade has been installed, the cable routes usually cannot be fully concealed, and some cables end up exposed.

The working sequence looks like this: the lighting designer issues a concept and luminaire layout, the facade designer transfers it to the cladding layout and details, the electrical engineer designs the circuits and routes, and the cladding manufacturer builds holes and fixings into the products. Cables are installed after the substructure and insulation but before the cladding is hung, so the sequence of works is fixed in the schedule.

Ornament and light

Ornament in glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) and stone is ideal for lighting because it has depth. A few practical principles:

  • A cornice can serve as a hood for a linear luminaire: a fitting built into the cornice lights the wall from below or above while remaining invisible.
  • Columns and pilasters are lit from below with a narrow beam, so the vertical and the texture read well.
  • Window surrounds and pediments cast shadows under grazing light and trace the rhythm of the windows.
  • Balustrades look good backlit, with balusters silhouetted against a light background.

When designing GFRC ornament, recesses for luminaires, cable channels and embedded fixings can be built directly into the mould. TECHNOFORMA makes models and moulds in its own mould-making workshops, so such modifications are made at the production stage rather than on site. Cladding colour also affects the light: light warm tones reflect more and look softer under a warm colour temperature.

Operation

Lighting operates outdoors all year round, so operational decisions matter as much as aesthetics:

  • luminaires are chosen with dust and moisture protection suitable for outdoor installation and with a temperature margin;
  • every luminaire must be accessible for maintenance from the roof, a cradle or a lift;
  • timer or daylight sensor control reduces electricity costs;
  • directional luminaires must not dazzle residents or shine into windows; this is a frequent complaint in residential buildings and hotels, so tilt angles and optics are checked at the design stage;
  • as-built cable route drawings and the luminaire schedule are handed over to the operator.

Dirt is a separate issue. Upward-facing luminaires collect dust and leaves, and lit cornices show streaks sooner. Regular cleaning of luminaires and the cladding around them is part of the maintenance schedule; water-repellent treatment of porous surfaces slows the appearance of stains.

Which colour temperature suits stone and GFRC

Warm-toned claddings such as beige GFRC, travertine, limestone and sandstone are usually lit with warm white light, whereas grey granite, concrete and metal surfaces call for neutral white. Cool white on warm stone produces a greyish, lifeless tint, and an overly warm source turns grey concrete a dirty yellow. The colour temperature is fixed in the design either as a single value for the whole facade or according to a clear logic, for example warm light on the decorative elements and neutral light on the glazed volumes.

ParameterTypical rangeWhat it affects
Colour temperature2700–3000 K (warm white), about 4000 K (neutral)The tint of the cladding at night and how it sits with the light from the windows
Colour rendering indexAs a rule not below 80; preferably higher for natural stoneHow faithfully the colour and veining of the stone are read
Beam angleNarrow (roughly 10–15°) for columns and pilasters, medium for piers, wide or elliptical for flat surfacesHow high the light climbs and how sharp the light pool is
Glare controlVisors, snoots, honeycomb louvresComfort of residents and passers-by
ControlTimed switching, dimming, scenesEnergy use and the night-time mode

LEDs of the same model from different batches may differ in tint, so the luminaires for one facade are ordered as a single batch with spares for replacement. The same applies to the cladding: how the tone and relief of a surface change its perception is covered in the article on GFRC textures.

Coloured and dynamic light is used with caution on classical decor. Saturated colours distort the tone of stone and GFRC and blur fine profiles, so for the everyday mode white light of a single temperature is usually retained, while coloured scenes are switched on for holidays and events. If dynamic effects are nevertheless required, luminaires with a separate white channel are chosen: white mixed from coloured diodes renders the shades of natural materials noticeably worse. The speed of scene changes is limited so that the facade does not turn into a screen or irritate the residents of neighbouring buildings.

How far from the wall should luminaires be placed

The closer the luminaire is to the surface and the more acute the angle, the stronger the relief appears, and the more visible any unevenness becomes. Grazing light from 10–30 cm brings out the texture of stone and the depth of rustication, but it also reveals lipping between adjacent slabs, irregular joints and waviness of the plane. The decision to place luminaires close to the wall is therefore taken together with the tolerances for cladding installation.

  • Tight to the wall (up to 30 cm). Maximum texture; suitable for rusticated plinths, split-face and bush-hammered stone and panels with deep relief.
  • Moderate offset (roughly 0.5–1 m). Softer shadows and a more even wash over the height; a compromise for facades with fine profiles such as architraves, string courses and mouldings.
  • From a distance (poles, neighbouring roofs, ground level). Floodlighting that flattens the relief; used for the silhouette and for upper tiers where power is difficult to bring.

The height the beam reaches depends on the optics and the output, so for long verticals, such as pilasters spanning several storeys or tall columns, luminaires are sometimes arranged in two tiers: at the base and on an intermediate string course. Polished granite and glass are barely lit by grazing light and produce reflections, so they need other techniques.

What to give the decor manufacturer for integrated facade lighting

For recesses, ducts and cast-in fixings to be formed in the units at the factory, the cladding manufacturer needs the luminaire data before the models and moulds are made. Once the moulds are in production, changing the cornice section or the position of a recess means reworking the tooling.

  1. Luminaire schedule: dimensions, weight, fixing method and the clearances required for cooling.
  2. Setting-out on the facade elevations: luminaire axes, levels and spacing along the cornice or string course.
  3. Diameters and positions of cable entries, and the location of drivers and junction boxes.
  4. Maintenance requirements: how the luminaire is withdrawn from the recess and whether a removable piece or access hatch is needed.
  5. Drainage: the fall of the recess floor, weep holes and a drip above the luminaire.

On this basis the shop drawings of the units show the recess, the cast-in parts and the local thickening of the wall at the fixing point. For GFRC cornices this is usually a ledge or groove in the upper part of the profile; for columns and capitals it is a seat at the base and a concealed cable entry through the shaft or pedestal. Coordinating the lighting package with the cladding is easiest within a single facade design, where the panel layout, details and cable routes are brought together in one model.

Common mistakes in architectural lighting of a building

Most problems with the night-time appearance arise not from the luminaires but from poor coordination between design packages and the absence of a trial viewing. The list below can be used as a checklist when reviewing the documentation.

  • Uniform flooding of a facade with relief: the shadows disappear and the decor looks flat.
  • Different colour temperatures on adjacent areas without any architectural logic.
  • Luminaires that are conspicuous by day: housings and cables on the face of the cladding, not finished to match it.
  • Light entering the windows of flats and hotel rooms, and dazzling floodlights at eye level near entrances.
  • Recesses without drainage: water and ice put the luminaire out of service and leave streaks on the cladding.
  • Drivers behind fixed panels that cannot be removed without dismantling part of the facade.
  • No night-time mode: full brightness all night where a reduced scene would be sufficient.

A reliable way to avoid most of these mistakes is a full-scale sample. Luminaires of the chosen model are switched on temporarily on a cladding mock-up or on the first installed bay, and the result is assessed in the dark: colour, beam length, reflections and the visibility of defects. The optics, tilt angle and output are then adjusted before the whole batch is purchased.

How lighting differs for residential complexes, hotels and business centres

The techniques are the same, but the priorities differ: in housing the main thing is not to disturb residents, in a hotel it is the image and guest comfort, and in an office building it is recognisability and moderate running costs.

Building typeWhat is litWhat to watch
Residential complexEntrances, lower storeys, crowning cornice, accents at the cornersNo light in the windows, reduced brightness at night, maintenance without entering the flats
HotelPortal, colonnades, terraces, silhouetteWarm light, no reflections in the rooms, scenes for events
Business centreOutline of the volume, entrance zone, logoBalance with the interior light of the glazing, timed control
Public buildingPortico, pediment, main axisRestraint and compliance with municipal lighting requirements
Private houseEntrance, plinth, individual decorative elementsLow output and a simple control scheme

For hotels the night-time image is part of the product, so the lighting package should be discussed as early as the concept stage; such buildings are covered in more detail in the article on hotel facades. Whatever the building type, the maintenance schedule includes inspection of the luminaires and care of the cladding in the areas where light emphasises soiling.

How lighting affects the programme and budget of facade works

The lighting equipment itself usually accounts for a small share of the facade cost, but it has a noticeable effect on the sequence of works. The main costs and risks lie not in the luminaires but in what has to be built into the adjacent packages.

  • Modification of models and moulds for recesses, grooves and cast-in parts.
  • Cable routes, ducts, penetrations through the cladding and their sealing.
  • Distribution boards, drivers and the scene control system.
  • Access equipment for installing and later maintaining the luminaires.
  • Commissioning: aiming the luminaires and setting brightness and schedules.

The programme allows a window for cable works between the installation of the insulation and the hanging of the cladding on each bay. If no such window is provided, the installers have to remove panels that are already in place, which brings a risk of chipping and of delay. The as-built drawing of the routes is prepared immediately after the cables are laid, while they are still visible, and is appended to the facade documentation.

FAQ

When should facade lighting be designed?

At the same time as the facade: cables are then hidden in the rainscreen cavity, and fixings and recesses for luminaires are built into the cladding and ornament during production.

How should columns on a facade be lit?

With narrow-beam luminaires at the base or in the capital: the light draws the shaft upwards and emphasises the profiles of the base and capital.

Can a luminaire be built into a GFRC cornice?

Yes, if the recess and cable channel are built into the mould. Drilling a finished element on site is not recommended.

Does facade lighting need approval?

In many cities, architectural lighting on main streets is regulated by local rules and approved together with the building's appearance.

What lighting suits a relief facade?

Grazing light at a steep angle and accent lighting: unlike even floodlighting, they preserve the shadows.

Which colour temperature is best for a natural stone facade?

Warm stones such as travertine, limestone and sandstone are usually best served by warm white light in the 2700–3000 K range, while grey granite and basalt suit neutral white of about 4000 K. The final choice is made on a full-scale cladding sample after dark.

Does grazing light reveal defects in cladding installation?

Yes. A luminaire mounted tight to the wall emphasises not only the texture but also lipping between slabs, irregular joints and deviations of the plane. If this technique is part of the design, the installation tolerances for those areas are specified in advance.

What fails most often in facade lighting?

In practice it is usually not the LEDs themselves that fail but the drivers, the seals and the cable connections that have taken in moisture. For this reason drivers are placed in accessible dry locations and luminaire recesses are made with drainage.
