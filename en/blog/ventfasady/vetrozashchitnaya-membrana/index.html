Wind Barrier Membrane in a Ventilated Facade

A facade wind barrier membrane is a vapour-permeable sheet laid over the insulation in a ventilated rainscreen system. It prevents the airflow in the ventilation gap from eroding fibres and cooling the mineral wool, protects it from water droplets, and at the same time lets water vapour pass from inside the wall to the outside.

The key selection criterion is not the price per roll but whether the membrane matches the facade system in vapour permeability, UV resistance and, above all, fire performance. The question is relevant to designers, general contractors and technical supervisors, as well as to clients comparing rainscreen facade estimates.

The membrane's place in the overall construction is described in the Ventilated rainscreen facades section.

Why a membrane is needed

In a ventilated facade, air constantly moves behind the cladding: it rises through the gap due to temperature differences and wind pressure. This flow carries moisture away but also acts on the insulation. A rainscreen membrane solves several tasks:

  • Protection from wind erosion. Air in the gap gradually blows fibres off the board surface, especially with light grades of mineral wool and on tall buildings. The membrane covers the surface.
  • Preserving thermal performance. Insulation that air blows through loses part of its thermal performance: cold air penetrates its thickness. The wind barrier reduces these losses.
  • Protection from water droplets. Driving rain gets into the gap through open cladding joints. The membrane stops it soaking into the insulation and drains it downwards.
  • Vapour release. Vapour from the rooms diffuses through the wall and insulation outwards. The membrane must let it pass — otherwise moisture will condense in the insulation.

Whether a membrane is needed in a particular system is decided by the system developer. There are systems with dense water-repellent boards or faced boards where a separate membrane is not required. The decision must be recorded in the album of technical solutions and the system's technical certificate, not made on site.

Types

By material and construction, wind barrier membranes for rainscreen facades fall into several groups.

TypeConstructionFeatures
Polymer non-woven membranesPolypropylene or polyester sheets, single- or multi-layerHigh vapour permeability and light weight; combustible, must be checked against the system's fire performance
Fire-retardant membranesPolymer base with additives that reduce combustibilityFor systems with higher fire requirements; combustibility group per documents
Coated glass fabrics and glass tissuesMineral base with a thin coatingNon-combustible or low-combustibility; good resistance to high temperatures
Faced insulationMineral wool board with a factory glass tissue layerNo separate membrane is laid

Besides material, membranes differ in vapour permeability, water resistance, tensile strength and permissible UV exposure time before being covered by cladding. With open cladding joints, resistance to constant UV in the joint also matters — some membranes are made in a dark colour specifically for this. Exact values are taken from the technical data of the specific product.

Installation

The membrane is installed immediately after the insulation, in the same section.

  • The sheet is rolled out horizontally or vertically — as recommended by the system manufacturer — with adjacent strips overlapping, usually by 100–150 mm. The upper sheet overlaps the lower one so that water runs down the surface rather than into the joint.
  • The membrane is fixed with the insulation dowels; separate dowels are used only if the scheme provides for them.
  • Around brackets, the sheet is cut in a cross and pressed tightly; large cuts are taped.
  • At openings the membrane is turned into the reveals, and at sills it goes over the sill so that water is drained outwards.
  • The membrane is not stretched drum-tight: it must sit tightly on the insulation but without tension, so it does not tear with thermal movement.

Cladding should be installed soon after the membrane: many polymer sheets have limited sun resistance, and their properties deteriorate after a long delay. For technical supervision, the membrane is a concealed work and is accepted with a signed record together with the insulation.

Combustibility

Membrane combustibility is an important safety issue for rainscreen facades. The membrane is a continuous layer over the whole facade area inside the ventilation gap, and if it is combustible, fire can spread along it behind the cladding, where it is hard to see and extinguish.

  • Fire safety is assessed for the system as a whole — by fire tests on a facade mock-up. The membrane specified in the system documents must not be replaced with a cheaper one: this changes the fire performance of the construction.
  • For buildings with high requirements — high-rise, public use — non-combustible glass materials or membranes with reduced combustibility confirmed by tests are used.
  • Steel fire barriers must cut through both the membrane and the ventilation gap to block the path of fire.

Cladding made of non-combustible materials, such as GFRC of the NG (non-combustible) group, reduces risks but does not remove the requirements for the membrane and insulation.

Mistakes

Typical mistakes that make a membrane ineffective:

  • wrong overlaps — lower sheet over the upper one, so water runs into the joint;
  • membrane laid the wrong side out — many sheets have a face and a back;
  • a vapour barrier film used instead of a vapour-permeable membrane — moisture is trapped in the insulation;
  • wide cuts around brackets left untaped;
  • a long pause before cladding installation — the sheet is degraded by UV;
  • a combustible membrane in a system that requires a non-combustible one;
  • a blocked ventilation gap: the membrane billows and touches the cladding, obstructing airflow.

Most of these mistakes can be seen during acceptance of concealed works, while the insulation and membrane are still exposed. Technical supervision should check the sheet brand against the system documents, check overlaps and cuts at brackets, and record everything with photographs attached to the record — once the rails and cladding are installed, this will no longer be possible.

How should the properties of a wind barrier membrane be read?

Five or six figures in the product data sheet matter, and they have to be assessed together: high vapour permeability is of no use if the material does not meet the fire requirements of the system. The specific threshold values are set by the facade system developer, not by the seller of the rolls.

PropertyWhat it meansWhat it affects
Vapour permeability (often given as Sd, the equivalent air layer thickness)How easily water vapour passes through the sheet; the lower the Sd, the betterDrying of the insulation and the wall
Water resistanceThe water column pressure the sheet withstands without leakingProtection in driving rain and with open joints
Air permeabilityHow much air passes through the sheet under a pressure differenceWind washing of the insulation, heat loss
Tensile and tear strengthResistance to loads during installation and in windIntegrity at anchors and brackets
UV resistancePermissible period of exposurePlanning of work sections
Combustibility and flammability groupsBehaviour when exposed to fireWhether it may be used in the system

When two products are compared, it is worth making sure the figures were obtained by the same test methods: manufacturers state them to different standards, and the numbers are not directly comparable.

Is a membrane needed when the insulation is in two layers?

With two-layer insulation the outer layer partly takes on the role of the wind barrier: a dense slab is less prone to wind washing and loses fewer fibres than a light one. For this reason some systems do not provide for a membrane over a dense outer layer, while others retain it as protection against liquid water.

The decision depends on several conditions: the density and water repellency of the outer slab, the height of the building and the wind zone, the type of cladding joints, and the cavity width. On tall buildings and in corner zones the air speed in the cavity is higher, and the requirements for protecting the insulation surface increase. Where there is no membrane, tight butting of the slabs with staggered joints between layers is especially important: gaps between slabs are subject to more air flow than the surface itself.

Omitting the sheet to save money and replacing it with a denser slab is a change to the system. It is agreed with the developer and reflected in the design; otherwise questions about conformity with the documents will arise at acceptance.

When does a facade wind barrier membrane harm the moisture balance of the wall?

The membrane does harm when its resistance to vapour diffusion is higher than that of the layers closer to the room. The wall should open up towards the outside: each successive layer lets vapour through at least as well as the one before it. If this rule is broken, moisture is held at the boundary between the insulation and the sheet.

In practice the problem arises in three cases. The first is replacing the membrane with a film of unknown origin. The second is a double layer of sheet in areas where the laps were made with a large margin or the old sheet was not removed during repairs. The third is contamination: construction dust and cement laitance clog the pores, and the vapour permeability of the area drops.

During construction, in-situ concrete and masonry walls release a great deal of moisture, so the load on the wind barrier is highest in the first seasons. The moisture balance is verified by calculation as part of the thermal design section; how it relates to the thickness and grade of the slabs is covered in the article on insulating a ventilated facade.

Membranes behind different claddings: closed and open joints

The requirements for the wind barrier depend on how much water and light reach the cavity. The wider and more frequent the open joints in the cladding, the higher the requirements for water resistance and UV resistance of the sheet.

  • Porcelain tiles and fibre cement on clips. The joints are narrow but open; in driving rain water reaches the cavity regularly. Reliable water resistance and careful laps are needed.
  • Cassettes and panels with closed joints. There is less water in the cavity, and the main function of the sheet is protection against wind washing.
  • Louvres, perforation, cladding with wide joints. The sheet is visible from outside and constantly exposed to light; materials designed for prolonged sun exposure are used, usually dark ones.
  • Stone and GFRC. The cladding is heavy and the brackets are more numerous and more massive, so tear strength and the quality of the fit around the fixings matter.

With large-format cladding, installation proceeds more slowly and the sheet stays exposed for longer. This is taken into account when choosing the material and dividing the work into sections during planning of the ventilated facade installation.

Storing and handling the membrane on site

The properties of the sheet can be lost even before it is laid. Rolls are stored in the factory packaging, under cover or indoors, away from welding and naked flames; storage in the sun uses up the same UV resistance allowance as standing exposed on the facade.

  • The sheet is rolled out over dry insulation: moisture that has been covered will take a long time to escape.
  • In windy weather the work is done in short lengths that are fixed at once, otherwise the sheet billows and tears at the anchors.
  • Angle grinding and welding are not carried out near the laid membrane, or it is covered with a non-combustible screen.
  • Damage is repaired with a patch of the same material, lapped and sealed with compatible tape.
  • Overnight and during breaks in the work, the top edge of the insulation and membrane is covered against precipitation.

At sub-zero temperatures the laying itself is possible, but adhesive tapes hold less well, so they are chosen with the permissible installation temperature in mind. Restrictions on other operations are listed in the article on facade works in winter.

Difficult areas: corners, openings, fire barriers and junctions

The continuity of the wind barrier is most often broken not on the general wall area but at the boundaries: at building corners, around windows and where it crosses fire barriers. These areas are worth showing in the design as separate diagrams.

  • External corners. The sheet is carried round the corner with a margin and not cut along the arris; wind pressure is highest here, and a joint on the arris is the first to open.
  • Window openings. The membrane is cut envelope-fashion, turned into the reveals and fixed; above the opening the sheet laps over the head element of the surround, and below the opening it is led out onto the sill flashing.
  • Fire barriers. A steel barrier interrupts the sheet: the bottom edge of the upper sheet is brought out over the flange of the barrier so that water runs outwards and not behind it.
  • Plinth and parapet. The bottom edge is fixed so that water from the sheet reaches the plinth flashing; the top edge is protected by the parapet coping.
  • Movement joints. The sheet is laid with a compensating loop, without tension.

What should be checked in the documents before buying the wind barrier?

Before purchase it is enough to compare four documents: the design, the system's album of technical solutions, the technical certificate and the documents for the material itself. If the sheet grade in them does not match, the matter is settled with the system developer before delivery, not after laying.

  1. The grade and manufacturer of the membrane in the design match those stated in the system documents.
  2. There is a fire safety certificate or test reports giving the combustibility and flammability groups.
  3. The data sheet states vapour permeability, water resistance and the UV resistance period.
  4. Compatible tapes and the fixing method are specified.
  5. The date of manufacture and the condition of the packaging allow the batch to be used.

How the approval documents for a system are structured and what to look for in them is described in the article on the technical certificate and technical assessment for a ventilated facade system. In the cost estimate the wind barrier is usually shown as a separate line in the facade insulation section, with an allowance for laps and trimming.

What happens to the ventilated facade membrane in service?

Behind continuous cladding a properly laid sheet lasts a long time, because it is shielded from the sun and from mechanical impact. Its service life is shortened by local causes: open joints designed without regard to ultraviolet, leaks at the parapet and flashings, and work on the facade after handover.

The condition of the wind barrier is assessed by opening up the cladding selectively during scheduled inspections or repairs. Signs of trouble are a sagging or torn sheet, traces of wetting in the insulation, and material that feels brittle to the touch. When individual slabs or panels are replaced, the membrane beneath them is made good before the new cladding is fitted. The inspection intervals and scope of work are covered in the article on maintaining a ventilated facade.

FAQ

Does a ventilated facade need a wind barrier membrane?

Yes, if the system provides for one. There are systems with dense or faced insulation boards where a separate membrane is not needed; this must be stated in the system documents.

Which side of the membrane goes against the insulation?

As specified by the manufacturer: many membranes have the face side marked. Incorrect laying reduces water resistance and vapour permeability.

Can a combustible membrane be used in a ventilated facade?

Only if it is part of a system that has passed fire tests for the given building class. Buildings with higher requirements use non-combustible glass materials.

What overlap does a wind barrier membrane need?

Usually 100–150 mm, upper sheet over lower; the exact value is in the manufacturer's instructions.

How long can a membrane be left exposed without cladding?

It depends on the product and its UV resistance; the period is stated by the manufacturer. Cladding is best installed straight after insulation.

How does a wind barrier membrane differ from a vapour barrier?

A vapour barrier does not let vapour through and is placed on the room side, if the construction provides for one at all. A wind barrier membrane, by contrast, is vapour-permeable and is laid on the outside of the insulation. Confusing the two means trapping moisture in the wall.

Can the membrane be laid in two layers to be on the safe side?

No, unless the system provides for it. A second layer increases the resistance to vapour escaping and adds combustible material to the cavity, while it does not noticeably improve wind protection.

How is membrane consumption accounted for in the cost estimate?

By the insulated area plus an allowance for laps, turning into reveals and trimming at brackets. The size of the allowance depends on the roll width and the number of openings and is usually in the order of 10–15%.
