Fibre Cement Panels for Ventilated Facades

Fibre cement panels are flat facade boards made of cement reinforced with cellulose or synthetic fibres, with mineral fillers. In a ventilated facade they are fixed to the substructure with screws, rivets or concealed clips and provide a flat painted or textured surface at moderate weight.

The key selection criterion is the building's use and height: fibre cement suits residential buildings, detached houses, administrative and warehouse buildings that need inexpensive non-combustible or low-combustibility cladding without modelling. If three-dimensional elements or stone texture are required, GFRC or natural stone is usually chosen.

Fibre cement's place among other claddings is in the Ventilated rainscreen facades section.

Composition

The composition of fibre cement boards varies between manufacturers, but the basis is the same:

  • Portland cement — the binder that provides strength and non-combustibility;
  • reinforcing fibres — cellulose or synthetic; they take tensile stresses and prevent the board from cracking;
  • mineral fillers — quartz sand, limestone flour and others; they control density and shrinkage;
  • water and process additives.

Boards are formed in layers and pressed, then cured in steam chambers or an autoclave. The face is given a factory coating or left natural with an impregnation. Modern facade boards are asbestos-free — asbestos has been replaced by cellulose and synthetic fibres; when purchasing, this is worth checking against the declaration of composition, especially for cheap products of unknown origin.

Composition also determines practical properties: the higher the share of organic fibre, the more flexible and easier to work the board is, but the greater its moisture movement and the more important edge protection becomes. Mineral fillers, by contrast, increase dimensional stability and fire resistance. This is how fibre cement differs from GFRC: GFRC is reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre and can be cast into three-dimensional shapes, while fibre cement is mainly a flat sheet material.

The dimensions of fibre cement rainscreen panels are limited by the board's flexural strength and the design wind load. The material absorbs moisture through its edges more than through its face, so edges are treated with protective products after cutting.

Installation

Fibre cement boards are installed on a standard rainscreen substructure — aluminium or galvanised steel.

  • Visible fixing — screws or rivets through holes in the board, with washers matching the coating colour. Holes are drilled oversize so the board can move freely with changes in humidity and temperature; some points are made fixed.
  • Concealed fixing — expansion anchors into the back of the board with horizontal profiles, or adhesive systems approved by the manufacturer. Requires thicker boards.
  • Clip fixing — for small formats, similar to porcelain stoneware.

The sequence is standard: setting-out, brackets, insulation and wind barrier, rails, cladding. Boards are best cut on site with a saw fitted with dust extraction — cement dust is harmful, and wet cutting leaves stains on the coating. EPDM tape is laid between the board and the rail to avoid metal–cement contact and to stop water lingering on the profile.

Rail spacing and the number of fixing points are specified by the board manufacturer depending on thickness and wind zone; at corners and parapets, where wind suction is highest, fixing points are placed closer together. Minimum edge distances for holes are strictly observed — otherwise the edge breaks off when tightened. Joints between boards are left open, with the width per design; horizontal joints are covered with a flashing profile where necessary.

Painting

Fibre cement panels are supplied with or without a factory coating.

  • Factory painting — acrylic or other facade coatings applied and cured in the factory. Uniform colour and durability; when boards are cut, edges are painted with a touch-up product.
  • On-site painting — possible for primed boards. Facade paints compatible with a cement substrate are used, as recommended by the board manufacturer. Painting is done at above-zero temperatures in dry weather.
  • Natural surface with a water-repellent impregnation — keeps a concrete look but shows dirt more.

Over time the coating fades, and the facade will need repainting. The interval depends on the coating, wall orientation and climate — this should be factored into the building's cost of ownership. Repainting is done from cradles or scaffolding on a cleaned, dust-free surface, so it is easier to plan together with other facade maintenance work.

Types of fibre cement panels: by density, finish and format

Boards with different properties are sold under the same name, and not all of them are suitable for a facade. They are distinguished by curing method, density, type of face surface and format; durability and area of application depend on the combination of these features.

FeatureOptionsWhat it means for the facade
Curing methodAutoclaved and non-autoclaved boardsAutoclaving generally gives more stable dimensions when the board gets wet
DensityMedium and high densityDense boards are stronger and absorb less water, but are heavier and harder to cut
ColouringSurface-coated or through-colouredOn through-coloured boards, chips and scratches are less noticeable
TextureSmooth, wood-effect, stone-effect, with stone chippingsRelief hides minor irregularities of the plane; chippings resist fading
FormatLarge boards and narrow planks (siding)Boards — for public and multi-storey buildings, planks — for low-rise

For a ventilated system on a building higher than a few storeys, dense boards from 8 mm thick with a factory coating and a full set of manufacturer's documents are usually chosen. Lapped planks are designed for a different fixing method and are rarely used in multi-storey construction. It is important that the specific grade is named in the technical approval for the system: selecting boards by appearance from another supplier changes both the fixing spacing and the fire characteristics of the construction.

Where is a ventilated facade of fibre cement panels appropriate, and where is another cladding better?

Fibre cement is justified where a flat matt plane with widely spaced joints is needed on a moderate budget. On projects with high requirements for impact resistance, facade modelling or service life without repainting, it is usually supplemented with other materials.

  • Schools, nurseries, health centres. The main area of application: a non-combustible base, a wide palette, simple replacement of boards. The lowest tier is often made of a more impact-resistant material.
  • Mid-rise residential buildings. Boards are used on standard planes and in colour inserts, combined with glazed loggias.
  • Retail and industrial buildings. Large blank surfaces are closed quickly, and the large format reduces the number of joints.
  • Private houses. Both boards and planks are used; careful work on edges and junctions matters here.
  • High-rise buildings and projects with complex modelling. Fibre cement is used to a limited extent: wind loads require closely spaced fixings, and curved forms cannot be made from sheet material.

The nearest alternatives in the same price group are described in the articles on a porcelain stoneware ventilated facade and composite cassettes. The general procedure for comparing materials by weight, fire properties and cost of ownership is given in the article on how to choose facade cladding.

Cutting and working the boards on site

Fibre cement is cut and drilled on site, and this is what makes it convenient for facades with a large number of make-up pieces. But every operation leaves an exposed cut of cement matrix that needs to be protected.

Procedure for working with a board:

  1. Cutting is done with a circular saw fitted with a diamond or carbide blade intended for fibre cement, on a guide rail and with dust extraction.
  2. Dust is removed from the face immediately and dry: cement dust left behind bites into the coating after rain and leaves whitish stains.
  3. Edges are lightly eased, and cuts are treated with the primer or paint recommended by the board manufacturer.
  4. Fixing holes are drilled in advance, on a support, keeping the edge distances given in the manufacturer's instructions.
  5. One fixing point is made fixed and the rest sliding, with a larger-diameter hole, so that the board can change its dimensions freely.

Boards are carried on edge and stored on a level base under cover: a board wetted on one side can warp even before installation. Cutting and drilling in a stack, without marking out each sheet, should be avoided — a deviation of a few millimetres on visible fixings reads on the facade as an uneven line of rivets.

Joints and layout: what the appearance of the facade depends on

The joint pattern shapes the perception of a fibre cement facade more strongly than the colour of the boards. It is designed before the material is ordered, tied to the axes of openings, floor slab levels and the corners of the building.

Joints between boards are left open or closed with a decorative profile. The joint width is set by the manufacturer and is usually in the order of 8–10 mm: this is enough to accommodate thermal and moisture movement. Under a vertical joint, a sealing tape is applied to the rail; it protects the profile from water and makes the joint look dark and even. Horizontal joints are either left open or closed with a profile that drains water outwards.

Practical layout rules:

  • joint lines are aligned with the reveals and heads of openings to avoid narrow make-up strips;
  • the minimum width of a make-up piece is limited — a narrow strip holds fixings poorly and splits easily;
  • at corners, a corner profile or a joint with an exposed edge painted in the board colour is used;
  • the rails are positioned to suit the layout, not the other way round.

The last rule links the cladding to the frame: the spacing and type of profiles are selected together with the board format. How this is done is described in the article on the ventilated facade subframe.

Typical defects of fibre cement cladding and their causes

Most defects arise not from the material but from fixing that ignores board movement and from unprotected cuts. Almost all of them are prevented at the installation stage and are difficult to correct afterwards.

DefectProbable causeHow to avoid it
Cracks from fixing pointsAll points rigid, small edge distance, overtightened fixingsFixed and sliding points, edge distances and tightening torque as instructed
Warping of boardsUneven wetting, widely spaced railsCorrect storage, profile spacing as in the manufacturer's documentation
Darkening and peeling of the coating at edgesUntreated cuts absorb waterPriming and painting all edges after cutting
Whitish stains and streaksCement dust, water from parapets and sillsDry cleaning after cutting, drip edges and correct sill projections
Shade variation across the facadeBoards from different batches, uneven fadingPurchasing with a reserve from one batch, allocating batches by elevation
Chipped corners in the lower zoneImpact during use and site cleaningImpact-resistant plinth cladding, protective elements

During operation, the condition of the coating and fixings is monitored at scheduled inspections; exactly what to check and how often is set out in the article on ventilated facade maintenance. The repainting interval depends on the type of coating, the orientation of the elevation and the climate, so it is best confirmed with the manufacturer of the specific grade.

Fibre cement in combination with decorative elements and other materials

Sheet cladding covers a plane, but it does not form cornices, window surrounds or portals. On buildings with expressive architecture, fibre cement is therefore used as a background material, while the modelling of the facade is created with three-dimensional elements.

The working arrangement is as follows: standard fields — boards on a typical subframe; plinth and entrance areas — natural stone or porcelain stoneware; string courses, architraves and GFRC cornices — on their own embedded parts and brackets. The decorative elements do not bear on the boards and do not transfer load to them. A joint is left between the materials to accommodate the difference in movement, and the junction is protected from above with a flashing.

In such a facade three things need to be agreed: the colour and texture of the materials on samples in natural light, the thicknesses of the constructions where they meet, and the installation sequence. Usually the brackets for all elements are fixed first, then the decorative elements are installed, and only after that are the planes closed with boards.

FAQ

How is a facade clad with fibre cement panels?

The boards are fixed to a metal rainscreen substructure with screws, rivets or concealed anchors, over insulation and a wind barrier, with a ventilated gap.

What panel sizes are used for a ventilated facade?

Fibre cement boards come in formats up to 1200–1500 × 3000 mm, 8–14 mm thick; the actual element size is set by the layout and wind calculation.

Do fibre cement boards burn on a facade?

Most brands are non-combustible or low-combustibility; the combustibility group is stated in the documents for the specific product and depends on the fibre and coating.

Do fibre cement panels need painting?

Factory-coated boards are not painted during installation, only their edges after cutting. Over time the coating fades and the facade is repainted.

How does a fibre cement ventilated facade differ from a GFRC facade?

Fibre cement is flat sheet cladding, while GFRC allows three-dimensional architectural elements and stone-like textures.

What are fibre cement panels cut with on site?

With a circular saw fitted with a diamond or carbide blade for fibre cement, on a guide rail and with dust extraction. After cutting, the dust is removed dry straight away, and the cuts are treated with the primer or paint recommended by the board manufacturer.

Can fibre cement boards be used on the plinth?

It is acceptable if provided for by the manufacturer and the design, but the lower zone is exposed to impact, splashes and de-icing agents. More often the plinth is made of stone or porcelain stoneware and the fibre cement starts higher up — this way the facade keeps its appearance for longer.

What joint is left between fibre cement boards?

The width is set by the manufacturer and is usually in the order of 8–10 mm. The joint accommodates thermal and moisture movement of the boards; it must not be reduced for the sake of appearance, otherwise the edges will bear against one another and chip.
