Render Facade vs Ventilated Facade: A Comparison

A render facade (a "wet" facade, known in Russian standards as SFTK, an external thermal insulation composite system, ETICS) consists of insulation bonded and dowelled to the wall and covered with a reinforced render layer. A ventilated facade is insulation on the wall, cladding on a metal substructure and an air gap between them.

In short: a render facade is cheaper up front and works well for low-rise and standard buildings; a ventilated rainscreen facade costs more but is more reliable at height, is quicker to repair element by element and can carry heavy cladding such as porcelain stoneware, stone or glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) panels. The main selection criteria are the number of storeys, the required service life and the cladding you want to define the building's look.

Below is a comparison of build-up, durability, cost and decorative options. More on the systems in Ventilated rainscreen facades.

How each system is built

The difference starts with how the layers are connected to the wall and to each other.

LayerRender facade (ETICS)Ventilated rainscreen facade
SubstrateFlat, sound, prepared wallLoad-bearing wall or frame, tolerates unevenness
InsulationMineral wool or expanded polystyrene, adhesive + dowelsMineral wool, dowels, wind barrier membrane where required
Protective layerBase render coat with reinforcing meshAir gap through which moisture escapes
FinishDecorative render, paint, occasionally thin tilesPorcelain stoneware, composite, fibre cement, stone, GFRC on a substructure
Finish fixingAdhesion to the system layersMechanical: clips, agraffes, embedded fixings

In a render facade all layers work as a single build-up and depend on each other: a crack in the render lets moisture reach the insulation. In a rainscreen facade the cladding is separated from the insulation by a gap, and vapour from the wall escapes through the ventilated cavity.

Durability

The service life of both systems depends on the quality of materials, design and installation, so the fair comparison is not advertised figures but the ways each system ages.

FactorRender facadeRainscreen facade
Moisture in insulationEscapes through the render layers; sensitive to vapour permeabilityEscapes through the ventilated gap
RepairIn patches, followed by colour matchingBy replacing individual cladding elements
Dependence on substrateHigh: wall cracks transfer to the renderLow: the substructure compensates for unevenness
Impact resistance at ground levelRequires reinforced plinth meshDetermined by the cladding material
  • A render facade is vulnerable to errors at junctions (reveals, plinth, roof abutments), to impacts at ground level and to cracks from movement of the substrate. The decorative layer needs periodic renewal: repainting or patch repairs.
  • A rainscreen facade ages mainly through its substructure and fixings: the metal must be protected against corrosion, and fixings must be designed for wind load and cladding weight. Cladding materials such as porcelain stoneware, stone and GFRC are long-lasting in themselves, and a damaged element is replaced individually.

For tall buildings there is one more factor: fire safety. Requirements for external insulation systems and rainscreen facades are set by the current Russian fire safety codes (SP); they are stricter for multi-storey buildings. Non-combustible cladding makes compliance easier; for example, according to the manufacturer, GFRC is classed as non-combustible (NG).

Cost

A render facade is cheaper in materials and labour: there is no metal substructure, fewer fixings, and the finish is render and paint. A ventilated facade is more expensive because of the substructure, more costly cladding and labour-intensive installation.

But it is worth comparing the total cost of ownership:

  • a render facade has to be repainted and repaired periodically, and at height that again requires scaffolding or cradles;
  • a rainscreen facade can be clad in any season, while wet processes depend on temperature;
  • a rainscreen facade makes it easier to achieve complex architecture without adding load to the insulation.

The actual estimate depends on the number of storeys, area, chosen cladding and condition of the walls. TECHNOFORMA determines the installation cost after inspecting the site.

Decorative elements on each type

Decoration is one of the main differences between the systems.

On a render facade decorative elements are made from lightweight materials: expanded polystyrene profiles with a reinforced coating or thin run render mouldings. They suit small cornices and surrounds, but their projection is limited and they are sensitive to mechanical damage.

On a ventilated facade decoration is fixed mechanically to the substructure or the load-bearing wall. This allows large elements in glass fibre reinforced concrete (cornices, pilasters, columns, window surrounds, balustrades) as well as natural stone. According to TECHNOFORMA, GFRC consists of four components (glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement, water), is non-combustible and can be cast in almost any shape; the company makes its models and moulds in its own mould-making workshops.

Junctions have to be designed separately: cornice projection, drainage from horizontal surfaces, expansion joints between elements. On a render facade these details have to be solved within a single layer; on a rainscreen facade, with separate brackets and flashings.

How to choose

Guidelines for choosing:

  • Render facade: low-rise construction, limited budget, simple architecture, flat block or brick walls.
  • Ventilated facade: multi-storey residential buildings, business centres, public buildings, hotels; cladding in porcelain stoneware, stone or GFRC; buildings with classical ornament; uneven walls that need to be concealed by a substructure.
  • Combination: render on the main wall areas and ventilated sections with ornament at the plinth, entrance or upper floors. This requires careful detailing of the junctions.

Base your choice on the design: thermal calculation, wind load calculation for the substructure and fire safety requirements for the specific building height.

Heat and moisture: how rendered and ventilated facades behave

In terms of thermal protection, both systems can reach the same design thermal resistance: it is governed by the thickness and quality of the insulation, not by the type of finish. What differs is how the structure loses heat through its fixings and how it gets rid of moisture.

In a rendered system the insulation is held by adhesive and disc dowels, with almost no metal elements passing through it, so the layer is thermally very uniform. In a ventilated system the wall is crossed by brackets, and each one acts as a point thermal bridge. The losses are offset by thermal break pads, by bracket spacing set by calculation and by a small allowance in insulation thickness; this is covered in more detail in the article on thermal bridges in a facade.

With moisture the picture is reversed. In a rendered facade, vapour passes through the insulation and the render coat, so the vapour permeability of the layers has to increase from the inside outwards, and finishing paints are selected to suit the system. In a ventilated facade, vapour escapes into the cavity and is carried away by the air flow, so wet insulation dries out faster. For buildings with a humid indoor regime, and for walls that took on moisture during construction, this is a noticeable advantage.

What the wall must provide: substrate, weight and tolerances

A rendered facade is demanding in terms of surface flatness and strength; a ventilated one depends on the load-bearing capacity of the substrate for the anchor. This determines the scope of preparatory work, which is often underestimated in the cost estimate.

ParameterRendered facadeVentilated facade
Wall preparationCleaning, priming, levelling of irregularities to the system tolerancesGeodetic survey; irregularities are taken up by the bracket projection
What is checkedAdhesion of the adhesive to the substrate, strength of the surface layerAnchor pull-out force by on-site testing
Load on the wallLow, distributed over the areaHigher, transferred at points through brackets; depends on the cladding
Weak substratesAerated concrete and ceramic blocks are acceptable with suitable adhesive and dowelsSpecial anchors or fixing into the floor slabs are required
Old finishRemoved down to a sound substrateMay remain if it does not interfere with anchoring

The mass of a rendered system with mineral wool is usually in the order of 15–30 kg/m². A ventilated facade with porcelain stoneware or composite, together with the subframe, generally weighs more, and with natural stone or three-dimensional elements the load increases several times over. For this reason, when old buildings are refurbished, the choice of system starts with a survey of the walls and foundations, not with a cladding catalogue.

Programme and seasonality: which facade is quicker to install?

On a simple building of small area a rendered facade is usually completed faster; on a large project with a tight programme the ventilated system wins. The reason lies in process breaks and dependence on the weather.

Wet processes proceed in layers, and each layer needs time to gain strength and dry: adhesive, base coat with mesh, primer, decorative render, paint. Work is generally permitted at temperatures of not less than +5 °C, without rain or direct sun on a fresh coat; in the shoulder seasons the scaffolding is enclosed with netting or a heated enclosure, which adds cost. A ventilated system is assembled mechanically, so no breaks are needed between operations, and the work front can be divided into sections and handled by several crews in parallel. The limitations here relate to wind when large elements are being hung and to icing of the access platforms.

How to plan the cold period and which operations are moved to the warm season is discussed in the article on facade works in winter. For a developer, the predictability of the completion date matters more than the speed of a single crew: with a ventilated facade, a considerable share of the elements is manufactured at the factory while the subframe is being installed on site.

Can a rendered and a ventilated facade be combined on one building?

Yes, combined solutions are used frequently and allow the budget to be allocated according to the importance of each elevation. There is one condition: the junctions between the systems must be detailed in the design, not resolved on the scaffolding.

Common combinations:

  • main elevations and entrance areas — a ventilated system with stone or GFRC panels, courtyard elevations — render;
  • plinth and ground floor — slab cladding on a subframe, resistant to impact and soiling, with a rendered system above;
  • the main plane — thin-coat render, while cornices, string courses and opening surrounds are elements mechanically fixed to the wall.

At the junction, different build-up thicknesses, different thermal movements and different water drainage paths meet. A movement joint, a flashing or junction profile and a fire barrier, where the system requires one, are needed. If these details have not been developed, cracks and streaks appear along the junction line after one or two seasons; the causes and diagnosis are described in the article on facade cracks.

What input data is needed to choose a system

A well-founded choice is made from the project documents, not from general comparisons. The minimum set worth bringing to a designer or contractor:

  1. Architectural elevation drawings with levels, number of storeys and the functional fire hazard class of the building.
  2. Material and thickness of the external walls, information on the frame and floor slabs.
  3. Construction area: climate, wind zone, seismicity of the site.
  4. The required appearance: colour, texture, presence of decorative elements, share of glazing.
  5. Budget and commissioning date constraints, the season in which work starts.
  6. Expectations for operation: who will maintain the building and how often repairs are acceptable.

Using this data, the designer carries out a thermal calculation, assesses the loads and proposes one or two design options with an approximate cost. Operation and repair: what to expect after 10–15 years

Both systems require scheduled inspections, but the nature of the work differs: a rendered facade is maintained through its coating, a ventilated one through its details and fixings. Inspection is usually carried out once a year, in spring, after the frost season, and additionally after strong winds.

On a rendered facade, attention goes first to hairline cracks, delamination at reveals and sills, darkening of the plinth and traces of water under parapets. Minor defects are filled and painted, but matching the shade to a faded surface is difficult, so a local repair often ends with repainting the entire plane from corner to corner. Mechanical damage at ground-floor level is a separate cost item: a thin render coat over soft insulation tolerates impact poorly.

On a ventilated facade, the checks cover the integrity of the slabs, the condition of visible fixings, the position of elements in the plane, the cleanliness of ventilation gaps at the bottom and top, and the tightness of junctions with windows. A damaged slab is replaced with a new one without wet processes, and a reserve of cladding from the same batch should be allowed for at the purchasing stage. The surface is washed in accordance with the cladding manufacturer's procedure, while the overall order of work is set by the operating manual that the contractor hands over on completion.

When assessing the budget, it makes sense to include the cost of access straight away: scaffolding, towers or rope access are needed for any repair and on a tall building often cost more than the materials themselves.

Typical mistakes when comparing the two systems

Most unsuccessful decisions are related not to the system itself but to the way it was compared with the alternative.

  • Comparing by price per square metre without details. Reveals, parapets, the plinth and junctions can change the total noticeably in either direction.
  • Transferring decorative elements from one system to the other. A cornice conceived in stone or GFRC cannot be replaced with a lightweight profile without redesign — the projection, section and durability all change.
  • Choosing on the basis of a neighbouring project. The height, substrate and climate of another building may differ so much that the same system build-up proves inadmissible.
  • Ignoring operation. If the building has no service that will look after the coating, a rendered facade will lose its appearance sooner.
  • Substituting materials within the system. Both rendered and ventilated systems are used as a kit with confirmed characteristics; replacing the insulation, adhesive or fixings deprives the solution of that confirmation.

FAQ

Render facade or ventilated facade: which is better?

For low-rise houses with simple architecture and a limited budget, a render facade is often sufficient. For multi-storey, public and commercial buildings, and for cladding in stone, porcelain stoneware or GFRC, a ventilated facade is preferable.

What is SFTK?

It is the Russian standards term for an external thermal insulation composite system (ETICS), i.e. a render facade: insulation fixed with adhesive and dowels, a reinforced base coat and decorative render.

Can a render facade have decorative elements?

Yes, using lightweight profiles with a reinforced coating. Large cornices, columns and balustrades are more reliable in GFRC on a rainscreen system with mechanical fixing.

Which facade is cheaper?

A render facade is cheaper up front. A ventilated facade costs more, but its cladding needs less periodic repair and can be replaced element by element. The exact cost is determined after inspecting the site.

Can facades be installed in winter?

Wet processes are limited by temperature. A rainscreen facade is fixed mechanically, so it depends less on the season.

Which facade is warmer — rendered or ventilated?

With the same design thermal resistance there is no difference for the occupants. In a ventilated system some heat is lost through the brackets, so the insulation is sometimes made slightly thicker and thermal break pads are fitted. In a rendered system the layer is more uniform, but it is more sensitive to moisture.

Can a rendered facade be replaced with a ventilated one during refurbishment?

It can, provided the walls and foundations can take the additional load and the substrate holds the anchor. This is confirmed by a survey and on-site anchor tests. The old system is usually removed down to the load-bearing wall so that its condition can be checked and the insulation installed anew.

Which facade should be chosen for a framed building with aerated concrete infill?

Both options are possible. For a rendered system, the adhesive and dowels are selected for cellular concrete. For a ventilated system, the subframe is more often fixed into the slab edges, or anchors tested on the actual masonry are used; on high-rise buildings this is a standard solution.
