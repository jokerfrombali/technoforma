Natural Stone Ventilated Facade

A natural stone ventilated facade is a rainscreen system in which stone slabs are fixed to a metal substructure with clips or anchors, leaving an air gap between the cladding and the insulation. The stone is not bonded to the wall, so it is unaffected by the wall's moisture and movement, and the wall works with insulation on the outside.

The key selection criterion is the stone type and slab thickness for a specific fixing detail: not every stone that looks good indoors will withstand wind, frost and point loads on its edge. This solution is chosen for buildings with a long life cycle — premium residential buildings, public and office buildings where the texture of real stone matters.

The general logic of rainscreen systems is covered in the Ventilated rainscreen facades section.

Which stones are suitable

A rainscreen facade needs stone with low water absorption, sufficient flexural strength and proven frost resistance. These properties vary not only between stone types but also between quarries of the same stone, so exact values are taken from the test report for the specific batch.

The company also has extensive experience with Dagestan stones. Its equipment processes both soft stones (sandstone, shell limestone, dolomite, limestone) and hard ones. Onyx is not usually used on rainscreen facades — its place is in interiors and backlit elements.

Slab thickness

Slab thickness is a trade-off between the weight the substructure must carry and the strength of the stone at the fixing point. A clip or anchor loads the slab locally, and a thin slab of weak stone can spall at the kerf.

  • Hard stones (granite, basalt) — rainscreen facades usually use slabs from 20–30 mm; at height, in zones of increased wind load and for large formats, thickness is increased by calculation.
  • Marble, dense limestone, travertine — as a rule thicker than granite for the same format, since their flexural strength is lower.
  • Soft porous stones — thicker still, sometimes 40–50 mm or more; slab format is often reduced.

These figures are industry guidelines, not a standard. Thickness is set by calculation based on the flexural strength of the specific batch, slab dimensions, wind load and fixing scheme. The larger the format, the thicker the slab and the higher the weight per square metre — which means stronger brackets and closer anchor spacing.

Fixing with clips and anchors

Fixing systems for stone ventilated facades can be visible or concealed. The choice depends on the architectural intent, slab thickness and stone type.

  • Kerf clips. Stainless steel holders with plates or pins that engage a kerf or hole in the slab edge. Each slab bears on the lower clips and is restrained by the upper ones. The clip tabs are visible in the joint unless finished in the stone colour.
  • Concealed undercut anchors. Undercut holes are drilled in the back of the slab, and expansion anchors are installed in them and connected to horizontal substructure profiles. The face stays clean, but requirements for thickness and stone quality are higher.
  • Dowel fixing. Stainless steel dowels in edge holes — used for individual elements and non-standard details.

The facade fixing angle — the load-bearing or support bracket — transfers the load from the rails to the wall. Reinforced brackets with verified load-bearing capacity are used for stone. It is also checked that the clip does not touch the stone with a sharp metal edge: isolating pads are placed between them, otherwise the slab edge will chip.

Fixing stone to a facade requires precise geometry of kerfs and holes: this machining is done at the factory or on dedicated equipment, not with an angle grinder on the scaffolding.

Substructure

The substructure for stone is one of the most heavily loaded among rainscreen systems. The cladding's self-weight is high, so the calculation covers vertical load and wind pressure, and the load-bearing capacity of anchors is verified by on-site tests.

  • Material. Heavy slabs more often use galvanised or stainless steel; aluminium if its load-bearing capacity is confirmed by calculation.
  • Brackets. Load-bearing brackets that carry the weight are distinguished from support brackets that resist wind. Load-bearing brackets are fixed to floor slabs and cast-in-situ concrete where possible.
  • Rails. Vertical rails with horizontal profiles for clips or anchor fixing. Rails have sliding connections so that thermal expansion of the metal is not transferred to the slabs.
  • Insulation and membrane. Mineral wool boards, non-combustible wind barrier, ventilation gap as per design.

TECHNOFORMA installs the substructure and insulation with its own installation teams and designs the stone layout itself: surveys are done by its own surveyors, the layout and 3D visualisation by the design department, and design supervision is provided during installation.

Risks

The main risks of a stone ventilated facade stem not from the stone type as such but from a mismatch between stone, fixing detail and load.

  • Spalling at the fixing point. A thin slab, weak stone or an incorrect kerf. The only fix is replacing the slab, so thickness and fixing type are best confirmed by calculation in advance.
  • Efflorescence and darkening. Porous stones absorb water; salts from mortar or from the stone itself come to the surface. Protection is by water-repellent treatment and open joints.
  • Shade variation. Stone from different blocks and batches differs in colour. A layout with numbered slabs and a reserve from the same batch are required.
  • Corrosion and metal contact. Clips and anchors must be stainless steel only; contact between dissimilar metals must be through isolating pads.
  • Underestimated weight. Brackets designed for light cladding are not suitable for stone. Without anchor pull-out tests in the actual substrate, the substructure design remains an assumption.
  • Frost damage. If the batch's water absorption and frost resistance have not been checked, the first winters will reveal it through cracking and flaking.

Stone finishes for a ventilated facade: which treatment works better outdoors

For external cladding, matt and rough finishes are chosen more often: they show less dirt, give no glare and age evenly. Polishing is possible on a facade, but it behaves differently on different types of stone.

FinishAppearanceBehaviour on a facade
PolishedMirror gloss, maximum depth of colourLasts well on granite; on marble and limestone it turns matt over time under precipitation
Honed, satinSmooth matt or with a soft sheenA universal option; traces of water and dust are barely noticeable
FlamedRough, with slightly fused grainUsed for granite; lightens the tone and hides minor defects
Bush-hammeredUniform pitted reliefExpressive on plinths and portals; requires an allowance in slab thickness
SawnMatt, with saw marksAn economical option for limestone, travertine, sandstone
Split-face (rock)Coarse natural reliefLarge thickness and mass; used to a limited extent in a ventilated system

Any impact or thermal treatment removes part of the material and creates microcracks in the surface layer, so the design thickness of the slab is taken with the chosen finish in mind. Combining two or three finishes of the same stone makes it possible to set off the plinth, string courses and surrounds without changing the material. More on the methods can be found in the article on stone processing.

How much does a natural stone ventilated facade weigh?

The mass of stone cladding is determined by the density of the stone and the thickness of the slab, and for common solutions usually lies in the range of roughly 50 to 110 kg/m², excluding the subframe. This is several times more than porcelain stoneware, and it is this figure that the calculation of the frame and anchors starts from.

The values are given for dry stone and serve as a guide; the actual density is taken from the test report for the batch. The weight of the subframe is added to the mass of the cladding, and for steel frames under stone it is noticeably higher than for typical aluminium ones.

Three practical conclusions follow from this load. First, the load-bearing capacity of the substrate for the anchor is verified by tests before design. Second, on walls of lightweight blocks the load is usually transferred to the floor slabs. Third, increasing the thickness to be on the safe side without calculation makes the facade heavier and the frame more expensive. The calculation procedure is described in the article on ventilated facade calculation.

Slab format and layout: what is decided before the stone is ordered

The layout of a stone facade is developed before the material is purchased, because slab dimensions, yield from the block and the amount of waste depend on it. The format cannot be changed once the batch has been sawn.

What is determined at this stage:

  • Slab module. Course heights are tied to the levels of floor slabs, sills and lintels; widths to the axes of openings and piers.
  • Maximum format. It is limited by the strength of the stone, the thickness, the wind load and the ability to lift and hang the slab safely.
  • Joint pattern. Continuous joints, staggered bond or a free layout in courses of varying height.
  • Direction of the pattern. With travertine and bedded limestones, cutting along and across the bedding gives a different look and different strength.
  • Shade matching. Slabs are sorted and distributed across the elevations so that transitions in shade are gradual.
  • Special pieces. Reveals, corner pieces, coping and sill slabs are ordered to the same drawings and from the same batch.

For critical areas — entrance groups, portals, columns — a dry lay is carried out at the works with the slabs numbered. They arrive at the facade in the order in which they were matched. Example schemes and typical mistakes are given in the article on stone layout on a facade.

Joints in stone cladding: open or closed

In ventilated systems with stone, the open joint is used more often: it provides additional air exchange in the cavity and needs no maintenance. A closed joint with sealant is used where a continuous appearance of the surface is important or where water ingress behind the cladding has to be limited.

The joint width is set by the design and is usually a few millimetres; it must accommodate thermal movement of the slabs and subframe, tolerances on stone dimensions and the fixing elements. With visible clips the joint is determined by the thickness of the lug; with concealed fixing it can be made narrower. An excessively thin joint on a large plane emphasises any deviation in slab dimensions, so narrow joints require calibrated stone.

If the joint is filled, sealants intended for natural stone are used. Ordinary compounds may release plasticisers that penetrate the pores and leave dark greasy bands along the joint which cannot be cleaned off. Before use, the sealant is tested on a sample of the same stone.

Along the lines of the building's movement joints, the cladding and subframe must be interrupted. In tall buildings, horizontal compensation joints are additionally provided floor by floor, so that the load from the upper slabs is not transferred to the lower ones.

Corners, reveals, plinth and parapet: details of a stone ventilated facade

The details of stone cladding are more complex than those of sheet materials: stone cannot be bent, and the exposed edge of a slab always remains visible. Decisions on them are therefore taken together with the architect at the layout stage.

  • External corners. The options are a joint with the exposed edge of one of the slabs, a 45° mitre, or a one-piece L-shaped element. A mitre looks neater, but the thin edge is vulnerable to chipping, especially in the lower zone.
  • Reveals of openings. Made of the same stone on their own fixings, or of metal in the colour of the frames; the depth of the reveal is determined by the insulation thickness and the projection of the subframe.
  • Sill and coping slabs. Made with a fall and a drip groove on the underside so that water does not run down the face.
  • Plinth. For the lower zone, stones with low water absorption and increased thickness are chosen; granite for cladding remains the main material. A gap is left between the stone and the paving.
  • Junctions with other materials. The joint with render, porcelain stoneware or GFRC is formed with a joint and a flashing; different materials do not bear on one another.

Three-dimensional elements are a separate matter: cornices, string courses, architraves. In solid stone they turn out heavy and expensive, so on many facades the planes are made of stone and the profiled elements of GFRC with colour and texture matched to the stone.

How stone is accepted before installation

A batch of stone is checked twice: at the works after sawing and on site during unloading. The aim is to make sure that the properties and geometry of the slabs correspond to those for which the fixing details were designed.

  1. Documents. Test reports with density, water absorption, compressive and flexural strength, frost resistance; for granites — information on the radioactivity class.
  2. Geometry. Length, width, thickness, difference of diagonals, flatness. Deviation in thickness is critical: the position of the kerf or hole relative to the face depends on it.
  3. Kerfs and holes. Depth, width, position, absence of chips around the fixing point.
  4. Defects. Hairline cracks, loose inclusions, cavities, filled areas. Slabs with cracks are not admitted to a ventilated system.
  5. Shade and pattern. Comparison with the approved reference sample and the range of permissible deviations agreed in advance.
  6. Marking. Correspondence of the slab number to the layout scheme.

Stone is a natural material, and variation in shade is inevitable; it is useful for the client to approve not one sample but three or four showing the limits of what is acceptable. This is especially important for stones with a pronounced pattern, such as travertine. Slabs are stored upright, on timber spacers, without contact with metal or soil, so that rust stains and dirt marks do not appear.

Care of a stone facade in use

Stone cladding does not require painting or periodic renewal, but it does need inspections and careful cleaning. The main tasks are to monitor the fixings and to prevent prolonged wetting of the stone.

At a scheduled inspection, the checks cover the position of the slabs in the plane, the condition of visible clips, the absence of new cracks and chips at fixing points, the cleanliness of the gap at the plinth and the performance of flashings and copings. Streaks and dark bands under cornices usually point to a faulty water drainage detail rather than to the properties of the stone; the cause must be removed, not just the trace.

The facade is cleaned with water at moderate pressure and neutral agents. Acidic compounds are inadmissible for marble, limestone, travertine and dolomite — they destroy the surface of carbonate stones. For porous stone, water-repellent impregnations are used, which reduce water absorption without closing the pores to vapour; their effect is limited in time, and the treatment is repeated as recommended by the manufacturer of the product. Types of impregnation and the application procedure are discussed in the article on protection of natural stone.

When purchasing stone, it is worth providing a small reserve of slabs from the same batch and keeping it on site. Matching a replacement by shade several years later from a different block is considerably more difficult.

FAQ

Which is better: a natural stone ventilated facade or a stone-look facade?

Natural stone gives authentic texture and durability but is heavier and needs a strong substructure. Stone-look porcelain stoneware or GFRC is lighter and cheaper to install; the choice depends on the architecture, budget and the wall's load-bearing capacity.

Which stone is suitable for a rainscreen facade?

Most often granite and basalt; also dense marble, limestone, dolomite, travertine and sandstone, provided the batch's water absorption, flexural strength and frost resistance are confirmed.

How thick should a stone slab be for a ventilated facade?

For granite the guideline is from 20–30 mm, for softer stones more. The exact thickness is set by calculation based on batch properties, slab format and wind load.

Can a natural stone mosaic be used on a facade?

Mosaic is usually bonded with adhesive on specific areas — plinths, inserts, portals. In a rainscreen system it is mounted on backing panels; the solution is worked out separately in the design.

What fixings are used for stone in a rainscreen facade?

Visible stainless steel kerf clips, concealed undercut anchors and dowel connections. The choice depends on stone type, slab thickness and the architect's requirements.

Which stone finish is best for a ventilated facade?

For most projects — honed, satin or flamed: they are matt, give no glare and age evenly. Polishing is chosen for granite; on marble and limestone the gloss is lost outdoors over time.

How much does a square metre of stone cladding weigh?

As a guide, for a 30 mm slab — about 80 kg/m² for granite and 65–75 kg/m² for travertine, limestone and sandstone, excluding the subframe. The exact value is calculated from the density in the test report for the specific batch.

Do the joints between stone slabs need to be filled with sealant?

In a ventilated system usually not: an open joint improves ventilation of the cavity and needs no upkeep. If the design provides for a closed joint, a sealant for natural stone tested on a sample is used, to avoid greasy stains along the edges.
