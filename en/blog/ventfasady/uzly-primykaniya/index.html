Rainscreen Facade Junctions with Windows and the Roof

Rainscreen facade junctions are the places where the cladding system meets windows, the roof, the plinth, building corners and other structures. This is where leaks, thermal bridges and visual defects most often occur, even though junctions account for a small share of the facade area.

A good junction performs three functions at once: it protects the insulation from water, keeps the cavity ventilated and maintains neat cladding geometry. Each cladding type (porcelain stoneware, profiled metal sheet, stone, glass fibre reinforced concrete) is detailed in its own way, but the principles are the same.

This article is for designers, site managers and technical supervisors. The general build-up of the systems is covered in Ventilated rainscreen facades.

Window reveals

Window reveals are the most common and most problematic rainscreen facade junction. The window sits in the plane of the wall, while the cladding is set out by the depth of the insulation, cavity and substructure; this "well" has to be closed around the whole opening.

  • The sill projects beyond the face of the cladding, with a drip, an outward fall and upturned ends; beneath it the insulation has waterproof protection.
  • Side reveals and head are made of polymer-coated galvanised steel, aluminium or cladding elements, with insulation inside so that no thermal bridge forms along the frame.
  • Fire protection: current Russian fire safety codes (SP) require the surrounds of openings in a rainscreen facade to prevent fire spreading into the cavity; reveals are usually made of steel and fire barriers are installed.
  • The joint between frame and reveal is sealed while allowing for thermal movement.

On facades with ornament, the reveal is complemented by a GFRC window surround, pediment or keystone; the element is fixed to the substructure or to the wall with its own brackets.

Reveal depth needs separate consideration. If the window is in the middle of the wall and the facade build-up is thick, the reveal becomes deep, which can be noticeable from the room as reduced daylight. Sometimes windows are moved closer to the outer face of the wall and projected on brackets into the insulation zone; this is decided at the design stage together with the frame fixing detail.

With natural stone cladding, reveals are often made from the same stone: side slabs are fixed on separate brackets, and the head slab gets additional restraint against falling, since it works in tension.

Parapet

The parapet is where the facade meets the roof. Two waterproofing systems join here, and mistakes show up immediately: leaks on upper floors, wet insulation, streaks on the cladding.

  • The top of the parapet is covered with a coping sloped towards the roof, with overhangs and drips on both sides.
  • The roof membrane is turned up the inner side of the parapet and secured under the coping.
  • The top of the ventilated cavity is left open for air to escape but protected from direct water entry.
  • If the parapet is crowned by a GFRC or stone cornice, its top surface is designed with a fall and a flashing, and its fixings are designed for wind suction, which is greatest at the top of the building.

If the roof has anchors for facade cradles or guardrails, their fixings pass through the parapet; these penetrations are also drawn in the detail and sealed. Otherwise, the waterproofing will have to be opened up to install a cradle on a finished roof.

Plinth

The plinth is the bottom junction of the facade, where the system meets the paving or the below-ground part of the building. Three things matter here:

  • The cavity air inlet is closed with a perforated profile against rodents and debris without blocking air movement.
  • Protection of the insulation from splashes and snow: a plinth flashing, a gap between cladding and ground level, moisture-resistant insulation in the lower zone.
  • Mechanical strength: plinth cladding takes the most knocks and loads during cleaning, so granite, basalt or reinforced panels are used here.

The plinth is often emphasised architecturally with rustication, darker stone or a projection, in which case the transition from plinth to wall also becomes a decorative band.

Corners

Building corners are zones of increased wind load: substructure design uses higher values for corner areas than for the main field. This leads to practical rules:

  • the spacing of brackets and cladding fixings near corners is reduced according to calculation;
  • external corners are finished with corner cladding pieces, a butt joint with a profile or a special corner piece in the same material;
  • for GFRC, one-piece corner panels are convenient: they close the corner without a visible joint and resist chipping better;
  • internal corners are designed with a gap for thermal movement.

Common mistakes

Mistakes that technical supervisors see on site most often:

  1. The sill does not project beyond the cladding or has no drip, so water runs down the facade and into the cavity.
  2. Reveals made of combustible materials or without fire barriers, which breaches fire safety requirements.
  3. Insulation stops short of the window frame, causing a thermal bridge and condensation on the inside of the reveal.
  4. The ventilated cavity is blocked at the plinth or parapet, so the insulation cannot dry out.
  5. The same bracket spacing across the whole facade without allowing for corner and top zones.
  6. Decorative elements are fixed only to the cladding rather than to the substructure or wall.
  7. Junctions are not drawn in the working documentation and are improvised by installers on site.

All of these problems are eliminated at the design stage: the working drawings must include details for each type of junction, specifying materials and fixings.

How is a ventilated facade detailed at balconies, loggias and canopies?

A balcony slab, an entrance canopy or an awning cuts through the facade plane, so the cladding, insulation and ventilated cavity have to be interrupted and closed again at this point. The general principle is the same as at a window: water is led away from the wall above the structure, air enters the cavity freely below it, and the insulation runs continuously around the perimeter.

Above the slab the cladding stops short of the finished surface, usually by a few centimetres, and a flashing is fitted that overlaps the slab waterproofing turned up the wall. If the cladding is taken right down to the screed, the bottom course will stand in water and snow and the air inlet will be blocked. Beneath the slab the top of the cavity is left open and covered with a trim that has a drip edge.

The cantilevered slab itself conducts heat strongly. Its edge and soffit are insulated and clad, or finished with a render layer; the choice depends on the design. More on such areas can be found in the article on thermal bridges in a ventilated facade. Fixings for balcony balustrades and glazing are taken to the load-bearing slab or wall rather than to the subframe, unless the structural calculation provides otherwise.

Junctions between a rainscreen facade and curtain walling, render systems and movement joints

On a single building a ventilated facade almost always sits next to other systems: stick curtain walling, rendered insulation in the courtyard or on loggias, or plinth cladding built by a different method. Each such junction is a boundary between two contractors and two sets of documentation, so it is drawn separately and it is agreed in advance who supplies the flashings and trims.

  • Curtain walling. A gap for thermal movement is left between the curtain wall mullion and the cladding and is closed with a cover strip; the insulation is brought up to the frame without a break, and the end of the cavity is closed with a steel element.
  • Render system. The planes of the two systems usually differ in projection, and the step is closed with a trim with a drip edge; the end of the ventilated cavity must not open towards the render layer.
  • Building movement joint. Rails and panels must not bridge the joint rigidly: the subframe is broken on both sides, and the joint is closed with an expansion profile or a cover strip fixed on one side only.

Expansion joints in the subframe itself are a separate matter. Long rails are joined with a gap, and the cladding joint should preferably coincide with it; otherwise a tile or panel ends up fixed to two profiles that move independently.

Penetrations through the cladding: downpipes, air conditioning units, signage

Any item fixed to the wall through the facade must bear on the structural substrate through its own bracket, and the opening in the cladding must keep a clearance around it. A panel or tile must not carry the weight of equipment: clips and concealed fixings are designed only for the self-weight of the cladding and for wind.

  • Brackets for downpipes, outdoor units, awnings and signs are installed before the cladding and marked on the installation drawings.
  • Ventilation grilles and sleeves are framed in steel so that air flow and moisture do not enter the cavity.
  • Lighting cables and low-voltage lines are run in non-combustible conduits without obstructing ventilation.
  • The insulation around the bracket is made good, and cuts in the membrane are taped.

If the list of facade-mounted equipment is not known when work starts, the design allows for reserve zones with a reinforced subframe, which costs less than dismantling the cladding after handover.

Facade-to-roof junctions: pitched roof, eaves overhang and accessible terrace

The parapet of a flat roof is not the only top detail. With a pitched roof the facade ends beneath the eaves overhang, and here it is important that air leaves the cavity freely and that the soffit does not seal it off. Usually a gap is left between the top of the cladding and the soffit, and the soffit itself is made ventilated, or the cavity is led into the roof void if the design so provides.

At an accessible terrace or podium the wall rises above a surface that people walk on and where snow accumulates. The waterproofing is turned up the wall above the expected level of snow and water, the bottom course of cladding is made of a material resistant to impact and de-icing agents, and the air inlet is protected by a flashing. Roof outlets and overflows are positioned so that, if they are blocked, water does not rise to the bottom of the cladding.

A separate case is where a wall meets the roof of a lower part of the building. Here the detail is built by the roofer and the flashing above it by the facade contractor, so the sequence of work and responsibility for watertightness are fixed before installation begins.

How do junction details differ for stone and GFRC?

Heavy, three-dimensional cladding changes the load-bearing part of the detail rather than its principle. Stone slabs and GFRC units have appreciable thickness and a projection of their own, so reveals, corners and crowning elements become independent components with their own fixings and calculations.

In GFRC facade panels the return into the reveal and the drip can be moulded in one piece with the face, which reduces the number of joints in the detail. For stone the layout matters more: joints on the reveal and on the facade must line up, and this is settled when the slabs are ordered.

What should the working drawings for junction details contain?

The minimum set is a sheet marking every type of junction on the facade elevations and a separate drawing for each type at a scale that shows thicknesses and fixings clearly. A detail shown only schematically turns into an improvised decision on site.

  1. A layout of details on the facades, referenced to grid lines and levels.
  2. Sections showing the build-up layer by layer: substrate, bracket with thermal break, insulation, membrane, cavity, cladding.
  3. Flashings and trims: material, metal thickness, developed profile, fixing spacing.
  4. Fire protection elements in accordance with the system documents.
  5. Sealants, tapes and gaskets with the type stated.
  6. Tolerances and gap sizes for thermal movement.

For complex cladding the details are supplemented by facade shop drawings, which show each unit with its cast-in inserts. The position and make-up of the barriers are checked against the requirements described in the article on fire barriers. When the design and the ventilated facade installation are handled by one contractor, questions about details are resolved before work starts on a section.

In what order are ventilated facade junction details installed?

Details are assembled from the wall outwards and from the bottom up, so that each successive element laps over the previous one in the direction of water flow. Breaking this order is a common reason why the sill flashing ends up fitted incorrectly and water from the reveal gets behind it.

  1. Setting out the openings and checking actual dimensions: deviations in walls and frames affect the developed profiles of trims.
  2. Brackets and fire protection elements around the opening.
  3. Insulation brought up to the frame, and membrane turned into the reveal.
  4. Rails and framing profiles.
  5. Sill flashing, then the side reveals and the head element.
  6. Cladding of the general wall area, cut to fit the surround.
  7. Sealing of joints and fitting of decorative elements.

Trims are ordered from actual site measurements taken after the windows are installed, not from design dimensions: a discrepancy even within the tolerance for the opening is noticeable on the finished facade.

How does the site inspector check details before the cladding closes them?

Details are accepted in stages while the layers are still accessible: once the cladding is on, the insulation at the frame or the joint between sill flashing and reveal can no longer be seen. A practical approach is to build the first opening, the first corner and the first length of parapet as a sample, approve it and check the rest against it.

  • The insulation is brought tight up to the frame, slab and parapet, with no voids.
  • Flashings slope outwards, have upturned ends and project beyond the cladding.
  • The ventilated cavity is open at the bottom and the top of each section.
  • Steel surrounds and barriers are in the positions shown in the design.
  • Trims are fixed at the spacing on the drawing, and joints are lapped in the direction of water flow.
  • Joints are sealed with the material specified in the design.

The results are recorded in concealed-works certificates with photographs. The general inspection procedure is described in the article on acceptance of facade works.

FAQ

How is a rainscreen facade joined to a window?

The opening is framed with steel or cladding reveals with insulation inside, a sill with a drip projecting beyond the cladding at the bottom, and fire protection of the cavity around the perimeter.

How do rainscreen details differ for porcelain stoneware and profiled metal sheet?

The principles are the same; the fixings and trims differ: porcelain stoneware uses clips and separate reveal pieces, profiled sheet uses flashings made of the same metal.

Where can I get rainscreen facade details in DWG?

Substructure manufacturers publish standard details. For a specific building, details are developed in the working documentation, taking the cladding and substrate into account.

Is a fire barrier needed around windows in a rainscreen facade?

Yes. Current Russian fire safety codes require openings to be protected against fire spreading into the cavity; the solution is built into the detail.

Why does a ventilated facade leak around windows?

Most often because of a short sill without a drip, a poor joint between sill and reveals, or missing seals between the frame and the surround.

At what design stage are junction details developed?

At the working documentation stage, before the subframe and cladding are ordered: the details determine the sizes of trims, the slab layout and the number of brackets. Key decisions on the position of windows and parapets are best agreed earlier, at the design stage.

Can an air conditioning unit or a sign be fixed to the cladding of a ventilated facade?

No. Equipment is fixed to the load-bearing wall through separate brackets that pass through the cladding with a clearance. Cladding fixings are not designed for additional load.

What clearance is left between the cladding and a balcony slab or roof?

The value is set by the design; it is usually a few centimetres above the finished surface, so that the bottom course does not stand in water and snow and the air inlet to the cavity stays open.
