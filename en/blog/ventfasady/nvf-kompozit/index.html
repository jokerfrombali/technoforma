Composite Cassette Ventilated Facade: Pros and Limitations

Composite facade cassettes are cladding elements folded from aluminium composite material (ACM): two thin aluminium sheets with a core of polymer or mineral filler. Cassettes are fixed to the ventilated facade substructure on hooks or rivets, form a flat plane with crisp joints and put almost no load on the wall.

The key selection criterion is the core type and the confirmed fire hazard class of the system: it determines where composite can be used at all. Composite cassettes are chosen for retail and office buildings, petrol stations, inserts and portals that need light cladding with a bright plain surface, where modelling and stone texture are not required.

Composite's place among other claddings is described in the Ventilated rainscreen facades section.

What composite panel is

A composite facade is made of sandwich panels: outer and inner aluminium sheets a fraction of a millimetre thick and a middle layer that provides stiffness and determines fire properties. The face sheet has a factory-applied polymer coating. Total panel thickness for facades is usually 3–4 mm.

Core typeCompositionFire properties
Polyethylene (PE)Unfilled polyethyleneCombustible material; use on facades is severely restricted
Fire-retardant (FR)Polymer with a high mineral filler contentReduced combustibility; permissibility is determined by system tests
Mineral (A2)Mainly mineral filler with a small share of binderClose to non-combustible; widest scope of application

Panels with different cores look the same, so the core type is checked by documents and markings, not by eye. Alucobond is a trade mark of one manufacturer that is commonly used as a generic name for ACM; the design specifies the particular product and its properties.

Fire safety limitations

It is fire safety limits that determine where composite is permissible. For rainscreen facades, requirements depend on the building's use, height, fire resistance degree and structural fire hazard class. The key point: it is not an individual material that is assessed but the facade system as a whole — cladding, insulation, membrane, substructure, fire barriers. The system's fire hazard class is confirmed by fire tests on a facade mock-up.

  • Panels with a combustible polyethylene core are effectively not used on facades of multi-storey and public buildings.
  • FR and A2 panels are used as part of systems that have passed tests and hold a technical certificate stating the scope of application.
  • Even with permissible cladding, fire barriers around openings and non-combustible insulation are mandatory.

For the client this means that composite quotes cannot be compared without checking the core class and system documents. The price difference between a polyethylene and a mineral-core panel is noticeable, and saving here may end in failed acceptance.

Cassettes

A cassette is a panel whose edges are folded at 90° to form a return; fixing elements are installed on the returns. It is made by routing a V-groove on the back and folding along it — this can be done in a workshop or on site, but quality is higher with factory fabrication.

  • Dimensions are limited by coil or sheet width and by stiffness: a large cassette without stiffeners may flex in the wind.
  • Types. Open (with a joint and a visible profile) and closed (hung on hooks with concealed fixing). Closed cassettes give a clean look but require a precise substructure.
  • Joints are usually open, 8–20 mm; their width is part of the facade architecture.

Advantages of cassettes: low weight, wide choice of colours, fast installation, and the possibility of complex cutting for logos and lettering. Limitations: a flat plane with no relief, susceptibility to dents, fading of the coating over time, and fire restrictions depending on the core class.

Installation

Composite facade installation follows the general rainscreen sequence: setting-out, brackets, insulation, wind barrier, rails, hanging the cassettes. Specifics:

  • the substructure is light, most often aluminium, with bracket spacing calculated for wind load, since the cladding weight is small;
  • cassettes are hung on runners or pins fixed to the rails, or riveted through the return;
  • thermal expansion must be accommodated — aluminium expands noticeably when heated, so fixing holes are slotted or sliding connections are used;
  • the protective film is removed immediately after installation: in the sun it bonds to the coating;
  • panel direction according to the arrow on the film must be respected — otherwise shade variation shows on the facade.

Maintainability is considered separately. A cassette on concealed fixing is harder to remove than a clip-fixed tile: if one panel is damaged, adjacent ones sometimes have to be taken down. So in risk zones — at entrances, on the ground floor, near car parks — cassettes are designed for individual replacement or replaced with a more impact-resistant material. A reserve of panels from the same batch should be ordered from the start: paint from different batches may differ in shade.

High installation speed is one of the main arguments for composite, but it does not remove the need to check fire barriers and insulation fixing.

Which panel coating should be chosen so that the facade does not fade?

How long a ventilated facade of composite cassettes keeps its colour is determined by the paint coating of the face sheet, not by the aluminium itself. Coatings with increased UV resistance are used for external cladding; simpler formulations are intended for interiors and advertising structures.

CoatingWhere it is usedWhat to consider
Fluoropolymer-based (PVDF and equivalents)Facades, including south-facing and high-riseLoses colour and gloss more slowly; the main choice for external cladding
PolyesterInteriors, signage, temporary structuresCheaper, but fades and chalks faster in the sun
Metallic and pearlescentAccent planes, portalsThe shade depends on the rolling direction, so panels are oriented the same way
Anodised or brushed surface, wood and stone imitationsIndividual insertsA full-size sample is required: the pattern repeats

Bright and saturated tones, especially reds and blues, change more noticeably over time than light and grey ones. If the facade is built around a corporate colour, the sample should be viewed outdoors, in daylight and from different angles. The warranty terms for the coating are stated by the panel manufacturer and differ between product ranges.

The whole requirement for panels of one colour is ordered as a single batch with a reserve. Sheets bought later may differ in shade even with the same colour code, and on a smooth plane this difference is clearly visible.

Cutting and cassette geometry: what determines the flatness of the facade

A flat plane on a composite facade is achieved when the cassette size, the depth of the return edge and the number of fixing points are matched to the wind load and thermal movement. An oversized cassette without stiffening produces a ripple that is especially noticeable on dark and glossy surfaces in raking light.

  • Dimensions. The cassette width is limited by the sheet width less the return edges, the length by stiffness and ease of installation. Large elements are reinforced with stiffening ribs bonded to the rear face.
  • Thermal movement. Aluminium lengthens by approximately 2.4 mm per metre when heated by 100 °C. For a long cassette on the sunny side this amounts to millimetres that the joint and the sliding connection have to accommodate.
  • Corners and radii. An external corner is made with a one-piece folded corner cassette or a joint between two elements. Curved surfaces are produced by rolling, but the minimum radius depends on the panel thickness and core type.
  • Routing. The groove depth must leave the face sheet and a thin layer of core intact. Cutting too deep weakens the fold line, and over time cracks appear in the coating along it.
  • Drainage. Holes are left in the bottom return of closed cassettes so that condensate and rainwater do not collect inside.

Cutting layouts are prepared from the working drawings after a geodetic survey of the walls. Making cassettes to dimensions taken from the architectural design, without a measured survey, almost always ends in adjustment on site, and cutting in situ compromises edge protection and joint accuracy.

Which buildings is a ventilated facade of composite cassettes suitable for?

Composite cassettes are appropriate on buildings that need a light, smooth envelope with precise geometry and where the fire requirements allow a system with the chosen core to be used. The taller the building and the more people it holds, the narrower the range of acceptable solutions.

Building typeRole of compositeWhat to look out for
Shopping centres, car showrooms, filling stationsMain cladding, portals, fascias for signageCorporate colours, protection of the lowest tier against impact
Office buildingsPlanes between glazing bands, surrounds, canopiesFire hazard class of the system, interface with curtain walling
Industrial and warehouse buildingsAdministrative blocks, entrance zonesSimple layout, minimum number of sizes
Residential buildingsIndividual inserts, balcony guarding, plant floorsAdmissibility by height and by the system documents
RefurbishmentNew envelope on old walls with low load-bearing capacityAnchor tests, survey of the substrate

The choice of cladding for commercial property is discussed in detail in the article business centre facade. On buildings with developed architecture, metal is usually kept for plant zones, canopies and surrounds, while the main character is created by GFRC facade panels, stone or porcelain stoneware.

When materials are combined on one facade, it is decided in advance how claddings of different thickness and weight meet. The frame for light cassettes and for heavy panels is calculated separately, and the junction line is aligned with a rustication groove, a string course or a change of plane.

Which documents should be requested from the supplier and contractor?

For composite cladding the set of documents matters more than for most other materials: it is what confirms that the panel delivered to site has the declared core and that the system is permitted for use on the building in question. The paperwork needs to be checked before the batch is paid for, not when the facade is handed over.

  1. Technical approval for the facade system with its scope of application: building type, height, permitted cladding and insulation. What it should contain is covered in the article on the technical approval for a ventilated facade system.
  2. A report on the fire tests of the system with exactly the panel brand being purchased.
  3. Certificates for the panel stating the combustibility group and core type, and the batch data sheet.
  4. The book of technical details with opening surround details and the fire barrier layout.
  5. Marking on the protective film and sheet edge that matches the documents.

On site the actual details are compared with the book. Particular attention is paid to window surrounds and floor-by-floor barriers: they must be steel and continuous, with no gaps at corners. The requirements for them are described in the article fire barriers in ventilated facades.

If the contractor proposes replacing the panel with an equivalent of another brand, the scope of application of the system is checked again. The same core class designation from different manufacturers does not mean that the system has been tested with that panel.

Typical installation mistakes and how to avoid them

Most defects of a composite facade show up in the very first warm season: planes bow, joints open up, gaps appear at corners. The cause is almost always connections that do not allow the metal to move freely, or departures from the cutting layouts.

  • Rigid fixing at every point. A cassette is fixed in one zone and the remaining fixings are made sliding. With rivets set without clearance in round holes, the sheet bulges when heated.
  • Joints narrower than designed. Saving on joint width for the sake of a tight pattern leaves the cassettes no room to expand.
  • Cutting and reworking on the scaffolding. Edges without factory finishing, inaccurate angles, swarf under the film.
  • Panels facing different directions. On metallics an inverted cassette looks like an element of a different colour.
  • Replacing steel barriers with aluminium ones or omitting surrounds. This is a direct breach of the conditions under which the system is permitted for use.
  • Fixing equipment to the cassettes. Signs, cameras and light fittings are mounted on their own brackets passing through the cladding to the wall or frame.

Control is easiest section by section: before the cassettes are hung, the frame, insulation and barriers are accepted; afterwards the joints, the plane and removal of the film are checked. Turnkey works, including design and frame, are described on the page installation of ventilated facades.

How to maintain a composite facade and what to do about damage

Maintenance of a composite facade comes down to regular washing and inspection of the fixings; repair of the cassette itself generally means replacing it. A dent in a thin aluminium sheet cannot be straightened without trace, and local touching-up is noticeable on a smooth surface.

  • Washing. Usually once every year or two, more often in a polluted urban environment. Soft brushes and neutral detergents are used; solvents and abrasives damage the coating.
  • Inspection. The hooks and rivets, the condition of joints, sills and parapet cappings, and the absence of cassette vibration in wind are checked.
  • Replacement. A new cassette is made to the cutting layout from a reserve sheet of the same batch. For this reason the as-built documentation and the remaining panels are kept for the whole service life.
  • Lowest tier. Bump rails are installed at car parks and entrances, or the metal is replaced with a more resistant cladding.

The general inspection schedule for ventilated systems is given in the article maintenance of a ventilated facade. For composite, coating checks are added to it: chalking, loss of gloss or delamination at the edges is recorded and reported to the supplier within the warranty period.

FAQ

What is Alucobond on a facade?

It is a common name for aluminium composite panels, taken from one manufacturer's trade mark. They are folded into cassettes for a ventilated rainscreen facade.

Can composite cassettes be used on a residential building?

Only as part of a system with a confirmed fire hazard class and an FR or A2 core, taking into account the building's height and use. Polyethylene cores are effectively not used on such buildings.

How can an A2 panel be told apart from a polyethylene one?

Not visually: by certificates, test reports and markings on the edge or film. The documents for the specific batch must be checked.

Which lasts longer: composite or GFRC?

Composite coating fades over time and dents are possible; GFRC resists UV and mechanical impact but is heavier. The service life of both depends on material and installation quality.

How thick are composite facade cassettes?

Usually made from 3–4 mm panel; cassette depth is determined by the return, which is set by the substructure design.

How much does composite cassette cladding weigh?

The panel itself, 3–4 mm thick, usually weighs around 5–8 kg per square metre depending on the core: a mineral core is heavier than a polymer one. With return edges, ribs and fixings the load is somewhat higher but remains one of the lowest among facade claddings.

Can composite cassettes be made directly on the construction site?

Technically this is possible, but routing and folding accuracy is higher in a workshop. On site it is harder to maintain groove depth and angles, and dust and swarf damage the coating. Cassettes are usually made in a workshop to cutting layouts after the walls have been surveyed.

How do composite cassettes differ from sheet metal cassettes?

For the same stiffness composite is lighter and gives a flatter plane in large formats. Cassettes made of steel or aluminium sheet contain no polymer layer, so the question of cladding combustibility is simpler for them, but large elements require greater thickness or ribs.
