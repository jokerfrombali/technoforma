Ventilated Facade Insulation: Materials and Thickness

Insulation behind a ventilated rainscreen facade is made with non-combustible mineral wool boards fixed to the wall with insulation dowels and, where required, covered with a wind barrier membrane. A ventilated cavity is left over the insulation to remove moisture, so the board stays dry and keeps its thermal performance.

The main selection criteria are the density and intended use of the board (for rainscreen facades, not for rendered facades or roofs) and a thickness confirmed by thermal calculation for the specific region and wall. This article is useful for designers, clients and technical supervisors of multi-storey and public buildings, and for private home owners choosing an insulated rainscreen facade.

The general logic of the systems is covered in the Ventilated rainscreen facades section.

Mineral wool

Rainscreen facades use mineral wool boards based on stone (basalt) wool. Glass wool is used less often, and only in systems where it is specified.

ParameterGuide value for rainscreen facadesWhy it matters
CombustibilityNon-combustible (NG under Russian classification)Fire safety requirement for the insulation layer in a rainscreen facade
DensitySingle-layer system: around 80–90 kg/m³; two-layer: lighter inner layer, denser outer layerRigidity, resistance to wind erosion

Values depend on the manufacturer and product; exact figures are in the product documentation. Insulation for a rainscreen facade must be marked as suitable for ventilated facades and be on the list given in the system's technical approval: substituting a similar board of another brand breaks the system's conditions of use.

A two-layer scheme, with a dense outer layer over a lighter inner one, saves money and fills wall irregularities better, but requires staggered joints and adds labour.

For a private low-rise house the requirements are less strict, but even here stone wool is the sensible choice for a rainscreen facade: a cavity with moving air and combustible insulation is a poor combination.

Calculating thickness

The thickness of facade insulation is set by thermal calculation according to the Russian code for thermal protection of buildings (SP 50.13330). The calculation takes into account:

  • the climate zone and heating degree-days;
  • the building's use and the required thermal resistance;
  • the material and thickness of the load-bearing wall;
  • thermal bridges such as brackets, dowels and window reveals. Every bracket is a cold bridge, so the actual thermal resistance of a wall with a rainscreen facade is lower than the insulation thickness alone suggests.

In practice, residential buildings in central Russia end up with around 100–150 mm, more in northern regions and less in southern ones. This is a guide, not a rule: thickness can vary on one building depending on the wall.

The overall depth of a rainscreen facade, from the wall to the face of the cladding, is the sum of the insulation, the ventilated cavity (usually at least 40 mm; the exact value per the design and the system manual), the substructure depth and the cladding thickness. For GFRC and stone, the depth of the fixing details is added. This parameter affects reveals, window junctions and facade alignment.

The logic of the calculation: the higher the required thermal resistance and the lower the wall's own resistance, the thicker the insulation. A brick wall and aerated concrete masonry give different mineral wool thicknesses under the same requirements; a cast-in-place reinforced concrete wall insulates almost nothing by itself and needs the greatest thickness. Cold bridges from brackets are accounted for with a thermal uniformity factor, which depends on the number and type of brackets per square metre. That is why thermally broken brackets and an optimised substructure spacing sometimes achieve more than an extra 20–30 mm of insulation.

Boards come in standard thicknesses, so the calculated value is rounded up to the nearest thickness or a combination of two layers.

Fixing with dowels

Each insulation board is fixed with insulation dowels, either plastic or with a metal expansion pin. The number and layout are set by the design and the system's technical manual.

  • A typical layout is at least 2 dowels per board for the inner layer and more for the outer one; more often at building corners and in zones of higher wind load.
  • Dowel length is the insulation thickness plus the anchorage depth in the substrate, which depends on the wall material.
  • The dowel plate should sit flush with the board without crushing it.
  • Dowels with a metal pin are cold bridges; models with a thermal head are used.

In hollow and lightweight substrates, dowels with an extended expansion zone are used; the type is confirmed by on-site pull-out tests.

Sequence of insulation work on a section:

  1. Setting out and installing the substructure brackets.
  2. Cutting the insulation boards around the brackets tightly, without gaps.
  3. Installing the inner-layer boards staggered, with tight joints.
  4. The outer layer with joints offset from the inner layer.
  5. The membrane, if specified, and final installation of dowels.
  6. Sign-off with a hidden works certificate before the rails are installed.

Wind barrier

A wind barrier protects the insulation from fibres being blown out and from cooling by the airflow in the ventilated cavity. It is a vapour-permeable membrane that lets vapour out of the insulation but stops wind and water droplets.

  • The membrane is laid over the insulation with overlaps and fixed with the insulation dowels.
  • Some systems do not use it: boards with a laminated facing or dense water-repellent boards that can work without a membrane are used instead. This must be stated in the system documents.
  • The membrane's combustibility affects the fire performance of the system.

The decision "with or without a membrane" is made not on site but when the system is chosen. If a membrane is specified, it cannot be omitted to save money; if the system allows working without it, the insulation must be exactly the product for which this has been confirmed. On tall buildings, where air speed in the cavity is higher, wind barrier requirements are usually stricter.

A separate issue is protecting the insulation during construction. If the cladding is installed late, exposed mineral wool loses its properties in rain and wind, and the membrane degrades under UV. So insulating in sections that are immediately covered by cladding is not a formality but a condition of durability.

Mistakes

Typical insulation mistakes that are discovered after the cladding is installed:

  • gaps between boards and around brackets, which act as cold bridges;
  • too few dowels, so boards pull away from the wall and air circulates behind them;
  • wet insulation left under the cladding;
  • insulation of the wrong brand or density;
  • a blocked ventilated cavity, where cladding or rails press on the insulation and moisture cannot escape;
  • no fire barriers around openings;
  • holes through the membrane not taped after the brackets were installed.

All these defects are hidden, so insulation is signed off with a certificate before the rails are installed. Later they can be detected by thermal imaging during the heating season: cold bridges and areas with detached boards show up as zones of higher outer surface temperature. But fixing them on a finished facade means removing the cladding, so it is cheaper to inspect each section before it is covered.

Another common mistake is skimping on insulating window reveals and junctions: that is exactly where damp patches and freezing appear after installation. Reveal details are made according to the system manual, and fire barrier boxes around openings from steel of the specified thickness.

Facade insulation on different wall types: what changes

The wall material determines three things: what insulation thickness the calculation gives, which fixings hold the boards and brackets, and how the wall behaves with respect to moisture. The same board on in-situ concrete and on aerated concrete works under different conditions.

SubstratePoints relevant to insulationFixings
In-situ reinforced concreteHigh thermal conductivity, so all thermal protection comes from the insulation; slab edges are covered without breaksExpansion anchors and dowels, high load-bearing capacity
Solid brickUneven masonry, joints; the condition needs checkingDowels with a standard expansion zone, pull-out tests
Hollow brick and clay blocksThin webs, voidsExtended expansion zone or chemical anchors, drilling without hammer action
Aerated concrete and other cellular blocksHigher inherent thermal resistance, lower strength; it is important not to trap moisture in the wallSpecial dowels for cellular concrete; brackets are often fixed into the floor slabs

On framed buildings with block infill the load-bearing brackets are often supported on the edges of the floor slabs, with only restraint brackets placed in the infill. The insulation is still laid as a continuous layer. The arrangement is decided once the anchor tests on site have been carried out.

For walls of cellular blocks the moisture regime is checked separately. The blocks arrive on site with elevated moisture content and release it over the first seasons; mineral wool and a ventilated cavity do not hinder this, so a rainscreen system suits such walls well. Undried masonry must not, however, be covered with vapour-tight materials.

On old brick walls the thickness and condition of the masonry may vary over the height of the building, so fixings are tested in several areas rather than at a single point.

How do subframe brackets reduce thermal protection?

Each bracket passes through the insulation and acts as a point thermal bridge. The calculated resistance of the insulation layer taken on its own is always higher than the actual resistance of the wall with the subframe, and this difference is accounted for in the thermal calculation by a homogeneity factor.

The losses depend on the bracket material, its cross-section, the number per square metre and the presence of a thermal break. Aluminium conducts heat considerably better than steel, and stainless steel conducts less than galvanised steel. The thicker the insulation, the more noticeable the relative contribution of the brackets.

  • A thermal isolating pad, of paronite or polymer, is placed under the bracket foot.
  • Bracket spacing is taken from the calculation, without an excessive margin.
  • The board is slit for the bracket rather than hacked out, and the gap around it is filled with insulation.
  • Where thermal requirements are high, stainless steel brackets are considered.

The phenomenon is examined in detail in the article on thermal bridges in a facade, and the design of the load-bearing components in the article on the ventilated facade subframe.

In practice this means that insulation thickness cannot be set solely from the resistance of the board itself: once brackets, dowels and reveals are taken into account, the required thickness turns out to be greater. Point losses through the disc dowels are smaller, but there are many dowels, so their number is not increased beyond the pattern specified by the system.

With heavy cladding, such as stone or large GFRC panels, there are more brackets and they are more massive than for porcelain stoneware. This is allowed for in advance: the thermal and structural calculations are carried out together rather than one after the other.

Why insulation in a ventilated facade must stay dry

Damp mineral wool loses part of its insulating properties, so the whole construction of a rainscreen facade is built around the removal of moisture. Vapour from the room passes through the wall and the insulation and is carried away by the air flow in the cavity between insulation and cladding.

For the scheme to work, the vapour permeability of the layers must increase from inside to outside, and the cavity must remain continuous over the full height. Its width is set by the system documentation and is usually a matter of several centimetres. Air enters at the plinth and leaves below the parapet, and also through open cladding joints where these are provided.

  • Do not block the cavity with insulation, expanding foam or construction debris.
  • Do not use films of low vapour permeability as wind protection.
  • Protect the inlet and outlet openings with perforated profiles without reducing their cross-section.
  • Do not leave installed insulation exposed to rain for long periods.

Brief wetting by driving rain through open cladding joints is normal for the system: the moisture runs down the wind-protection layer or the outer surface of the boards and evaporates. Prolonged wetting is what is dangerous: leaks from the parapet, uncontrolled run-off from window sills, a wet plinth. The condition of the insulation therefore depends largely on the quality of the junctions and not only on the board itself.

Particular care is taken that the cavity does not narrow at wall projections, at reveals and above openings. These are the places where it is most often blocked when the cladding is being fitted, and the air flow over the height of the facade is then interrupted.

Insulating difficult areas: reveals, plinth, parapet

The main heat losses and complaints about cold spots arise not on the plain wall but where the insulation layer is interrupted or changes thickness. These areas are drawn in the design as separate details.

  • Window reveals. The insulation is lapped onto the window frame; the position of the window within the wall thickness affects how far this is possible.
  • Plinth. Materials with low water absorption are used in the splash and snow zone; the transition from plinth insulation to mineral wool is made without a gap.
  • Parapet. It is insulated on three sides, joining the facade layer to the roof layer.
  • Slab edges and balcony slabs. They are covered with a continuous layer; projecting slabs are the strongest linear bridge.
  • External corners. The boards are bonded with toothed interlocking and the number of dowels is increased.

Behind architectural elements such as cornices, string courses and GFRC surrounds the insulation is likewise not interrupted: the decorative elements are hung on their own brackets over the thermal insulation. Standard solutions are collected in the article on junction details of a ventilated facade.

Special attention is needed where services pass through and where wall-mounted equipment is fixed: brackets for air-conditioning units, luminaires, signage and rainwater pipes. Embedded fixings for these are installed before insulation and the boards are trimmed to fit; breaking through the finished layer after cladding means leaving unfilled voids in it.

In fire barriers and opening surrounds the insulation used is the one stated in the system documents, as a rule denser stone wool boards.

All of these details can conveniently be checked by one criterion: the insulation line on the drawing must be continuous around the whole envelope of the building, from plinth to parapet.

How are ventilated facade insulation works accepted?

Insulation is concealed work: once the rails and cladding are installed it can be checked only indirectly. Control is therefore exercised at every step, before, during and after installation.

  1. Incoming inspection: the grade and thickness of the boards correspond to the design, the packaging is intact, the boards are dry and the documents are attached.
  2. Storage: under cover or in the factory wrapping, on pallets, with no contact with the ground.
  3. In-process control: tightness of joints, staggering of joints, number and seating depth of dowels, filling around brackets.
  4. Inspection of the section with a certificate and photographic record before it is closed.
  5. Thermal imaging during the heating season, which reveals omissions and gaps on the finished facade.

The dowel disc must sit flush with the surface of the board: an overtightened dowel crushes the wool, while an undertightened one does not press it against the wall. This simple sign is a good indicator of the overall standard of workmanship.

The certificate records the grade and thickness of the boards, the number of layers, the type and number of dowels per square metre and the presence of a membrane. It is accompanied by the documents for the batch of materials actually installed. If several crews are working on the project, acceptance is carried out for each separately, which makes it easier to trace the source of a defect.

Thermal imaging is carried out with a stable temperature difference between inside and outside and without direct sun on the facade, otherwise the picture is distorted. The thermograms are attached to the as-built documentation as confirmation of the quality of the concealed layers.

Facade insulation when refurbishing an existing building

On a building in use, insulation is preceded by a survey of the walls. Its results determine whether the system can be fixed to the existing substrate and what thickness of thermal insulation is needed to bring the building up to current requirements.

Old render, delaminated finishes and weak areas are removed, and cracks and damaged masonry are repaired. A damp wall is dried out before it is covered and the cause of the dampness is eliminated: roof leaks, faulty rainwater goods, absence of plinth waterproofing. The layer thickness is also checked against geometry: increasing the projection of the facade alters the depth of the reveals, the roof overhangs, the sills and the junctions with neighbouring buildings.

The procedure for such works is described in the article on facade refurbishment. The effect of the new layer is assessed together with the windows and building services; this is covered in the article on facade energy efficiency.

On residential and office buildings the works are often carried out without moving the occupants out. A rainscreen system is convenient here: the processes are dry, nothing changes inside the rooms, and the insulation is installed section by section without opening up the whole facade at once. Working hours, protection of windows and entrances and the arrangements for storing materials are agreed in advance.

If the building has been insulated before, the old layer is assessed separately: thermal insulation that has slumped, become damp or is combustible is removed rather than covered by the new system.

What to prepare for an insulation calculation

To select the insulation and estimate the scope of works, data on the building and the future cladding are needed:

  • the region of construction and the use of the building;
  • the construction of the external walls layer by layer, with thicknesses;
  • facade drawings, wall area net of openings, the number and sizes of windows;
  • the chosen facade system and type of cladding;
  • for existing buildings, the survey results.

TECHNOFORMA carries out facade insulation as part of the full cycle: design, selection of materials in accordance with the system documents, installation and handover of concealed works.

These data are used to determine the thickness and number of layers, the consumption of boards and dowels, the need for a membrane and the amount of work at junctions. If the building is in a region with cold winters or strong winds, the calculation is supplemented by checking the details for cold spots.

The insulation is selected together with the subframe and cladding: the bracket projection depends on the layer thickness, and the number of brackets on the weight of the cladding. Choosing these elements separately usually leads to recalculation.

FAQ

Can a rainscreen facade be built without insulation?

Yes, on unheated buildings or if the wall is already insulated. The substructure and cladding remain; only the bracket projection changes.

What insulation is needed for a rainscreen facade?

Non-combustible stone wool boards intended for ventilated facades and listed in the documents for the chosen system.

How thick should facade insulation be?

As determined by thermal calculation for the region and wall; for residential buildings in central Russia the guide is 100–150 mm.

Is permission needed to insulate the facade of an apartment building in Russia?

The facade is common property of the owners, so a decision of the general meeting and agreement with the management company are needed; in some cities, approval of the architectural appearance as well. Check the procedure with the local administration.

How much does facade insulation cost?

It depends on the area, insulation thickness and brand, access to the facade and the scope of work. TECHNOFORMA determines the cost after a site inspection.

Is a vapour barrier needed on the room side when insulating behind a ventilated facade?

As a rule, no: in a wall of concrete, brick or blocks the vapour escapes outwards through the vapour-permeable insulation into the ventilated cavity. The need for additional layers is checked by a moisture regime calculation.

Can insulation be installed in winter?

Yes, it is a dry process. What matters is that the boards and the wall are dry, free of ice and snow, and that the installed layer is not left exposed for long.

What should be done if the insulation gets wet during installation?

Water-repellent facade boards become wet on the surface in a brief shower and dry out within the ventilated construction. Boards that are soaked through or have lost their shape are replaced.
