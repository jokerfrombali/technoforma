Porcelain stoneware cladding in a ventilated rainscreen facade means fixing porcelain slabs to a metal substructure with an air cavity in front of the insulation. Porcelain stoneware is dense fired ceramic with very low water absorption, so it withstands frost and rain, and a flat slab of standard format makes installation predictable.

The main selection criterion is the combination of format, slab thickness and fixing method: it determines the look of the facade and the weight and cost of the substructure. Porcelain stoneware is chosen for residential complexes, office and public buildings that need a flat, maintainable facade without complex modelling, and for plinths and individual surfaces combined with GFRC or stone.

General principles of rainscreen systems are covered in Ventilated rainscreen facades.

Properties of porcelain stoneware

Porcelain stoneware is made by pressing a mix of clays, feldspars and quartz and then firing it at high temperature. The result is a sintered material without open porosity. For a facade this means several practical properties.

PropertyWhat it means for a rainscreen
Water absorption a fraction of a per centHigh frost resistance, no efflorescence or darkening from moisture
Non-combustible materialThe cladding does not support combustion; requirements for the system as a whole still apply
Brittle under impact and bendingNeeds the right clips, pads and a format limited by calculation
Stable geometryEven joints, simple layout, quick slab replacement
Wide choice of designsImitation stone, concrete, wood, plain colours

Special facade ranges are produced, including porcelain stoneware imitating travertine, marble and slate. Sizes and patterns vary between manufacturers, so the collection is chosen together with the layout. Exact properties (water absorption, flexural strength, frost resistance) are taken from the data sheet of the specific range.

The installation sequence for a porcelain stoneware rainscreen is standard: setting out and survey, brackets, insulation with anchors, a wind barrier where needed, rails, cladding. Components (brackets, rails, clips, rivets or screws, paronite pads) must belong to one system with supporting documents. A ventilated porcelain stoneware plinth is also possible, usually with a reinforced fixing pattern and protection against mechanical damage.

Rainscreen facades with porcelain stoneware slabs but no insulation layer are used on unheated buildings, when re-cladding over an already insulated wall, and on individual areas. Cost estimates list such work separately as 'with insulation' and 'without insulation'; under the Russian state estimate standards (GESN), adhesive-fixed ceramic tiling and a porcelain stoneware rainscreen are priced under different rates. Adhesive-fixed porcelain stoneware is possible on low-rise private houses but is practically never used in multi-storey construction.

Types of facade porcelain stoneware by surface finish:

SurfaceHow it is madeOn a facade

StructuredRelief in the press mouldImitates stone, slate, wood; dirt collects in the relief

By colouring, there is through-body porcelain stoneware (the colour is the same through the whole thickness, so chips are barely visible) and digitally printed surfaces, which give a realistic stone pattern but a chip reveals a body of a different colour. For plinths and areas exposed to impact, the first option is more practical.

Visible and concealed fixing

Porcelain stoneware slabs are fixed in two main ways.

  • Visible fixing. The slab rests on clips, steel holders with tabs that grip the edges of adjacent slabs. The tabs are visible in the horizontal joints and are often painted to match the cladding. This is the most common and affordable option, and a slab is easy to replace.
  • Concealed fixing. Holes for undercut anchors are drilled in the back of the slab, or a kerf is cut in its edge; sub-frames attached to the slab are hung on horizontal profiles. A second option is bonding to the rails with dedicated facade adhesive systems. The face has no visible metal, but requirements for accuracy and for test-verified systems are higher.

The concealed method is more expensive and needs thicker slabs and a more accurate substructure, but it gives clean facades in architecture with large formats. The visible method is easier to maintain. It is important that the chosen option is described in the system's technical details manual rather than improvised on site.

Comparison of fixing methods:

ParameterVisible (clips)Concealed (anchors, kerf, adhesive)

Clips are fixed with stainless steel rivets or screws; damping pads are placed under the slab at bearing points to stop rattling and protect the edge from chipping.

Tile format

Slab format affects appearance, layout and substructure design.

  • 600×600 and 600×1200 mm are the most common rainscreen formats, usually 10–12 mm thick. They fit easily into a standard rail spacing.
  • Large formats (1200×1200, 1200×2400 mm and more) are impressive but heavier, harder to install at height, and require concealed fixing and additional wind load calculation.
  • Narrow slabs and battens suit linear architecture, often laid horizontally.

Layout starts by coordinating joints with openings and corners: cutting slabs around windows increases waste and the number of details. Wind pressure also matters for format: on upper floors and near building corners, the permissible slab size with the same fixing is smaller.

How to calculate the amount of porcelain stoneware: the cladding area is taken from the facade elevations minus openings, then an allowance for cutting and breakage is added. As a guide, the allowance is 5–10% on simple facades with a layout coordinated with openings and 10–15% or more on complex ones with many corners and cuts. The exact quantity comes from the layout in the design, which shows the number of whole and cut slabs for each wall. It is worth keeping a reserve of slabs from the same batch on site for repairs: a few years later it may be impossible to find the same shade.

When choosing a collection, check water absorption and frost resistance in the range data sheet, calibration and dimensional tolerances, flexural strength for the chosen thickness, shade consistency within one batch (the batch number is on the packaging), and the availability of trims and factory cutting.

Large-format slabs are delivered and lifted in factory packaging, upright, using vacuum lifters.

Substructure

The porcelain stoneware substructure is one of the most established in the industry. It consists of load-bearing and restraint brackets, vertical rails (T- and L-profiles), clips or concealed fixing profiles, fasteners and pads.

  • Material: aluminium alloys, galvanised or stainless steel; the choice depends on building height, fire safety requirements and budget.
  • Rail spacing is set by the slab format: each slab must bear on at least two rails.
  • Bracket spacing and anchor type follow the calculation for dead load and wind, with pull-out tests of anchors in the substrate.
  • Rails are connected to brackets through sliding connections to accommodate thermal expansion.

TECHNOFORMA's own installation team carries out the whole cycle (substructure, insulation, cladding), so responsibility for compatibility of the details is not split between contractors.

Installation of a porcelain stoneware rainscreen step by step:

  1. Survey of the facade and setting out bracket positions according to the design.
  2. Pull-out tests of anchors in the substrate, adjusting spacing and anchor type if needed.
  3. Installing brackets with thermal break pads.
  4. Installing insulation with insulation anchors and, where needed, a breather membrane.
  5. Installing rails and aligning the plane; fire barriers and opening surrounds according to the system manual.
  6. Hanging slabs from the bottom up, checking that joints are level.
  7. Installing trims: sills, reveals, parapet cappings, corner elements.

Mistakes to avoid: a single rail under the joint of two slabs instead of each slab bearing on two rails; rigid fixing of rails without sliding connections; mixing metals that cause galvanic corrosion (galvanised steel and aluminium without isolation); filling joints with sealant where the system specifies an open joint; no ventilation gap at the plinth and below the parapet.

Cost

Published prices are usually quoted per square metre turnkey, but the real estimate is made up of several items, and their proportions vary greatly from project to project.

  • porcelain stoneware: collection, format, thickness, cutting waste;
  • substructure: material, projection, bracket spacing, fixing type;
  • insulation, membrane, anchors;
  • labour: height, number of storeys, facade complexity, number of openings and corners;
  • scaffolding or cradles, design, anchor testing.

At TECHNOFORMA, the cost is determined after inspecting the site and developing a design solution. A ready price per metre without the input data does not reflect the real cost.

For a preliminary estimate it helps to understand the cost structure: the share of the porcelain stoneware itself in the cost of a finished metre is usually smaller than it seems; a large part goes to the substructure, insulation and labour. So switching to a cheaper collection saves less than optimising the layout and the substructure projection.

Where a porcelain stoneware ventilated facade makes sense, and where another cladding is better

A porcelain stoneware ventilated facade makes sense where the elevation consists mainly of flat planes and the client values a predictable budget and easy replacement of damaged tiles. Where the architecture relies on cornices, pilasters and relief, porcelain stoneware remains a background material and the modelling is provided by other products.

By building type the picture is usually as follows:

  • Residential complexes. Tiles cover the main wall field from the first floor upwards; ground floors and entrance areas are more often made of materials with better impact resistance. The requirements for such buildings are covered in the article on the facade of a multi-storey residential building.
  • Business centres and retail buildings. Large formats with concealed fixing and neutral concrete-look or stone-look surfaces are in demand; porcelain stoneware is combined with glazing and metal.
  • Schools, clinics, administrative buildings. Non-combustible cladding, resistance to washing and graffiti removal, and a long period without renewing the finish are valued.
  • Hotels and boutique residences. Porcelain stoneware is used on courtyard and side elevations, and on the main ones together with decorative elements that set the character of the building.
  • Private houses. A ventilated system is appropriate for large wall areas and contemporary architecture; on a small house of complex shape the share of cutting and trim elements rises sharply.

The limitations are a matter of geometry rather than material. Curved walls, arched openings and small-radius bay windows require the tiles to be cut into narrow strips, and the facade loses its main advantage, a large even module. In such places tiles are usually replaced with moulded elements that follow the curve without being broken into small pieces.

Porcelain stoneware, fibre cement, stone or GFRC: which to choose for a ventilated facade?

Porcelain stoneware sits between lightweight sheet claddings and natural stone: it is heavier than fibre cement and metal cassettes but lighter than a stone slab, it does not burn and absorbs almost no water. The choice between materials comes down to three questions: whether the facade needs modelling, how long it is expected to last without repair, and what load the substrate can carry.

CriterionPorcelain stonewareFibre cement panelsNatural stoneGFRC panels
Cladding weightMedium, usually around 20–30 kg/m² at 10–12 mm thicknessLowHigh, depends on stone type and thicknessMedium or higher, depends on shape and frame
ShapeFlat tileFlat sheetSlab, profiled parts to orderAny: plane, profile, relief
PatternRepeatable, factory-madePaint or textureNatural, uniqueSet by the mould and finish
Water absorptionVery lowNoticeable, edge protection mattersFrom low to high depending on the stoneLow, depends on the mix
RepairTile replacementSheet replacementSlab replacement or restorationLocal repair or element replacement

In practice the materials complement rather than compete with each other. A typical scheme for a residential complex is porcelain stoneware on the ordinary planes and glass fibre reinforced concrete panels on accent areas, string courses and surrounds. The plinth and entrance areas are often made of granite or another dense stone that copes better with impact and de-icing agents.

If the choice is specifically between tile and a natural material, it is worth comparing them by service life, appearance at close range and requirements for the subframe; a separate review, stone or porcelain stoneware on a facade, is devoted to this. In short, porcelain stoneware wins on uniformity and subframe cost, while stone wins on depth of texture and the standing of the building.

Corners, reveals, parapet and plinth: how junctions are detailed

The appearance of a porcelain stoneware facade is determined less by the ordinary tiles than by the details: external corners, window reveals, the parapet and the bottom edge of the cladding. This is where edges, cuts and the quality of the installers' work are visible.

External and internal corners

A corner is made in one of three ways: butt-jointed with the exposed edge of one tile, with edges mitred at 45°, or with a metal corner profile. A mitre gives the neatest appearance, but the thin edge is vulnerable to chipping, so it is used with caution on the lower floors. A profile is more robust but adds an extra line to the facade, and its colour is agreed in advance.

Reveals and sills

Reveals are closed either with strips of the same porcelain stoneware or with painted galvanised steel. A steel surround also acts as a fire barrier box around the opening where the system provides for it. The sill must project beyond the cladding plane and have a drip edge, otherwise streaks quickly appear under the windows.

Parapet and plinth

At the top the cavity is closed with a parapet capping sloped towards the roof and with a slot for air to escape. At the bottom an inlet gap is left, protected from birds and rodents by a perforated profile. The cladding is not taken down to the paving: splashes, snow and de-icing agents shorten the life of the fixings. Typical solutions are collected in the article on ventilated facade junction details.

What input data is needed to design and price a porcelain stoneware facade?

To price a ventilated facade with porcelain stoneware, architectural drawings, information about the walls and an idea of the desired tile format are sufficient. The more complete the input data, the fewer assumptions in the first estimate and the closer it is to the final bill of quantities.

  1. Elevations, plans and sections of the building with levels, opening sizes and reference to grid lines.
  2. Material and thickness of the external walls, type of structural frame, position of the floor slabs; the anchors and subframe layout depend on this.
  3. Location: wind zone, design winter temperature, seismicity.
  4. Required insulation thickness, or the thermal calculation if it has already been done.
  5. Preferences for tile format, surface and colour, and for the fixing method, visible or concealed.
  6. List of junctions: curtain walling, balconies, entrance areas, roof, signage, lighting.
  7. Construction stage and programme: readiness of the shell, availability of scaffolding, handover dates.

For existing buildings a measured survey and an inspection of the walls are added. Actual deviations of the wall plane often amount to several centimetres and are taken up by bracket adjustment; if the deviations exceed the adjustment range, the bracket size or the system stand-off is changed.

On the basis of this data the tile layout, subframe layout, material schedule and details are developed. At TECHNOFORMA Group the design is handled by an in-house department of 12 design engineers and 2 architects, and installation is carried out by the company's own installation division. The scope of the service is described on the page porcelain stoneware facade cladding.

Which mistakes are built in at the design stage?

Most problems with a porcelain stoneware facade arise not on the scaffolding but on paper: the format, layout and frame arrangement are chosen from a picture without being checked against calculation and the real geometry of the building. Correcting such decisions after the tiles have been purchased is expensive, so they are worth catching early.

  • Format chosen without a wind check. A large tile that is acceptable in the middle of a wall may fail the calculation in corner zones and on upper floors. There the rail spacing, the number of fixing points or the format itself is changed, and this must be visible in the design.
  • Layout not tied to the openings. If the tile module does not match the window spacing, narrow make-up pieces appear at every opening. They are held less securely in the clips, chip more often and spoil the joint pattern.
  • Building movement joints ignored. Rails and tiles must not bridge such a joint: the frame is interrupted and a compensation gap is formed in the cladding.
  • Substrate tolerances ignored. The system stand-off is set for an ideal wall, and the actual deviations eat into the ventilated cavity or prevent insulation of the required thickness from being fitted.
  • Dark tiles on south elevations without checking the connections. A dark surface heats up more, thermal movement of the frame increases, and rigid connections begin to push the tiles out.
  • Calibres and batches mixed in the schedule. Tiles of the same nominal size but different calibre differ by fractions of a millimetre, and on a long wall the joint drifts noticeably.

A separate group of mistakes is connected with substituting materials during procurement. A tile of a different thickness, a clip from another manufacturer or insulation of a different density cannot be dropped into a finished design without checking: the system has been tested and calculated in a specific configuration. Any substitution is agreed with the designer and recorded as a change to the documentation.

Quality control: what to check when accepting a porcelain stoneware ventilated facade

Acceptance of a ventilated facade has two parts: concealed works are checked as they are completed, and the cladding after the scaffolding is struck or section by section. Most defects are built in before the tiles are hung, so checking the face surface alone is not enough.

StageWhat is checkedHow it is confirmed
Anchors and bracketsAnchor type and depth, spacing, presence of thermal break padsPull-out test report, concealed works certificate
InsulationTight fit of the slabs, staggered joints, number of fixingsConcealed works certificate, photographic record
RailsPlumb, plane, sliding connections, gaps between profilesAs-built drawing
Fire barriers and opening surroundsCompliance with the system's book of technical detailsConcealed works certificate
CladdingJoint width and straightness, lipping between tiles, chips, shadeVisual inspection, measurements

When inspecting the cladding, several signs deserve attention. Tiles must not rattle when tapped; rattling indicates missing pads or loosely tightened clips. Joints must be uniform over the full height, with no accumulated error towards the upper floors. Shade variation between sections points to mixed batches.

The as-built documentation is checked separately: data sheets and certificates for the tiles, profiles and fixings, site logs and certificates. The general procedure is described in the article on how acceptance of facade works is carried out.

In use: how to maintain the facade and replace damaged tiles

A porcelain stoneware ventilated facade does not need its finish renewed regularly: maintenance comes down to periodic inspections, washing of soiled areas and replacement of individual tiles. The service life of the system as a whole is determined not by the tile but by the subframe and fixings.

  • Inspections. They are usually carried out once a year and after strong winds: the inspector looks for displaced tiles, cracks, bent clips, damaged sills and parapet cappings.
  • Washing. Matt surfaces are washed with water at moderate pressure and neutral detergents. Abrasives and acidic products are not used without a trial on an inconspicuous area, especially on glazed and polished tiles.
  • Graffiti and dirt near ground level. The dense surface allows paint to be removed with special removers; on structured tiles this is more difficult.
  • Tile replacement. With visible fixing the clips are bent back or removed and a new tile from the reserve stock is fitted. With concealed fixing the procedure depends on the system and may require adjacent elements to be removed.

The most common reason for call-outs in the first years is not wear but mechanical damage to the lowest tier and the consequences of installing third-party equipment. Air conditioners, signs and light fittings must not be fixed to the tiles or rails: separate brackets taken back to the wall are provided for them. This rule should be written into the building's operating manual and communicated to the management company.

How should the programme and sequence of works be planned?

The programme for a ventilated facade consists of three blocks: design, supply of materials and installation section by section. The duration of each block depends on the project, but the sequence is the same, and it is useful to understand it before the contract is signed.

  1. Survey and design. Geodetic survey, anchor tests, layout, details, schedules. Until there is a layout, ordering tiles is risky: the quantity and the share of cutting are unknown.
  2. Ordering tiles and frame. Stock collections are available quickly, while non-standard formats, colours and factory-cut tiles are made to order by the manufacturer. The whole requirement, including the reserve, is best taken from a single batch.
  3. Site preparation. Scaffolding, hoists or cradles, storage areas. Tiles are stored in factory packaging on a level base, under cover.
  4. Installation of frame and insulation. This proceeds section by section from the top down or from the bottom up, depending on the means of access. In the same period the insulation is covered with a membrane or the cladding.
  5. Hanging the tiles and trim elements. Sills, reveals and the parapet are installed together with the cladding rather than left to the end of the works.

The programme is affected most by adjacent trades. Windows must be installed before the reveals are clad, roof junctions completed before the parapet cappings are fitted, and service penetrations through the wall agreed before the rails are installed. If these works run late, the facade is left with open areas and the installers have to return to sections already completed.

Weather restricts installation less than with rendered systems, but in strong wind work with large tiles at height is stopped. This time allowance is built into the programme in advance, especially for high-rise blocks and exposed sites.

FAQ

What is porcelain stoneware rainscreen cladding?

A ventilated system: porcelain stoneware slabs are fixed with clips or concealed fixings to a metal substructure, leaving a ventilated cavity between them and the insulation.

Which porcelain stoneware is suitable for a ventilated facade?

Facade ranges, usually from 10 mm thick, with low water absorption and proven frost resistance. Properties are taken from the data sheet of the specific range.

Can a porcelain stoneware rainscreen be built without insulation?

Yes, for unheated buildings or over an already insulated wall. The substructure and cladding stay the same; only the bracket projection changes.

How does visible fixing of porcelain stoneware differ from concealed fixing?

With visible fixing the clips show in the joints, and installation is simpler and cheaper. With concealed fixing the face has no metal, but thicker slabs and an accurate substructure are needed.

How much does porcelain stoneware rainscreen cladding cost?

The cost depends on the collection, format, substructure, height and complexity of the facade. At TECHNOFORMA it is determined after inspecting the site.

What allowance of porcelain stoneware should be ordered for a rainscreen?

Roughly 5–10% on simple facades and 10–15% or more on complex ones with many corners and cuts. The exact quantity comes from the layout in the design; it is worth keeping some slabs from the same batch for future repairs.

Can a porcelain stoneware ventilated facade be installed in winter?

Yes, assembling the subframe and hanging the tiles are dry processes and do not depend on temperature in the way rendering does. The limitations relate to the safety of work at height in wind and icing, and to the fact that insulation must not be left exposed to precipitation.

What is the service life of a porcelain stoneware ventilated facade?

The tiles themselves, if the technology is followed, last for decades and do not change colour. The service life of the facade is determined by the subframe, fixings and insulation, so it depends on the frame materials and the quality of installation; a reference figure is given in the documents for the specific system.

Can porcelain stoneware and GFRC decorative elements be combined on one subframe?

Yes, this is a common solution: tiles cover the planes, while GFRC cornices, string courses and surrounds are fixed to the same system through their own connections. The layout and loads are then calculated together at the design stage.
