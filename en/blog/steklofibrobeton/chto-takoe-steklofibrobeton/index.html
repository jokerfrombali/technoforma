What Is GFRC and Where Is It Used

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) is a fine-grained concrete reinforced throughout its volume with alkali-resistant glass fibre. The fibre takes the tensile stresses, so GFRC can be used to make thin-walled, lightweight elements of complex shape: facade panels, cornices, columns, window surrounds, decoration and small architectural forms.

The main selection criterion is not the material itself but the quality of production: the mix design, the fibre type, the forming method and control at every stage. These determine strength, frost resistance and the geometry of the elements.

The material is chosen by architects and developers who want an expressive facade without heavy stone or cast-in-place concrete, and by private homeowners who want classical decoration that can stand up to the weather. More about the material in GFRC: material and technology.

Definition and composition in brief

According to TECHNOFORMA Group, its GFRC consists of four components: glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement and water. The cement-sand matrix provides compressive strength and durability, while the glass fibre carries bending and tension and resists cracking. Admixtures such as plasticisers and frost-resistance modifiers are added to improve the properties.

To understand where GFRC is used, it helps to know a few related terms. The facade is the outer face of a building: walls, decoration, opening surrounds, cornices. A ventilated rainscreen facade, in simple terms, is cladding fixed to a metal substructure at a distance from the wall; an air gap remains between the cladding and the insulation, through which moisture escapes. GFRC panels are often installed on exactly this type of substructure.

Fibre-reinforced concrete in the broad sense is any concrete with dispersed reinforcement: steel, basalt, polymer or glass fibre. GFRC is one type, in which alkali-resistant glass fibre is used.

How GFRC differs from ordinary concrete

Ordinary heavyweight concrete performs well in compression but poorly in tension, so it is reinforced with steel bars. The rebar needs a protective concrete cover, which makes structural walls thick and elements heavy.

In GFRC the reinforcement is distributed throughout the volume, and there is no steel rebar or cover layer. Hence the main differences:

ParameterOrdinary concreteGFRC
ReinforcementSteel rebar, meshAlkali-resistant glass fibre throughout the volume
Wall thickness of the elementUsually several centimetres or moreTypically 10–20 mm for facade elements
Element weightHighSeveral times lower for the same area
Shape complexityLimited by formwork and reinforcementAlmost any: relief, curvature, fine detail
Reinforcement corrosionPossible if the cover is damagedNone — there is no steel

Exact thickness and weight values depend on the mix design and the element's construction; they are stated in the product data sheet and test reports.

Where GFRC is used

GFRC is chosen where freedom of form, low weight and weather resistance must be combined. The thin wall reduces the weight of each element, so GFRC on facades is primarily used for cladding elements that do not overload the wall and substructure. In photos of finished buildings it is hard to tell from stone or architectural concrete: the surface can be smooth, textured or imitate a natural finish. The main applications are below.

Facades

The main use of GFRC is external building cladding. It is used to make:

  • facade panels, including large-format and curved ones;
  • cornices, string courses, mouldings, parapets;
  • window and door surrounds — architraves, pediments, keystones;
  • columns, pilasters, capitals, balusters;
  • plinth and entrance cladding.

The elements are fixed to a ventilated rainscreen facade substructure or to cast-in anchors. According to the company, its GFRC is classed as non-combustible (NG under the Russian classification), which matters for multi-storey residential and public buildings, where the applicable fire safety codes restrict combustible cladding materials.

Interiors

Indoors, GFRC is used for large decorative elements where plaster is not strong enough and stone is too heavy: columns and pilasters in halls, lobby wall cladding, fireplace surrounds, relief wall panels, window sills and worktops. The material is not affected by moisture, so it suits swimming pools, spa areas and entrance halls with temperature swings. The surface can be left as a concrete finish, coloured integrally or coated with protective treatments.

Landscape and small architectural elements

Small architectural forms are landscaping and landscape architecture elements: planters and pots, benches, railings, balustrades, fountains, pavilions, decorative steles. They need resistance to water and frost, and low weight that makes delivery and installation possible without heavy machinery.

In architecture the facade and the landscaping are designed as a whole, and GFRC helps keep a single style: a terrace balustrade, planters at the entrance and a facade cornice can all be made of the same material in the same colour.

Limitations of the material

GFRC has limitations worth knowing in advance:

  • Not a load-bearing material. GFRC elements are cladding and decoration; they do not carry loads from the building structure.
  • Dependence on technology. An incorrect mix, ordinary (non-alkali-resistant) glass fibre or poor curing reduce strength over time. Quality is not visible straight away, so the manufacturer needs to be checked.
  • Demands on fixing details. Thin-walled panels are sensitive to errors in the design of the substructure and movement joints.
  • Not natural stone. GFRC can imitate the texture of travertine, limestone or sandstone, but it is concrete, not a natural material. Travertine is a sedimentary rock with characteristic pores; if the client wants a genuine natural structure, natural stone is the choice.
  • Mould costs. Unique elements need a custom mould, and in small runs its cost noticeably affects the price per element.

How to choose a manufacturer

When choosing a GFRC manufacturer, check:

  1. In-house production and mould shop. If the models and moulds are made in-house, geometry and lead times are easier to control.
  2. Quality control. Ask at which stages the mix and the elements are checked, and whether there are test reports on samples for strength and frost resistance.
  3. Design department. Panel layout, fixing details and substructure design should be done by engineers, not by eye.
  4. Installation. A manufacturer with its own installation teams is responsible for the whole result, without shifting blame between contractors.
  5. Completed projects. Ask for addresses and the chance to see the facades in person.
  6. Capacity. For a residential complex volumes run to thousands of square metres, and the manufacturer must deliver them on time.

How GFRC is made: spraying and casting

Glass fibre reinforced concrete is produced in two main ways: by spraying the slurry on to a mould together with chopped fibre, or by casting a ready-mixed compound (premix) with vibration. The method determines the fibre content, its orientation and, ultimately, the flexural strength of the product.

In the spray process the roving, a strand of glass filaments, is chopped in the gun itself and reaches the mould at the same time as the cement and sand slurry. The fibres lie in the plane of the skin and their share is usually higher than in a cast mix, so sprayed shells perform better in bending. Large panels, cornices and column casings are made this way. In casting, short fibre is added in the mixer, and the finished mass is poured into the mould and compacted. The method suits small three-dimensional parts and products with two visible faces: balusters, planters, small ornament.

After forming, the product is cured in humid conditions, demoulded, surface-treated where required and cured again until it reaches release strength. A disturbed curing regime is one of the common causes of hairline cracks and colour variation, so this stage is controlled as strictly as the batching of components.

The choice of method is not a matter of factory preference. For thin-walled elements of large area, where flexural strength is decisive, spraying is preferable; for small three-dimensional ornament with a complex rear side, casting is more rational. On a single project both methods are normally used in parallel.

Batch quality is not judged by eye. Test boards are formed alongside the products and later tested in bending; on finished elements the skin thickness, geometry and position of the embedded parts are measured. Both methods and the control points are covered in detail in the article on GFRC production technology.

Which GFRC properties matter for a facade

Six properties are significant for facade cladding: density, flexural strength, compressive strength, frost resistance, water absorption and combustibility class. The values in the table are reference figures usually quoted for the material; actual figures depend on the mix design and forming method and are confirmed by the test reports of the particular manufacturer.

PropertyIndicative rangeWhat it affects
DensityAbout 1,700–2,250 kg/m³Product weight and load on the subframe
Flexural tensile strengthAs a rule 15–30 MPa for sprayed material, noticeably lower for castPermissible panel size and fixing spacing
Compressive strengthUsually 40–70 MPaResistance to impact and local loads at fixings
Frost resistanceAs a rule F150–F300Service life in climates with frequent freeze-thaw cycles
Water absorptionDepends on the mix, reduced by water-repellent treatmentEfflorescence, soiling, resistance to freezing
CombustibilityNon-combustible (NG class)Use on high-rise and public buildings

The fibre type is examined separately. Ordinary glass degrades over time in a cement environment, so alkali-resistant fibre containing zirconium dioxide is used. If a manufacturer cannot name the roving grade and show the documents for it, the remaining figures in the data sheet mean little.

Stability of properties over time also matters. The flexural strength of young material is higher than that of aged material, so fixings are designed not on peak values but on long-term values with a margin. A full review of the figures is given in the article on the properties of GFRC.

How a GFRC product is built and what holds it in place

A GFRC product is not a solid block but a shell, as a rule 10–20 mm thick, stiffened on the rear side with ribs or a steel frame. It transfers its self-weight and wind load to the building through embedded parts and brackets.

Three structural arrangements are found:

  • Shell with stiffening ribs. The ribs are formed from the same material along the perimeter and across the field of the product. The arrangement suits small and medium ornament: window surrounds, string courses, pediments over windows.
  • Shell on a steel frame. The thin skin is connected to a frame of galvanised or stainless steel through flexible anchors that allow thermal movement. Large-format GFRC panels are made this way.
  • Three-dimensional element in two halves. Columns and balusters are assembled from half-shells around a load-bearing rod or post.

On the wall the products are fixed either to the subframe of a ventilated facade or to anchors and embedded parts directly in the load-bearing substrate. In both cases the fixings must be protected against corrosion and the connection must allow adjustment in three directions, otherwise the joint width cannot be maintained.

Joints between elements are a working part of the structure, not a defect. Concrete expands when heated and wetted and shrinks when it dries, so products are never butted tight: the gap is left open or filled with an elastic sealant over a backer rod. How the cladding works as part of a rainscreen system is described in the article on ventilated facades clad in GFRC.

How does GFRC differ from stone, polyurethane and gypsum?

The material occupies a niche between natural stone and lightweight polymer ornament: in durability and fire performance it is closer to stone, in weight and freedom of form closer to polymers. This comparison helps to show what GFRC is among other facade solutions.

MaterialWeightComplex modellingCombustibilityWhere it is justified
GFRCLow, owing to the thin skinPractically any, defined by the mouldNon-combustibleOrnament and panels on buildings of any height
Natural stoneHighLimited by machining and costNon-combustiblePlinths, entrance areas, flat cladding
PolyurethaneVery lowGood, stock profilesCombustibleSmall ornament on low-rise houses
Coated expanded polystyreneVery lowSimple profilesCombustibleBudget ornament where there are no fire restrictions
GypsumMediumExcellent detailNon-combustibleInteriors and areas protected from moisture

Each solution has its own field of use. Polymer ornament wins on price and speed on low-rise houses, but on buildings with fire restrictions it is, as a rule, not applicable. Stone is irreplaceable where a natural surface and high abrasion resistance are needed, yet a complex profile in stone requires costly machining and reinforced fixings.

In practice the materials are combined: a stone plinth that takes impact and splashes, with GFRC ornament and panels above. The criteria for choosing between the two non-combustible options are set out in the comparison of GFRC and natural stone.

From sketch to installation: how the material reaches the facade

Work with GFRC begins not in the factory but in the design office: products are not taken from stock, they are made for a specific building. The usual sequence is as follows:

  1. Site survey or receipt of the architectural drawings, recording the actual deviations of the walls.
  2. Development of the layout: division of the facade into products, joints, standard sizes, unification.
  3. Calculation of fixings and issue of working drawings for products and connections.
  4. Manufacture of models and moulds, approval of colour and texture samples.
  5. Casting, curing, acceptance, marking and packing.
  6. Delivery to site in line with the installation sequence, and installation.

The most expensive mistakes are built in at the second and third steps: a poor division increases the number of moulds, and unrecorded wall deviations force products to be trimmed on site. It therefore makes sense to keep facade design and production in the same hands, or at least in constant contact.

For a preliminary estimate it is enough to have dimensioned elevations and sections, information on the wall material and number of storeys, and the wishes regarding colour and texture. The earlier the manufacturer joins the project, the more scope there is to reduce the number of standard sizes without affecting the appearance of the building.

Which buildings is GFRC suitable for

The material is used on buildings of any purpose and height, but the tasks it solves differ. In mass housing, repeatability and delivery rate matter; on one-off projects, the accuracy with which the architect's modelling is reproduced.

  • Residential complexes. Window surrounds, string courses, cornices and panels are cast in large runs from a limited set of moulds; the ornament is hung on the subframe together with the standard cladding.
  • Business centres. Large-format panels, fins and three-dimensional elements with precise geometry and even colour are in demand.
  • Hotels and resort properties. Resistance to humid and salt-laden air is important here, as is the ability to produce arches, ornament and colonnades without heavy stone.
  • Public buildings. Non-combustibility and durability allow the ornament to be used on universities, theatres and administrative buildings.
  • Private houses. Low weight removes the need to strengthen walls and foundations for columns, cornices and balustrades.
  • Refurbishment. Lost ornament is recreated from surviving fragments without overloading old walls.

In seismic regions and on high-rise buildings the low weight of the cladding is particularly valuable: it reduces the inertial loads on the fixings and the structural frame.

Common misconceptions about GFRC

Several persistent myths have grown up around the material and get in the way of a balanced decision.

  • ‘It is still concrete, so it is heavy.’ The mass of a square metre of shell is several times lower than that of a reinforced concrete panel; a noticeable share of the weight often comes from the frame and subframe.
  • ‘A thin skin means a fragile one.’ Dispersed reinforcement restrains crack growth, and the product withstands transport and installation loads. Sharp arrises and corners are vulnerable and are protected by packaging.
  • ‘It can be mixed on site.’ Stable properties come only from accurate batching, dedicated equipment and controlled curing in the workshop.
  • ‘The product needs no maintenance.’ The facade lasts for decades, but periodic inspection of joints and fixings and renewal of the water-repellent treatment are planned in advance.
  • ‘All manufacturers make it the same way.’ The spread in fibre content, skin thickness and quality of embedded parts is wide, so it is samples, data sheets and completed projects that are compared, not price lists.

A sober view of the material is simple: it is an industrial product whose quality is determined by the design, the mix and the production culture. Where these three conditions are met, a GFRC facade behaves predictably; where savings are made on them, problems appear in the very first winters as cracks at the embedded parts, efflorescence and opening joints.

FAQ

What does GFRC stand for?

GFRC stands for glass fibre reinforced concrete: a fine-grained concrete reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre. It is used to make thin-walled facade panels, decoration and small architectural forms.

What is fibre-reinforced concrete and how does it differ from GFRC?

Fibre-reinforced concrete is the general name for concretes with dispersed reinforcement: steel, basalt, polymer or glass fibre. GFRC is a specific case that uses alkali-resistant glass fibre; it is mainly used for cladding and decoration.

What is a ventilated rainscreen facade in simple terms?

It is cladding fixed to a metal substructure at a distance from the wall. An air gap remains between the cladding and the insulation, through which moisture is removed.

What does a ventilated rainscreen facade consist of?

A system of brackets and rails, insulation and cladding (GFRC panels, porcelain stoneware, natural stone) installed with a ventilated cavity.

What is travertine and can GFRC replace it?

Travertine is a sedimentary rock with a characteristic porous texture, used for facade and interior cladding. GFRC can imitate its texture and colour but remains concrete; if a natural structure is required, natural travertine is chosen.

What is shell limestone (coquina)?

Shell limestone is a porous limestone made up of shells and shell fragments. It is a soft rock used for cladding and masonry; TECHNOFORMA Group supplies and processes shell limestone along with other soft stones.

Does GFRC burn?

No. The material consists of cement, sand and glass fibre and is classed as non-combustible, which is why it is used on high-rise and public buildings. The fire performance of the facade as a whole also depends on the insulation, the membrane and the subframe.

How thick are GFRC products?

The skin of facade products is usually 10–20 mm; stiffness is provided by ribs or a steel frame on the rear side. The exact thickness is set by calculation and stated in the working drawings.

Can GFRC products be installed in winter?

Hanging on a subframe is a dry process, so it is also carried out at sub-zero temperatures. The restrictions concern sealants, bonded anchors and pointing compounds: for these the temperature range specified by the manufacturer is observed.
