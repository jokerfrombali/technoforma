GFRC Properties: Strength, Density, Frost Resistance

The properties of glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) are described by several indicators: flexural and compressive strength, density, water absorption, frost resistance and combustibility. Flexural strength is what sets GFRC apart from ordinary concrete: it comes from alkali-resistant glass fibre, which is why element walls can be thin.

Specific values depend on the mix design, the forming method and the age of the samples, so manufacturers should be compared on test reports rather than advertising figures. According to TECHNOFORMA Group, its GFRC is non-combustible (class NG under the Russian classification) and has high frost resistance, further improved by admixtures.

This article is for designers, technical supervision and clients who need to know which figures to ask for. A general overview is in GFRC: material and technology.

Key performance figures

Below are indicative ranges typical for the industry. Values depend on the mix design and technology; exact data are in the product data sheet and the test report.

The indicators are interrelated. A dense matrix with a low water-cement ratio gives high compressive strength, low water absorption and good frost resistance at the same time. Fibre content and orientation have the greatest influence on flexural strength and on how the element behaves after the first crack. So when comparing manufacturers, look at a set of figures from one test report rather than a single favourable number.

Four groups of factors have the strongest effect on the final properties: the mix design (cement grade and content, water-cement ratio, polymer admixtures, sand fraction), fibre content and length, the forming method and the curing conditions — the moisture regime during the first day, when the matrix gains strength and bond with the fibre develops. An element that dries out in the first hours can have noticeably lower flexural strength than properly cured samples of the same mix.

Flexural and compressive strength

GFRC strength is described by two groups of indicators. Compressive strength is governed by the cement matrix and is close to that of high-grade fine-grained concretes. For cladding it is rarely critical: a panel carries no vertical load from the building.

Flexural strength is the key figure for facade elements. A panel is subject to wind load, its own weight between fixing points and thermal movement. For GFRC two values are distinguished: the limit of proportionality (the stress up to which the material behaves elastically, without cracks) and the modulus of rupture (the failure stress). The latter is provided by the fibres, which hold the crack together and prevent brittle failure.

Age also matters: GFRC flexural strength can decrease over the years, so calculations use long-term values with safety factors rather than initial ones. This is addressed by alkali-resistant fibre, admixtures and a correct mix design.

How flexural strength is measured. Strip specimens are cut from a control board formed together with the production batch by the same method and tested in bending, usually in a four-point arrangement. The load–deflection curve gives the limit of proportionality (first crack) and the modulus of rupture. For sprayed material, specimens are tested in two directions and loaded on both the face and the back side, because fibre orientation and variation through the thickness make a difference.

What this means for a project:

  • wall thickness and stiffening ribs are selected by calculation from long-term flexural strength and the wind load for the specific location and height;
  • fixing spacing and panel size are linked: the longer the span between supports, the higher the bending stresses;
  • working stresses are kept below the limit of proportionality so that no cracks appear in service; the margin up to the modulus of rupture is a reserve for overloads;
  • large flat panels are often stiffened with a steel frame on flexible anchors that do not restrain thermal and moisture movement of the skin.

Frost resistance and water absorption

The frost resistance of GFRC is its ability to withstand repeated freezing and thawing when saturated with water without losing strength or surface damage. It is directly linked to water absorption: the denser the matrix and the fewer open pores, the less water enters the material and the lower the internal ice pressure.

According to TECHNOFORMA Group, its GFRC has high frost resistance, improved with admixtures. In practice resistance is increased by:

  • a low water-cement ratio and plasticisers;
  • polymer and air-entraining admixtures;
  • water-repellent treatment of the face;
  • correct details that prevent water standing on horizontal elements (drips, falls on cornices).

Trade names of admixtures and products for winter concreting that turn up in search results have nothing to do with the frost resistance of a finished element: a frost resistance grade is confirmed only by testing specimens.

Besides frost resistance, durability is affected by shrinkage and moisture movement: the cement matrix changes size as it dries and gets wet, and in thin-walled elements this is more noticeable than in mass concrete. If a panel is rigidly restrained around its perimeter, this movement creates stresses and cracks. So fixings are designed with sliding supports, and panel sizes and joint widths allow for movement.

Typical mistakes that make a material frost-resistant on paper fail on the facade:

  • horizontal surfaces without falls, where water stands and freezes;
  • no drip grooves on projecting elements, so water runs onto the soffit and the back face;
  • open cavities and ends where snow packs in;
  • joint sealant not designed for the movement, so water gets behind the cladding through tears;
  • de-icing salts at plinths and steps; for these zones frost resistance is tested in a salt solution, not only in water.

Fire performance

GFRC consists of mineral components — cement, sand, glass fibre and water — so according to the company it belongs to combustibility class NG (non-combustible). This is an important difference from polyurethane, polymer and expanded polystyrene mouldings.

For multi-storey residential buildings, public buildings and hotels, requirements for the combustibility of cladding and rainscreen systems are set by the applicable fire safety codes (in Russia, sets of rules SP). Non-combustible cladding makes these requirements easier to meet, but the fire safety of the facade as a whole is determined by the entire system: insulation, substructure, wind barrier and fire breaks. So the system must be assessed, not just the panels.

When heated, GFRC does not melt, produce burning droplets or support combustion. Under prolonged high-temperature exposure, like any cementitious material, it gradually loses strength and the surface may crack. For facades this means that GFRC elements do not spread fire across the cladding, but their fixings and substructure must also meet the design requirements.

How to verify declared properties

Declared properties are a promise; documents and tests confirm them. What to request from the manufacturer:

  1. Test reports for flexural and compressive strength, density, water absorption and frost resistance, with the date, laboratory and specimen description.
  2. A product data sheet or the technical specifications the products are made to.
  3. Fibre documentation confirming alkali resistance.
  4. Combustibility data for the material.
  5. Control specimens from your batch, which can be tested in an independent laboratory.

The same approach works for any facade material. The properties of shell limestone (coquina) blocks or travertine depend on the deposit and the specific batch: density, strength and water absorption can differ markedly between two quarries of the same stone. Insulation properties (for example, mineral wool facade boards) are taken from the manufacturer's technical documentation for the specific product. In every case compare figures in documents, not in advertising.

Typical mistakes when comparing offers: comparing flexural strength of specimens of different ages; setting spray-up figures from one supplier against cast figures from another; accepting data for "the material in general" instead of reports on the products that will go to site; not checking that specimens were made by the same method as the elements. If a report does not state the forming method, specimen age and curing conditions, it is almost useless for comparison.

Modulus of elasticity and impact resistance of GFRC

Besides strength, the behaviour of a thin-walled product is governed by the stiffness of the material and its ability to absorb impact energy. The modulus of elasticity of GFRC is close to that of fine-grained concrete and, according to industry data, usually lies in the range of about 10–25 GPa depending on mix design and density. It determines the deflection of a panel under wind, so it enters the calculation alongside flexural strength.

The main difference from unreinforced concrete appears after the first crack. Ordinary concrete fails in a brittle manner, whereas in glass fibre reinforced concrete the fibres take up the load: the product continues to work, deforms and fails gradually. This is why the material copes well with transport, installation and accidental impacts.

  • impact resistance is higher for sprayed material with a greater content and length of fibre;
  • a thin wall without stiffening ribs is more vulnerable to point impact than a ribbed or framed panel;
  • in areas of possible mechanical action — plinth, entrances, vehicle passages — the wall is thickened or replaceable elements are provided;
  • a chipped edge can generally be repaired in place, whereas a through crack requires assessment by an engineer.

Stiffness and fracture toughness also determine the rules for handling products. Panels are transported and stored in a position close to the design one, lifted only by the embedded parts provided, and never rested on edges or corners. Long profiled pieces are supported at several points during lifting so that bending stresses do not exceed the limit of proportionality before the piece even reaches the facade. Breaking these rules produces hairline cracks that show up after installation and are often mistaken for a material defect.

Over time the material's ability to deform after cracking decreases, and this is taken into account when design values are set. How properties change over decades of service is examined in detail in the article on the service life of GFRC on a facade.

How do thermal and moisture movements affect panel dimensions and joints?

A GFRC panel changes size as it heats, cools, becomes wet and dries, and these movements must be free to take place in the joints and fixing points. The coefficient of linear thermal expansion of GFRC is comparable with that of ordinary concrete — about 10–20 millionths per degree, the exact value depending on composition.

A simple calculation helps to gauge the order of magnitude. A panel 3 m long on a south-facing facade may heat up to 60–70 °C in summer and cool to −30 °C in winter; the range is about 90–100 degrees. With a coefficient of 12 millionths per degree, the change in length will be roughly 3–4 mm. To this are added reversible moisture movements and initial shrinkage, which is more pronounced in cement-rich mixes than in normal-weight concrete.

Joint width is not set by calculated movements alone. It also includes manufacturing tolerances for the products and installation tolerances for the subframe, as well as the working range of the sealant, which can stretch and compress only by a certain proportion of its width. For this reason joints between large panels are usually about 10–20 mm wide, while smaller values suffice for small decorative elements. A joint that is too narrow looks neater, but when heated the edges of adjacent products bear against each other and spall.

Dark surfaces heat up more than light ones, so for large panels in saturated colours the joint width and support arrangement are checked separately. Rigid restraint of a panel at every point is one of the main causes of cracks unrelated to the quality of the material itself. Typical dimensions and support arrangements are given on the GFRC facade panels page.

How GFRC properties change over time

The properties of glass fibre reinforced concrete do not remain constant: the compressive strength of the matrix usually increases with time, while the modulus of rupture and post-crack deformability may decrease. The cause is continuing cement hydration: reaction products densify the zone around the fibres, and the fibre bundles behave less compliantly.

The limit of proportionality, as a rule, is retained or rises slightly. Since working stresses in a product are set below this limit, the in-service reliability of a correctly designed panel does not fall with time. What diminishes is the reserve between first crack and failure, and it is this that is regulated through long-term values.

The rate of change is influenced by several decisions taken in production:

  • use of alkali-resistant fibre with sufficient zirconium dioxide content;
  • addition of active mineral admixtures that bind free lime in the cement paste;
  • polymer admixtures, which improve curing of the thin layer and reduce water absorption;
  • maintaining the moisture regime during the first day and curing products before dispatch.

When comparing offers, it is worth asking which strength values the manufacturer uses in the calculation — initial or long-term — and how the transition between them is justified. The role of each component in the mix design is described in the piece on the composition of GFRC.

A practical conclusion follows for the client: comparing manufacturers by the strength of specimens at 28 days is not enough. It is useful to request the results of accelerated ageing tests, if they have been carried out, information on the fibre and admixtures used, and examples of projects that have been in service for a sufficiently long time. An indirect sign of mature technology is the stability of production control results: a small scatter of values from batch to batch says more about the repeatability of the process than a single high figure.

Comparison of GFRC properties with other cladding materials

It makes sense to compare materials by the indicators that affect the facade structure: mass per square metre, thickness, combustibility and freedom of form. The density of GFRC is close to that of stone and concrete, but thanks to the thin wall the mass of the cladding is noticeably lower.

A comparison by mass alone is incomplete. A thin skin needs stiffening ribs or a frame and embedded parts, so the actual load on the subframe is higher than the mass of the wall alone and is taken from the working drawings of the specific product. On the other hand, larger elements reduce the number of brackets and joints: one panel or cornice section replaces dozens of small slabs. In the end, the correct comparison is not between materials as such but between complete facade solutions, taking into account the subframe, insulation and installation labour.

The table shows where the material's niche lies. GFRC is comparable with porcelain stoneware in mass but offers three-dimensional modelling and any profile. It is comparable with stone in the durability of a mineral surface, but two to three times lighter in flat cladding and many times lighter in three-dimensional elements that would have to be carved from solid stone. Thermal and acoustic properties: what to expect from the cladding

GFRC is a dense mineral material and does not perform a thermal insulation function. Its thermal conductivity, according to industry data, is of the order of 0.5–1 W/(m·°C), and with a wall thickness of 10–20 mm the panel's own thermal resistance is negligible. The thermal protection of the wall is provided by the insulation layer behind the cladding.

In a ventilated facade system the panel acts as a screen: it protects the insulation from precipitation, wind and ultraviolet light and forms the ventilated cavity. For the thermal calculation, what matters more than the properties of the cladding itself is the number and design of the brackets passing through the insulation: they create point thermal bridges. Requirements for the subframe, cavity and insulation are described in the section on ventilated facades.

The situation with sound insulation is similar. The mass of the thin skin is small, so it makes no significant contribution to airborne sound insulation; the main role is played by the load-bearing wall, insulation and windows. At the same time, the dense mineral surface does not resonate or drum under rain and hail, which distinguishes it favourably from thin sheet-metal cassettes.

Vapour permeability is a separate issue. A dense cement matrix lets water vapour through less readily than render or masonry, but in a rainscreen system this causes no problems: moisture from the wall and insulation is removed through the ventilated cavity, not through the cladding. Matters are different when decorative elements are fixed tight against the wall without a cavity. In such details drainage is provided, and the product must not be allowed to trap moisture in the substrate.

The thermal inertia of thin cladding is low: a panel heats up quickly in the sun and cools just as quickly. This is of no consequence for the rooms, since the cladding is separated from the wall by the cavity and insulation, but for the panel itself it means frequent temperature cycles. That is why thermal performance and movement are considered together in the design: the better the wall is insulated, the colder in winter and hotter in summer the outer screen becomes.

Surface durability: colour, efflorescence and maintenance

For the client, material properties are not only strength figures but also how the facade looks years later. Appearance is determined by the stability of the pigments, the porosity of the facing layer and how water is shed from the surface.

  • Colour. Mineral pigments added to the mix are resistant to ultraviolet light; slight lightening of tone in the first months is linked to drying and carbonation of the surface.
  • Efflorescence. A whitish deposit appears when water carries soluble salts to the surface; the risk is reduced by a dense matrix, water-repellent treatment and correct flashings.
  • Soiling. Dust settles less on a smooth water-repellent surface than on a deep texture; streaks most often arise beneath sills and cornices without drip grooves.
  • Abrasion. It is not critical for a facade but matters for steps, parapets and landscape elements.

Before facade acceptance it is useful to record the initial condition of the surface: photographs of representative areas under the same lighting and a retained reference sample make it possible to assess any change of tone objectively several years later. Slight variation in shade between batches is characteristic of all cement-based materials, and its permissible limits are agreed in advance on a set of samples rather than a single tile. For projects with high requirements for colour uniformity, surface coating with mineral paints is used.

The water-repellent impregnation is renewed periodically; the interval depends on the product, facade orientation and air pollution, and is stated in the operation manual. Cladding is washed with water at moderate pressure using neutral agents; acid-based products are used only on the manufacturer's recommendation. Horizontal profiled elements are the most dependent on water, so for GFRC cornices falls and drip grooves are built into the mould itself.

Which properties matter most in different climatic regions?

The set of indicators is the same, but their priority changes with the climate of the site. A design calculated for the south cannot be transferred to Siberia without checking, and vice versa.

ConditionsWhat comes to the foreDesign measures
Temperate climate with frequent freeze-thaw transitions (central Russia)Frost resistance, water absorptionDrainage, water-repellent treatment, protection of horizontal surfaces
Sharply continental climate (Urals, Siberia)Thermal movement over a wide annual rangeWider joints, sliding fixings, control of panel dimensions
Southern coastal areasHumidity, airborne salts, wind loadsCorrosion-resistant embedded parts and fixings, wind calculation
Seismic areas of the North CaucasusCladding mass, compliance of connectionsLight thin-walled products, fixings allowing for frame movement
Hot dry climateSurface heating, day–night variationsLight colours, checking of bowing in large panels

Low cladding mass is especially valuable in seismic areas: the lighter the suspended elements, the smaller the inertial loads on the fixings and the building frame. For tall buildings in any climate, wind remains decisive: peak loads in corner zones and at the parapet can be several times higher than in the general zone, and wall thickness and support spacing are set separately there.

The climatic data for the site should be passed to the manufacturer at enquiry stage: the construction region, building height, orientation of the main facades, and proximity to the sea or to roads where de-icing agents are used. This information is used to refine the mix design, the requirements for embedded parts and protective treatment, and the frost resistance test regime. The company's supply geography — from Novorossiysk and Makhachkala to Yekaterinburg and Novosibirsk — covers all the climate types listed, so requirements are set for the specific site every time.

FAQ

What is the density of GFRC in kg/m³?

Usually about 1,900–2,200 kg/m³, depending on composition and degree of compaction. The exact value is stated in the test report for the specific mix design.

How strong is GFRC?

Flexural strength of sprayed GFRC is roughly 15–30 MPa, compressive strength 40–80 MPa. Values depend on the mix design, forming method and specimen age.

How frost-resistant is GFRC?

Highly: a dense matrix with low water absorption tolerates freeze–thaw cycles well. The specific frost resistance grade is confirmed by testing specimens.

Does GFRC burn?

No. According to TECHNOFORMA Group, its GFRC belongs to combustibility class NG — non-combustible.

How do I check a manufacturer's GFRC properties?

Request specimen test reports, the product data sheet and documents for the alkali-resistant fibre, and if necessary test control specimens from your batch in an independent laboratory.

How do the properties of sprayed and cast GFRC differ?

Spray-up gives a higher fibre content oriented in the plane of the element, so flexural strength is usually higher. Casting (premix) is better for fine relief and stable geometry, but its flexural strength is generally lower. Exact values depend on the mix design and are given in the test report.

What is the modulus of elasticity of GFRC?

According to industry data, it is usually about 10–25 GPa depending on mix design, density and forming method. For deflection calculations, a value confirmed by testing the specific material is used.

How much does GFRC expand when heated?

The coefficient of thermal expansion is close to that of ordinary concrete — about 10–20 millionths per degree. For a panel 3 m long with an annual range of about 100 degrees this amounts to several millimetres, which must be accommodated by joints and sliding fixings.

Is GFRC a thermal insulation material?

No. It is a dense mineral material, and thin cladding has almost no effect on the thermal protection of the wall. Thermal protection is provided by the insulation within the facade system, while the panels protect it from the weather.
