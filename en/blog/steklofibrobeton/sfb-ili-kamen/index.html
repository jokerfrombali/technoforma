GFRC or Natural Stone for Facades

GFRC or natural stone for a facade is a choice between authenticity and freedom of form. Natural stone offers a natural structure that cannot be fully replicated and, with the right choice of stone, lasts a very long time. GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) makes it possible to create complex facade sculpting — cornices, columns, curved panels — with low weight and serial production, and its texture can imitate stone.

The main criterion is the architectural task. Wall surfaces, plinths, steps and accent zones are often made of stone; complex three-dimensional decoration of GFRC. Many projects combine the two.

TECHNOFORMA Group works with both materials: it manufactures GFRC and has worked with natural stone for more than 15 years. For a general overview of GFRC, see GFRC: material and technology.

Appearance and texture

Natural stone is unique: pattern, inclusions and shades vary from block to block. Granite and basalt give a dense granular surface, marble offers veining and depth of colour, travertine its characteristic pores, shell limestone (coquina) and limestone a warm porous texture. Stone can be polished, honed, bush-hammered, sawn or split. Over the years stone develops a noble patina.

The variety of stones is a strength but also a challenge: within one project slabs must be matched by colour, and for large volumes supply from a single quarry must be secured.

GFRC reproduces shape and texture from a model. It can be smooth, relief, acid-etched, integrally pigmented, and can imitate travertine, limestone or sandstone. The advantage is uniformity: all products in a series match in colour and texture. The limitation is that it is an imitation: on close inspection an experienced eye will distinguish concrete from natural stone, especially for stones with a pronounced crystalline structure such as granite and marble.

Weight and substructure

Stone is usually installed on facades as 30–40 mm slabs on clips fixed to a ventilated rainscreen facade substructure. The weight of 1 m² of cladding depends on the density of the stone: granite is around 2,600–2,800 kg/m³, while limestone and shell limestone are noticeably lower and vary greatly between quarries. A 30 mm granite slab weighs about 80–85 kg/m².

A 10–15 mm GFRC panel weighs roughly 20–35 kg/m², and hollow three-dimensional elements are several times lighter than their stone counterparts.

ParameterNatural stoneGFRC
Typical cladding thickness30–40 mm10–20 mm
Weight per 1 m²≈ 60–110 kg, depends on the stone≈ 20–40 kg
Element formatLimited by slab strength and weightLarge-format panels, three-dimensional elements
SubstructureReinforced, with close spacingLighter, other things being equal
Three-dimensional decorationHeavy, solidHollow shells

Values are indicative; exact data for stone depend on the quarry, and for GFRC on the mix design and product construction. For walls with low load-bearing capacity, lighter cladding can be the deciding factor.

Durability

The durability of natural stone depends on the rock type. Granites and basalts are very dense, with low water absorption and high frost resistance — among the most durable cladding materials. Marbles on facades lose their polish over time due to rain. Soft porous stones — shell limestone, some limestones and sandstones — need water-repellent treatment and careful selection for climates with frequent freeze-thaw cycles.

Well-made GFRC is resistant to frost, moisture and UV; according to TECHNOFORMA Group, its frost resistance is improved with admixtures. A feature of the material is a gradual reduction in flexural strength over time, which is taken into account in design calculations. GFRC durability depends heavily on the mix design, the alkali resistance of the fibre and properly designed details.

In both cases details matter: falls and flashings on horizontal surfaces, correct joints and proper fixings made of corrosion-resistant steel.

Repairability also differs. A damaged stone slab can be replaced, but matching a slab of the same pattern and shade a few years later can be difficult. A GFRC product can be reproduced from the same mould and mix if the manufacturer has kept the tooling and documentation.

Cost and lead times

In both cases the cost depends on the project, and no prices are given in this article. TECHNOFORMA Group calculates the cost from drawings or after a site survey. The main factors:

  • For stone — the stone type and quarry, thickness, surface finish, profile complexity, cutting waste and logistics. Rare stones ordered specially cost more than common ones.
  • For GFRC — the number of unique moulds, run size, texture and colour, whether a frame is required, and volume.

Lead times also depend on different things. For stone — whether it is in stock or has to be imported. TECHNOFORMA Group imports stone from India, Turkey, China and Iran, keeps stock from these countries and arranges supply to order from anywhere in the world; its stone warehouses and production base are in the Moscow Region. For GFRC — time for models and moulds, sample approval and production capacity; the company's GFRC production capacity is over 20,000 m² per month (according to the company).

When to combine them

Combining stone and GFRC is a common architectural solution that makes use of the strengths of each material:

  • Stone plinth and entrance, GFRC on the upper floors. At lower levels, where the facade is closest to people and exposed to mechanical impact, granite does the work; above it, lightweight decoration.
  • Stone wall surfaces, GFRC architectural details. Cornices, pilasters and window surrounds match the colour and texture of the stone but are made as light hollow elements.
  • Steps, paving and parapets in stone; balustrades and planters in GFRC.

When combining materials, it is important to match the GFRC colour and texture to samples from the specific stone batch in advance and to design the substructure for materials of different weights. It is easier when one contractor supplies both stone and GFRC: they are responsible for the compatibility of solutions. For stone, TECHNOFORMA Group provides design, 3D visualisation, layout, measurements by its own surveyors, installation and design supervision.

How natural stone and GFRC are fixed: a comparison of details

On modern buildings both materials are installed mainly as hung cladding, but the fixing details differ. A stone slab is held by its edges or its back, whereas a GFRC product is hung on embedded parts cast in during manufacture.

DetailNatural stoneGFRC
Visible fixingClips gripping the edge of the slabRarely used
Concealed fixingKerfs in the edges or undercut anchors in the back of the slabEmbedded parts and brackets on the back, adjustable hangers
Fixing in mortarPossible for plinths and low heights; requires a rigid substrateNot used for hung panels
Three-dimensional elementsMassive blocks bearing on the structure and anchoredHollow shells on brackets or a steel frame
Position adjustmentWithin the travel of the clip and bracketIn three planes through the hanger details

The sequence of work on site differs too. Stone slabs usually arrive with a margin and are partly trimmed in place — at openings, corners and abutments. GFRC products are made to exact size from the working drawings and no trimming on site is envisaged, so the geometry of the building is checked by an as-built survey before production starts.

For stone, the strength of the slab itself at the fixing point is critical: a kerf or a hole weakens the section, so thickness and format are selected with the stone type and wind load in mind. For GFRC, the anchorage of the embedded part in the thin skin is critical — it is reinforced by local thickening. The specifics of stone cladding on a substructure are discussed in the article on ventilated facades in natural stone.

Which shapes are difficult in stone and where GFRC replaces it

Practically any detail can be made of stone; the question is labour and weight. A flat slab is the product of sawing a block; a moulded cornice, a capital or a fluted column requires milling, turning and hand finishing of a massive blank, with a considerable part of the material going to waste.

  • Cornices with a large projection. A stone cornice is a heavy block that must be anchored into the wall with the overturning moment taken into account. A hollow GFRC cornice of the same profile is hung on brackets.
  • Columns and capitals. In stone they are assembled from drums and carved blocks; in GFRC the shaft is made as half-shells around the load-bearing structure.
  • Curved and radiused surfaces. Stone requires individual cutting of every slab, while GFRC panels are formed to the curve straight away.
  • Relief and ornament in large runs. Stone carving is repeated for every piece, whereas the mould for GFRC is made once.
  • Large format. The size of a stone slab is limited by its strength and weight; a framed GFRC panel can have an area of several square metres.

The reverse situation also exists: a polished surface with depth of pattern, steps, thresholds and paving slabs are the domain of stone, where a concrete imitation loses out in both appearance and wear resistance.

Stone or GFRC: what to choose for different building types?

The choice depends on the number of storeys, the architecture and the conditions of use. Typical solutions are given below; they are no substitute for calculation for a specific building.

Building typeNatural stoneGFRC
Multi-storey residential complexPlinth, entrances, lower floorsDecor and panels over the main height of the building
Business centreFacade planes, lobby, entrance zoneLarge-format panels, fins, three-dimensional elements
HotelFormal areas, podium, landscapingClassical or national decor, arches, cornices
Public buildingPortal, plinth, staircasesColumns, pediments, bas-reliefs, giant order
Private housePlinth, porch, individual wallsWindow surrounds, cornices, balustrades

The maintenance budget deserves separate assessment. For the management company of a residential complex, what matters is that damaged elements can be replaced and that no regular repainting is needed; for a hotel or business centre, it is keeping up the appearance of formal areas that visitors see at arm's length. The same combination of materials is therefore distributed differently by height and zone on different projects.

On tall buildings, installation also favours light cladding: lighter products are easier to lift and to install from hoists and facade cradles. On the lower floors, where the facade is seen at close range and impacts are possible, dense stone is appropriate, for example granite for the plinth and entrance.

How to match the colour and texture of GFRC to stone

GFRC is matched to the stone, not the other way round: the colour of the natural material is set by the quarry, while the concrete mix can be adjusted. The work is done in several steps.

  1. Obtain stone samples from the batch that will go to the site, with the chosen surface finish.
  2. Decide which tone is taken as the reference: stone has a spread of shades, and the average should be the guide.
  3. Make several GFRC samples with different pigments, aggregate and texture.
  4. Compare the samples outdoors in daylight, dry and wet, at the angle from which the facade will be seen.
  5. Approve the reference sample and keep it with both the client and the manufacturer for batch control.

It should be borne in mind that the materials age differently: stone darkens and acquires a patina, while integrally coloured concrete may lighten slightly. A complete match is rarely achieved, so architects often separate the materials deliberately — for example, making the decor half a tone lighter than the wall plane. The available surface options are described in the article on GFRC textures.

Climate, seismicity and cladding weight: when they decide the choice

In areas of high seismicity the weight of the cladding directly affects the inertial loads on the fixings and the wall. The lighter the element, the easier it is to retain it during shaking, so heavy three-dimensional stone decor on upper floors is used with caution here. The requirements for fixing details are described in the article on facades in seismic regions.

Climate affects above all the choice of stone. In regions with frequent freeze–thaw cycles and damp winters, porous limestones, shell limestone and travertine need their frost resistance checked against data for the specific quarry, and horizontal surfaces need protection. In coastal cities airborne salt is added, and in the south strong heating of dark surfaces. The properties of the stones are conveniently compared using the comparison table of facade stones.

For GFRC, climate is taken into account at the level of the mix design and the details: the frost resistance grade, water-repellent treatment, the width of movement joints. The choice of material itself does not change, which simplifies standard solutions for projects in different regions.

Care and cleaning: how the upkeep of stone and concrete facades differs

Neither facade needs painting, but they are maintained differently. Dense stone — granite, basalt — is washed with pressurised water and neutral products; it absorbs almost no dirt. Porous stones and marble are sensitive to acid-based products, and rust and organic stains penetrate deeply, so they are protected with impregnations, renewed at the interval recommended by the supplier of the product.

In terms of care GFRC is closer to porous stone: water-repellent treatment reduces soiling and efflorescence, and cleaning is done with mild products without abrasives. Inspection points common to both materials are the condition of joints, flashings and fixings and the absence of chipped edges. On a combined facade, cleaning products are tested on both materials in an inconspicuous area: a product that is safe for granite may alter the tone of concrete or soft limestone.

If an individual element is damaged, the procedure is similar: the cause is recorded, the neighbouring fixings are checked, and then the slab or product is replaced. For stone it is useful to keep a stock of slabs from the same batch; for concrete, details of the mix design and the retained moulds.

Typical mistakes when choosing between stone and GFRC

Mistakes are more often linked not to the materials themselves but to the stage at which the decision was taken and the data on which it was based.

  • Comparing only the price per square metre of material, without the substructure, fixings, lifting and cutting waste.
  • Choosing a stone from a palm-sized sample: across the area of a facade the spread of pattern and tone turns out wider than expected.
  • Ordering a concrete imitation of marble or granite for areas that are viewed at close range.
  • Porous stone on the plinth and horizontal surfaces without protection and flashings.
  • A late decision to replace stone decor with GFRC, when the substructure and embedded parts have already been designed for a different weight.
  • Approving the GFRC colour against a stone sample from a different batch.

Most of these mistakes can be avoided if the materials are chosen at concept stage and the stone and concrete samples are approved at the same time.

FAQ

Which natural stone should I choose for a house facade?

For plinths and areas under load — dense stones such as granite and basalt. For walls — limestone, dolomite, sandstone or travertine, taking into account the frost resistance of the specific quarry. TECHNOFORMA Group supplies these stones, as well as marble, onyx and shell limestone.

Is travertine a natural stone?

Yes, travertine is a sedimentary rock formed from mineral spring deposits. It has a characteristic porous structure.

Can GFRC imitate stone?

Yes, GFRC can reproduce the texture and colour of travertine, limestone and sandstone. However, it is an imitation: it does not fully replace the natural structure, especially of granite and marble.

Which is better — artificial or natural stone?

Natural stone is unique and durable but heavy. Artificial materials, including GFRC, are lighter and more uniform and allow complex decoration. Combining them is often optimal.

Which stones does TECHNOFORMA Group supply?

Shell limestone, granite, basalt, dolomite, limestone, marble, onyx, sandstone and travertine. The stone is imported from India, Turkey, China and Iran; the company also has extensive experience with Dagestan stones.

Can a ventilated rainscreen facade be clad with natural stone?

Yes, stone slabs are installed on clips on the substructure of a ventilated rainscreen facade. The substructure is designed for the greater weight of the stone.

Can a stone cornice be replaced with a GFRC one on an existing building?

Yes, provided the profile is reproduced from a measured survey and the fixing is designed afresh. A hollow GFRC element is lighter than a stone block, so the load on the wall is reduced, but the hanging details and flashings are developed for the new construction. For historic buildings such a replacement requires approval.

Which keeps its colour longer — natural stone or GFRC?

The colour of dense stones such as granite is practically stable. Porous stone darkens over time and acquires a patina. GFRC integrally coloured with mineral pigments keeps its tone for a long time but may lighten slightly; a surface coating needs periodic renewal.

Is a different substructure needed for stone and GFRC on the same facade?

As a rule the load-bearing principle is the same, but the bracket spacing, profile sections and cladding fixing details differ because of the different weight and hanging method. The areas are calculated separately and coordinated with each other in plane and at the joints.
