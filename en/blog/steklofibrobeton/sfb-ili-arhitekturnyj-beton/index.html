Architectural Concrete and GFRC: The Differences

Architectural concrete is concrete whose surface stays exposed and serves as the finish itself: no render, paint or cladding. The main requirement is the quality of the face: even colour, a specified texture, crisp arrises, a minimum of blowholes and formwork marks. It is used in in-situ walls, precast panels, stairs, street furniture and facade ornament.

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) is a closely related special case: it is also concrete with an exposed face, but reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre, which makes thin, light elements possible. If you need massive in-situ work or a load-bearing element, choose architectural concrete; if you need lightweight cladding panels and ornament, choose GFRC.

A general overview of GFRC is in GFRC: material and technology.

What architectural concrete is

Architectural concrete is not a separate material but an approach in which the concrete surface becomes the finish. Other names include decorative concrete and fair-faced concrete. What distinguishes it from ordinary structural concrete:

  • Mix. White cement, pigments, selected aggregates (sometimes decorative stone chippings) and plasticisers are often used. Composition and dosage must be consistent from batch to batch, otherwise patches of different shades appear on the surface.
  • Formwork. High-grade formwork or special form liners are used to set the surface pattern: smooth, board-marked, relief.
  • Technology. Thorough compaction, temperature control, special release agents, careful striking.
  • Finishing. Grinding, sandblasting, acid etching, water-repellent treatment.

On facades architectural concrete is used as in-situ walls and precast panels; in landscaping, for street furniture: benches, railings, planters, stairs. Facade ornament in architectural concrete — cornices, pilasters, surrounds — becomes heavy when cast as a solid section, so it is more often made of GFRC.

DIY architectural concrete at home is feasible for small items such as tiles, planters and worktops. A facade needs factory technology, mix control and fixing calculations.

In-situ and precast elements

By production method, architectural concrete comes in two types:

  • In-situ. Walls, columns, slabs and stairs are cast on site in formwork. These are load-bearing structures that also serve as the finish. The difficulty is achieving the same surface quality over a large area and across different pours, in changing weather and with different crews. Defects are hard to correct.
  • Precast. Panels, steps and cladding elements are made in a factory under controlled conditions and installed on site. Surface quality is higher and more consistent, but elements of ordinary concrete are heavy and need a crane and heavy-duty fixings.

GFRC belongs to precast elements but holds a special place because of its thin wall: it is cladding, not structure. It gives the look of architectural concrete on the facade without highly demanding in-situ work and without heavy precast panels.

Thickness and weight

Thickness is the key difference. Architectural concrete members are reinforced with steel bars, which need concrete cover, usually at least 2–3 cm on each side. So even precast cladding panels of ordinary concrete are 60–80 mm thick or more, and in-situ walls 150–200 mm and more.

GFRC has no steel reinforcement: fibre reinforcement is distributed through the whole thickness. The element wall is usually 10–20 mm.

Shapes and textures

Both materials produce expressive surfaces, but their shaping possibilities differ.

Architectural concrete suits large flat surfaces and simple geometric forms: walls, prisms, steps, benches. The texture is set by formwork or a form liner: smooth, board-marked, geometric relief. Complex modelling is limited by the reinforcement and cover thickness.

GFRC is freer in form: a thin wall without bars follows complex relief, curved surfaces, cornice profiles, capitals and ornament. Textures:

  • smooth, including for painting;
  • concrete texture with pores;
  • acid-etched and sandblasted with exposed aggregate;
  • stone and timber imitation built into the mould;
  • integral colour.

With the same face-coat mix, GFRC and architectural concrete elements can look identical.

Applications

The fields of use partly overlap, but each material has its own tasks:

  • Architectural concrete: in-situ load-bearing walls with an exposed face, stairs, ramps, retaining walls, massive street furniture, benches and railings in landscaping, precast cladding panels for buildings with a strong frame.
  • GFRC: rainscreen facade panels, curved elements, cornices, pilasters, column casings, window surrounds, plinth cladding, entrance ornament, lightweight street furniture and planters, interior panels.

The materials are combined on one project: an in-situ stair or retaining wall in architectural concrete and GFRC facade panels of the same shade create a unified image.

With such a combination it is important to agree shade and texture on samples in advance: in-situ concrete develops its colour on site under different conditions from factory elements, and the same mix design does not guarantee a match. Reference samples are approved before work starts and kept on site for acceptance.

Maintenance in service also differs. The exposed surface of architectural concrete picks up dirt and efflorescence over time, so it is usually given a water-repellent treatment that is renewed periodically; chips in in-situ concrete are repaired with colour-matched compounds, and repairs are often visible. A GFRC cladding panel is easier to replace if seriously damaged: it is removed and remade in the same mould. Common rules apply to both: prevent water standing on horizontal surfaces, maintain joints and do not clean the facade with abrasives or aggressive acid products that damage the face.

Architectural concrete or GFRC: which to choose for a specific task?

The choice is determined by the function of the element. If it bears load or needs to be massive, architectural concrete is required; if it is suspended cladding or decor on a finished frame, GFRC. Cases where both options are technically possible remain open to debate, and then mass, geometry and lead times decide.

TaskUsual choiceWhy

Solutions for suspended cladding are collected on the page GFRC facade panels, and lightweight landscaping elements in the section landscape elements and planters. If the same element can be made in both ways, it is useful to request two versions of the fixing details and compare them together with the loads on the building.

In practice both materials are often used on one building. The podium, entrance stairs, retaining walls and landscaping elements are executed in in-situ or precast concrete, while everything above the ground floor that is hung on the frame is made of GFRC. The boundary usually lies where the zone of mechanical impact ends and the zone begins in which every extra kilogram increases the load on floor slabs and fixings.

How precast architectural concrete and GFRC panels are fixed

The difference in mass changes the whole installation scheme. A heavy architectural concrete panel bears on a floor slab or corbel and is lifted by crane, whereas a GFRC shell is hung on a subframe or anchors, like the cladding of a ventilated facade.

  • Precast concrete. Steel bearing and restraint fixings are designed for considerable mass; installation is carried out by tower or mobile crane; the fixings are incorporated into the building frame at its design stage.
  • GFRC. Embedded parts in the item, brackets and rails, fixed and sliding points for thermal movement; lifting by winch, installation from cradles and scaffolding.
  • In common. Sealed joints between elements, anti-corrosion protection of metal, checking the geometry of the frame before work begins.

GFRC cladding is often incorporated into a suspended system with insulation and an air gap; how such a system is arranged is described on the page installation of ventilated facades. The effect of cladding mass on the subframe and floor slabs is examined in the article on how much GFRC weighs.

The requirements for the accuracy of the frame also differ. A heavy panel bears on embedded parts cast in beforehand, and an error in their position is difficult to correct. The subframe for GFRC is adjustable in three directions, so deviations of an in-situ frame within normal construction tolerances are compensated during installation. Nevertheless, before the items go into production an as-built survey of the facade is carried out and the layout is adjusted to the actual dimensions.

How production differs and where surface defects come from

Architectural concrete is placed in formwork and compacted by vibration, whereas GFRC is sprayed or cast into a mould in a thin layer together with the fibre. Hence the different typical defects and different methods of quality control.

ParameterArchitectural concreteGFRC
Where the surface is formedIn formwork on site or at a precast concrete plantIn a mould in the workshop
What affects qualityFormwork, release agent, compaction, weather during curingFace coat, evenness of application, curing in the first 24 hours
Typical defectsBlowholes, formwork joint marks, cold joints, shade variation between poursPores, shade variation between batches, fibre showing through if the process is breached
RemedyRepair on a finished wall is, as a rule, visibleReplacement of an individual item

The main practical difference is the possibility of rejection. An unsuccessful area of an in-situ wall remains part of the building, whereas a factory-made item is checked before dispatch and remade if necessary. The stages of manufacture are described in the article GFRC production technology.

Which is more durable: concrete with steel reinforcement or with fibre?

Both materials are designed for decades, but they age differently. The weak point of reinforced concrete is the cover over the reinforcement; that of GFRC is the quality of the fibre and the fixing elements.

In architectural concrete the steel reinforcement is protected by the alkaline environment of the cement matrix. Over time the surface layer carbonates, and if the cover is thin or cracked, the reinforcement begins to corrode: rust streaks and spalling appear. This is why cover thickness, concrete density and water-repellent treatment are so important for exposed concrete surfaces.

In GFRC there is no steel reinforcement in the body of the item, and this mechanism is absent. On the other hand, alkali-resistant fibre and protection of embedded parts are essential: ordinary glass fibre loses strength in a cement environment, and unprotected metal corrodes. Provided the mix is observed and corrosion-resistant fixings are used, a thin wall lasts as long as a massive one. Other types of dispersed reinforcement are covered in the article GFRC and fibre concrete.

Lead times and organisation of work: in-situ, precast concrete and GFRC

In-situ architectural concrete is tied to the frame construction programme and to the weather, whereas GFRC items are manufactured in parallel with construction and installed on the finished building. This determines how programme risks are distributed.

  • In-situ. Each pour means formwork, reinforcement, concreting, curing and striking; in winter heating is needed, which affects the shade; correcting the result after striking is difficult.
  • Precast architectural concrete. Factory quality, but heavy logistics: few elements fit on a lorry, and a crane and storage space are needed on site.
  • GFRC. Time is needed at the start for the model, mould and sample, followed by regular deliveries; installation does not occupy the tower crane and proceeds independently of the main works.

With in-situ concrete the facade comes into being together with the frame and cannot be postponed. Suspended cladding makes it possible first to close the building envelope and then to carry out finishing section by section and, if necessary, to divide the project into phases.

Seasonality also works differently. Factory-made items cure in the workshop under controlled conditions, and the time of year does not affect their shade. In-situ surfaces concreted in summer and in winter may differ noticeably in colour, so exposed areas are, where possible, executed in one season with one mix, and the sequence of pours is planned with regard to the position of construction joints.

Typical mistakes when choosing between the materials

Mistakes most often arise because architectural concrete in the brief is understood as an appearance rather than a structural solution. As a result, things that are not comparable are compared.

  • The cost per square metre is compared without regard to the frame: heavy panels require more substantial fixings and a reserve of load-bearing capacity in the floor slabs.
  • A concrete texture is specified in the design without determining whether it is structure or cladding.
  • The uniformity of a factory-made item is expected from an in-situ wall.
  • Complex relief is designed in precast concrete, although the thick wall and reinforcement limit the depth and sharpness of the profile.
  • The shade of the in-situ concrete and of the suspended elements is not agreed on samples.
  • Maintenance is not provided for: water-repellent treatment and access for washing.

Most of these mistakes can be avoided if, as early as concept stage, the facade is divided into load-bearing parts and cladding and a technology is chosen for each.

What makes up the cost of each solution

The correct comparison is not material against material but the complete finished facade: with fixings, lifting, the effect on the structure and finishing. A kilogram of GFRC mix costs more than ordinary concrete, but several times less of it is used.

FAQ

What is architectural concrete?

Concrete whose face serves as the finish, with no render or cladding. It differs in mix, formwork quality and technology, which ensure even colour and a specified texture.

How does architectural concrete differ from GFRC?

Architectural concrete is reinforced with steel, so elements are thick and heavy but can be load-bearing. GFRC is reinforced with glass fibre; its 10–20 mm elements serve as lightweight cladding and ornament.

Can I make architectural concrete myself?

For small items such as tiles, planters and worktops, yes. Facade elements require factory technology, mix control and fixing calculations.

Which concrete is better for facade ornament?

For cornices, pilasters, surrounds and panels, GFRC: it is light, thin-walled and reproduces complex shapes. Architectural concrete suits massive and load-bearing elements.

Can precast concrete panels be replaced with GFRC in a completed design?

As a rule, yes, but the fixing details and layout will need to be reworked: thin-walled elements are hung differently from heavy panels. The load on the floor slabs is reduced, so strengthening of the frame is usually not required. The decision is taken by the designer after checking the details and the fire safety requirements for the facade system.

Does GFRC need steel reinforcement?

There are no reinforcing bars in the body of the item: tensile forces are taken by alkali-resistant glass fibre distributed through the thickness. Steel is used in embedded parts and, for large panels, in a rigid frame on the rear side to which the shell is attached. This metal is protected against corrosion.

Which material performs better at ground level — on steps and paving?

For steps, landings and other surfaces that are walked on and treated with de-icing agents, massive dense concrete or natural stone is used. GFRC is a material for cladding and decor; in the plinth zone it is used with allowance for impact loads, but not as paving.
