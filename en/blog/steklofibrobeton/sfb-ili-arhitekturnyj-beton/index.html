Architectural Concrete and GFRC: The Differences

Updated: 2026-09-25 · TECHNOFORMA editorial team

Architectural concrete is concrete whose surface stays exposed and serves as the finish itself: no render, paint or cladding. The main requirement is the quality of the exposed face: even colour, a specified texture, crisp edges and a minimum of blowholes and formwork marks. It is used for in-situ walls, precast panels, stairs, street furniture and facade decoration.

GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) is a special case with a similar purpose: it is also concrete with an exposed face, but reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre, which allows thin, light products. If you need a massive monolith or a load-bearing element, choose architectural concrete; if you need cladding panels and decoration with low weight, choose GFRC.

For a general overview of GFRC, see GFRC: material and technology.

What architectural concrete is

Architectural concrete is not a separate material but an approach in which the concrete surface becomes the finish. Other names include decorative concrete and fair-faced concrete. What distinguishes it from ordinary structural concrete:

  • Mix. White cement, pigments, selected aggregates (sometimes decorative stone chippings) and plasticisers are often used. The mix and dosing must be consistent from batch to batch, otherwise patches of different shades appear on the surface.
  • Formwork. High-quality formwork or special form liners are used to set the surface pattern: smooth, board-marked or relief.
  • Technology. Thorough compaction, temperature control, special release agents and careful striking.
  • Finishing. Grinding, sandblasting, acid etching, water-repellent treatment.

On facades, architectural concrete is used as in-situ walls and precast panels; in landscaping, for street furniture such as benches, barriers, planters and stairs. Facade decoration in architectural concrete — cornices, pilasters, surrounds — is heavy when cast as a solid section, so it is more often made of GFRC.

DIY architectural concrete at home is possible for small items — tiles, planters, worktops. Facades require factory technology, mix control and fixing design.

In-situ and precast elements

Architectural concrete comes in two types by method of manufacture:

  • In-situ. Walls, columns, slabs and stairs are cast on site in formwork. These are load-bearing structures that also serve as the finish. The difficulty is achieving consistent surface quality over a large area and across different pours, in changing weather and with different crews. Defects are hard to correct.
  • Precast. Panels, steps and cladding elements are made in a factory under controlled conditions and installed on site. Surface quality is higher and more consistent, but products made of ordinary concrete are heavy and require a crane and heavy-duty fixings.

GFRC is precast, but thanks to its thin wall it holds a special place: it is cladding, not structure. It gives the effect of architectural concrete on a facade without highly complex in-situ work and without heavy precast panels.

Thickness and weight

Thickness is the key difference. Architectural concrete structures are reinforced with steel bars, which need concrete cover — usually at least 2–3 cm on each side. So even precast cladding panels of ordinary concrete are 60–80 mm thick or more, and in-situ walls 150–200 mm or more.

GFRC has no steel reinforcement: the fibre reinforcement is distributed throughout the thickness. The product wall is usually 10–20 mm.

Shapes and textures

Both materials produce expressive surfaces, but their shaping capabilities differ.

Architectural concrete is good for large surfaces and simple geometric shapes: walls, prisms, steps, benches. Texture is set by formwork or a form liner: smooth, board-marked, geometric relief. Complex sculpting is limited by reinforcement and cover thickness.

GFRC allows more freedom of form: a thin wall without reinforcement follows complex relief, curved surfaces, cornice profiles, capitals and ornament. Textures:

  • smooth, including for painting;
  • concrete texture with pores;
  • acid-etched and sandblasted with exposed aggregate;
  • stone and timber effects built into the mould;
  • integral pigmentation.

With the same face-coat mix, GFRC and architectural concrete products can look identical.

Applications

The areas of application partly overlap, but each material has its own tasks:

  • Architectural concrete: in-situ load-bearing walls with an exposed face, stairs, ramps, retaining walls, massive street furniture, benches and barriers in landscaping, precast cladding panels for buildings with a strong frame.
  • GFRC: facade cladding panels, curved elements, cornices, pilasters, column shells, window surrounds, plinth cladding, entrance decoration, light street furniture and planters, interior panels.

The materials are combined on one project: an in-situ staircase or retaining wall in architectural concrete and facade cladding in GFRC panels of the same shade create a unified look.

With such a combination it is important to agree the shade and texture on samples in advance: in-situ concrete develops its colour on site under different conditions than factory products, and the same mix does not guarantee a match. Reference samples are approved before work starts and kept on site for acceptance.

FAQ

What is architectural concrete?

Concrete whose exposed face serves as the finish, with no render or cladding. It differs in mix, formwork quality and technology, which ensure even colour and a specified texture.

How does architectural concrete differ from GFRC?

Architectural concrete is reinforced with steel, so products are thick and heavy but can be load-bearing. GFRC is reinforced with glass fibre; its 10–20 mm products serve as lightweight cladding and decoration.

Can architectural concrete be made DIY?

Yes, for small items — tiles, planters, worktops. Facade elements require factory technology, mix control and fixing design.

Which concrete is better for facade decoration?

For cornices, pilasters, surrounds and panels — GFRC: it is light, thin-walled and reproduces complex shapes. Architectural concrete suits massive and load-bearing elements.
