How Much GFRC Weighs and How It Affects Facade Loads

The weight of glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) is determined by the density of the material (roughly 1,900–2,200 kg/m³) and the wall thickness. At the 10–15 mm thickness typical for facade panels, a square metre of GFRC weighs about 20–35 kg, excluding ribs, frame and embedded items. That is several times lighter than natural stone or precast concrete cladding of the same area.

The key thing for the designer is to calculate not a notional weight per square metre but the actual weight of each element with stiffening ribs, frame and fixings: this is what determines bracket spacing, substructure sections and the load capacity of the anchors in the wall.

This article is for ventilated facade designers, technical supervision and clients comparing cladding options. A general overview is in GFRC: material and technology.

Weight per square metre at different thicknesses

GFRC wall thickness in facade elements is usually 10–20 mm: thin walls are possible thanks to fibre reinforcement throughout the volume. The weight of a square metre of wall is density multiplied by thickness. Indicative values at a density of about 2,000 kg/m³:

Wall thicknessWeight of 1 m² of wallWhere used
10 mm≈ 20 kgSmall flat panels, cladding
12–15 mm≈ 24–30 kgStandard facade panels, cornices, surrounds
20 mm≈ 40 kgLarge-format panels, vibration-cast elements
25–30 mm≈ 50–60 kgMassive decoration, elements with higher requirements

The weight of stiffening ribs, a steel frame (for large panels), embedded items and fixings is added to the wall weight. For a three-dimensional element — a cornice or column — the developed area of all walls is used, not the facade area the element covers. The exact weight of each element is given in the working drawings.

Comparison with stone and concrete

A comparison with other cladding materials shows why GFRC is chosen for complex facade decoration. Indicative weight of 1 m² of cladding:

MaterialTypical thicknessWeight of 1 m²
GFRC10–15 mm≈ 20–35 kg
Porcelain stoneware10–12 mm≈ 22–30 kg
Granite, marble (slab)30 mm≈ 80–85 kg
Limestone, travertine (slab)30–40 mm≈ 60–100 kg, depending on rock density
Precast reinforced concrete (panel)from 60–80 mm≈ 150 kg or more

On flat wall areas porcelain stoneware is comparable in weight to GFRC. The difference shows on three-dimensional elements: a stone or concrete cornice or column weighs many times more than a hollow GFRC element of the same shape. Values are indicative; stone density depends on the quarry.

How the weight is distributed also matters. Natural stone on a facade is usually fixed as small-format slabs on clips, and each fixing point carries a significant load. A GFRC panel of the same size is lighter, so the format can be increased without strengthening the connection. This reduces the number of joints and fixing points on the facade.

Load on the wall and substructure

The cladding load is transferred along a chain: element → fixings → rails → brackets → anchors → wall. The low weight of GFRC gives several practical benefits:

  • smaller sections and wider spacing of substructure members — savings on metal;
  • lower load on anchors, which is especially important for walls of aerated concrete, hollow brick and other weak substrates;
  • lower load on foundations when refurbishing existing buildings;
  • easier and safer installation at height.

However, weight is not the only load. The substructure is also subject to wind (significant for tall buildings and corner zones), the self-weight of the insulation, thermal movement and, in seismic regions, seismic action. Calculations follow the applicable codes on loads and actions, taking into account the construction region and building height.

The wall is checked separately. Cast-in-place reinforced concrete and solid brick hold anchors reliably, while in aerated concrete, hollow brick and old masonry the anchor capacity can be several times lower. In such cases the number of fixing points is increased, special anchors are used or the load is transferred to the floor slabs. The low weight of GFRC provides a margin here that heavy cladding simply does not have.

Hollow elements

Three-dimensional GFRC elements — columns, cornices, pilasters, string courses, planters — are made hollow: the element is a thin shell following the external shape. As a result, a column that would weigh tonnes in solid concrete weighs many times less in GFRC and is installed without heavy machinery.

Features of hollow elements:

  • ribs, diaphragms or a steel frame are provided inside for stiffness;
  • hollow columns can serve as cladding for a load-bearing steel or reinforced concrete column;
  • the cavity can be used for concealed downpipes or lighting cables;
  • water must not collect in the cavity — drainage holes and protection against water entry from above are needed.

A hollow design requires precise mould geometry and careful jointing of parts. Large columns and cornices are often assembled from several sections, with joints placed along architectural lines so they are not visible. The number and size of sections are set at the design stage, taking into account the weight of each piece: it must be convenient to transport and install without large machinery.

For comparison: a cornice with a 400×300 mm section in solid concrete would weigh around 250–300 kg per linear metre. A 15 mm GFRC shell with a developed width of about 1.2 m weighs around 35–40 kg per linear metre plus fixings. The figures are indicative, but the order of difference is telling.

What to include in the calculations

When designing a facade with GFRC elements, the calculations take into account:

  1. The actual weight of each element with ribs, frame and embedded items, not a notional weight per square metre.
  2. Wind load, taking into account height, region and corner-zone aerodynamics.
  3. Substrate load capacity — results of on-site anchor pull-out tests in the specific wall.
  4. Thermal movement — gaps between elements and sliding fixings.
  5. Long-term GFRC strength — design values are lower than initial ones.
  6. Seismic action, if the site is in a seismic region.
  7. Installation loads — lifting and temporary supports.

How is the weight of a particular GFRC product calculated?

The weight of a product is calculated not from the facade area but from its developed area — the full surface of the skin, including all projections, returns and edges. For a flat panel the developed area is almost equal to the face area, whereas for a cornice or a profiled window surround it may be two to three times the visible projection. This is why two elements of the same length weigh differently.

The calculation procedure at the preliminary stage:

  1. Determine the developed area of the skin from the section drawing or 3D model.
  2. Multiply it by the wall thickness and the density of the material — roughly 2000 kg/m³.
  3. Add the weight of stiffening ribs and local thickenings at embedded parts.
  4. Add the weight of the steel frame and embedded parts, if provided.
  5. To calculate the load on the wall, add the weight of the subframe and fixings attributable to the product.

An example. A cornice with a projection of 300 mm and a height of 400 mm has a developed width of about 0.9 m per linear metre. With a 12 mm wall this gives approximately 0.9 × 0.012 × 2000 ≈ 22 kg, and with ribs and embedded parts roughly 25–30 kg per linear metre. The figures are notional: the manufacturer states the exact value in the working drawings once the section has been developed.

In practice the allowance for ribs, thickenings and metal ranges from a tenth to a half of the weight of the skin and depends on the format. It is smallest for small decorative elements and greatest for large-format panels on a steel frame. The input data for this calculation and its place in the design are described in the article on structural calculation of a ventilated facade.

How much do typical decorative elements weigh: indicative figures

For a preliminary assessment of load it is convenient to use indicative figures by product type. The values below were obtained by calculation for a 12–15 mm wall and do not replace the manufacturer's data: actual weight depends on the section, the length of the element and the method of fixing.

Note the difference between a panel without a frame and a panel on a steel frame: the metal can add half as much again, or more, to the weight of the skin. When comparing layout options, therefore, not only the number of products is counted but also the total mass of metal that will end up on the facade.

The table shows the general order of magnitude: most linear elements can be lifted and installed by two people. This is one of the reasons why GFRC cornices are used on high-rise buildings and in refurbishment, where massive equivalents would require strengthening of the wall. For large hollow products — such as columns made of GFRC — the weight of a single segment is limited at the design stage by dividing the shaft into parts of a height convenient for installation.

How product weight affects installation and the choice of plant

The mass of an individual element determines how it will be raised to level and how many people are needed to install it. The lighter the product, the simpler the organisation of the work: less dependence on a crane, a faster pace, lower demands on the site.

  • Up to 25–30 kg. The element is installed by hand by two fixers from scaffolding or a cradle. Most decorative elements and small panels are installed in this way.
  • From 30 to about 100 kg. A winch, pulley block or mini-crane on the roof is needed; installation is carried out by a gang of three or four.
  • Over 100 kg. Large-format framed panels are lifted by crane or lorry loader, using a spreader beam and the designated lifting points.

Weight also affects the means of access. The load on scaffold boards and the capacity of a cradle are limited, so the stock of products on a lift is calculated in advance and not simply stacked as far as space allows. The specifics of choosing between these means are considered in the article on scaffolding and facade hoists.

The designer takes this constraint into account when laying out the facade. Sometimes a panel is deliberately divided in two in order to avoid crane installation on a confined site; in other cases, on the contrary, elements are enlarged if there is a crane on site and the number of joints needs to be reduced. The decision is taken together with the installation contractor before the moulds go into production.

Lifting points are provided at the time of manufacture: a panel must not be lifted by its edges or at arbitrary points, as the thin skin is not designed for concentrated load.

Weight and transport: what limits the vehicle load

When GFRC products are transported, the limiting factor is usually not mass but volume and the method of stowage. Hollow decorative elements take up a great deal of space for little weight, so the vehicle is filled by volume before its payload is reached. Flat panels on A-frames, by contrast, are loaded densely, and here mass is what is counted.

The cost and organisation of delivery are affected by:

  • the dimensions of the largest element — these determine the type of vehicle and whether an abnormal load movement is needed;
  • the possibility of stacking in several tiers without loading the face surfaces;
  • the weight of packaging and stillages, which are returned to the factory;
  • the order of dispatch by section of the works, agreed with the installation programme.

Compared with natural stone for the same area of cladding, the tonnage to be transported is noticeably lower, which is significant for remote projects. On site the low weight allows products to be unloaded by forklift or by hand and stored on floor slabs without a check for concentrated load — but only after agreement with the main contractor.

For projects in remote regions dispatch is usually planned in consolidated consignments with a margin of time, so that installation does not depend on each individual vehicle. Marking of the products and a schedule showing the mass of each package simplify acceptance and the placing of the load in the site store.

Why does low weight matter in refurbishment and in seismic regions?

Lightweight cladding makes it possible to use elaborate decoration where the load-bearing capacity of the walls is limited, or where the mass of the facade directly increases the design loads on the building. These are two different cases, but the argument is the same in both: every kilogram on the facade must be carried by the fixing, the wall and the foundation.

In refurbishment the walls are often built of old brick or lightweight blocks whose strength is unknown and non-uniform. Recreating cornices and window surrounds in the original material would require strengthening of the masonry. Thin-walled hollow elements reproduce the original profile at a fraction of the mass, and the load-bearing capacity of anchors in the existing substrate is confirmed by tests on site — how this is done is described in the article on anchor pull-out tests. The general sequence of work on existing buildings is discussed in the article on facade refurbishment.

In seismic regions the inertial force acting on an element during an earthquake is proportional to its mass. Lightweight cladding produces smaller forces in the fixings and, if damaged, presents less danger to people below. Nevertheless the connections are designed for seismic actions and are made movable, so that the panels do not take up racking of the building frame. The requirements for such solutions are set out in the article on how a facade in a seismic region is designed.

There is a third situation as well — rooftop extensions and attic storeys, where the reserve of load-bearing capacity in the existing structure is small. For these, lightweight cladding is often the condition on which a decorative facade solution is possible at all.

Typical errors in accounting for cladding weight

Errors in estimating weight seldom lead to failure straight away, but they result in excess metal, reworking of the subframe or rejection at design review. The following are encountered most often.

  • Calculation by the «average square metre». The weight of decorative elements is spread over the whole facade area, although in fact it is concentrated at cornices and string courses and is transferred to individual brackets.
  • Weight of the skin instead of weight of the product. Ribs, frame and embedded parts are left out of the calculation, and the load is understated by tens of per cent.
  • Ignoring eccentricity. In elements with a projection the centre of gravity is offset from the wall, and the fixing works not only in shear but also in tension.
  • Dry weight without allowance for moisture. After rain the material weighs somewhat more, and snow or ice may lie on horizontal ledges.
  • No installation case. The product has been checked in its design position but not for lifting by its slinging points or for temporary support.
  • Catalogue data instead of the drawing. The weight is taken from a similar product by another manufacturer with a different wall thickness.

At concept stage indicative figures are sufficient, but the detailed design of the fixings is carried out only on the data of the specific manufacturer.

A reliable way to avoid these errors is to obtain from the manufacturer a schedule of products with the actual mass of each element and the coordinates of the fixing points before the subframe calculation begins, not after it.

FAQ

How much does 1 m² of GFRC weigh?

At a wall thickness of 10–15 mm, about 20–35 kg, excluding ribs, frame and fixings. The exact weight of each element is given in the working drawings.

How thick are GFRC elements?

Usually 10–20 mm for facade panels and decoration; for large vibration-cast elements and massive pieces the wall can be thicker.

How much lighter is GFRC than natural stone?

A 30 mm granite slab weighs about 80–85 kg/m², a 12–15 mm GFRC panel about 24–30 kg/m², i.e. roughly 2.5–3 times less. The difference is even greater for three-dimensional elements.

Can GFRC be fixed to an aerated concrete wall?

Yes, but only by calculation: anchor capacity in aerated concrete is confirmed by on-site pull-out tests, and bracket spacing is chosen taking into account element weight and wind load.

What is the density of GFRC?

Roughly 1900–2200 kg/m³; for sprayed material it is usually somewhat higher than for cast material. The exact value depends on the mix design and degree of compaction and is stated in the manufacturer's documents.

Can GFRC products be installed without a crane?

Most decorative elements and small panels weighing up to 25–30 kg are installed by hand from scaffolding or cradles. Winches and mini-cranes are used for heavier elements, and large-format panels on a steel frame are lifted by crane or lorry loader.

Does the wall need strengthening to carry GFRC decorative elements?

As a rule, no: the load from thin-walled products is small. However, the decision is taken on the basis of calculation and anchor test results, especially on walls of lightweight blocks, hollow brick and old masonry.
