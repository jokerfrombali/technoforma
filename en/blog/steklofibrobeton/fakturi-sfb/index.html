GFRC Textures: Smooth, Stone-Look, Acid-Etched

GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) textures are the face finishes of products: smooth, stone-look, acid-etched, sandblasted, relief or timber-look. Texture is built into the mould when it is made or created by surface treatment after demoulding. Thanks to its fine-grained mix, GFRC reproduces the mould pattern accurately, down to fine details.

The main criterion for choosing a texture is the architectural concept and service conditions: a smooth surface looks more austere and shows dirt more, while a textured one hides minor irregularities better and ages more gracefully. The decision is always confirmed on samples.

This article is useful for architects and clients at the finish selection stage. For a general overview of the material, see GFRC: material and technology.

How texture is created

GFRC texture is achieved in three main ways, often combined:

  1. Through the mould. The surface pattern is built into the model and transferred to the mould. Silicone and polyurethane moulds reproduce the finest relief — split stone, wood grain, ornament. Smooth fibreglass or steel moulds give an even surface.
  2. Through the face-coat mix. The first layer applied to the mould can differ from the backing mix: white cement, pigments, decorative aggregate — stone chippings, quartz sand of various grades.
  3. Through treatment after demoulding. Acid etching, sandblasting, grinding and sometimes bush-hammering. These operations remove the top cement skin and expose the aggregate, giving the surface the look of natural stone.

At TECHNOFORMA Group, models and moulds are made in its own mould-making shops, so a texture can be developed for a specific project.

Texture affects not only appearance but also performance. Open pores and deep relief retain more dust and moisture, so such surfaces particularly need water-repellent treatment. Smooth surfaces are easier to clean, but efflorescence, streaks and shade variation are more visible on them. This is worth considering when choosing a texture for a particular area of the facade.

Stone and timber effects

Stone-look GFRC is one of the most popular solutions. To imitate a particular stone, several parameters are matched at once: the mould relief (taken from a natural sample), cement colour and pigments, aggregate grade and colour, and the surface treatment method. Imitations of porous and sedimentary stones — travertine, limestone, sandstone, shell limestone (coquina) — are especially successful, since their texture is naturally conveyed by etching and mould relief.

Travertine-look GFRC is a frequent request. Natural travertine is a sedimentary rock with characteristic pores and a banded texture; GFRC reproduces this pattern using a mould taken from the stone and a matched warm shade. The limitation is imitating crystalline stones with depth of colour: polished granite and marble are hard to replicate in concrete, and natural stone is better for such tasks. TECHNOFORMA Group also supplies natural stones, including travertine, marble and granite.

A timber texture is achieved with moulds carrying a grain pattern taken from real boards. Such products are used for benches, street furniture and accent panels. Unlike timber, GFRC does not rot and needs no moisture-protection impregnation.

Samples and approval

Texture and colour are the most subjective properties of a product, so they are agreed in advance and documented:

  1. Matching to references. The architect provides a stone sample, photo, colour code or description.
  2. Trial samples. The manufacturer makes several texture and shade options on conveniently sized samples.
  3. Assessment in real conditions. Samples are viewed outdoors in daylight, dry and wet — concrete surfaces darken after rain.
  4. Approval of the reference sample. The chosen sample is signed and kept by the client and at the factory. Serial products are accepted against it.
  5. A trial product or facade mock-up — for large projects it is useful to install a fragment to assess joints and the overall look.

As part of design, TECHNOFORMA Group provides 3D visualisation, which helps to assess the combination of textures across the whole facade even before samples are made.

How texture affects price

No specific prices are given in this article: product cost is determined by the project. The factors through which texture affects price are:

  • Mould. Complex relief requires a more labour-intensive model and a flexible mould, which wears faster than a rigid one.
  • Face coat. White cement, pigments and decorative aggregate cost more than standard components.
  • Treatment. Etching, sandblasting and grinding are extra operations and time.
  • Number of variants. The more textures and colours on a project, the more complex production and quality control become.
  • Protective treatment — water repellents and anti-graffiti coatings.

A smooth or concrete texture is usually the most economical. For residential complexes with large volumes, it is best to limit the number of textures to two or three and use them serially. TECHNOFORMA Group calculates the cost for your project from drawings or after a site survey.

Which texture to choose for different facade zones

A single texture for the whole building is a rare case: the lower storeys are viewed close up and washed more often, while the upper ones are seen from a distance and in raking light. The surface is therefore selected by zone, based on viewing distance, soiling and mechanical impact.

ZoneConditionsWhat usually suits
Plinth and ground floorSplashes, de-icing agents, touch, close viewingDense fine texture, etched or stone-effect; deep relief only above the soiling zone
Entrance areas and portalsAttention to detail, lightingExpressive surfaces: stone-effect, ornament, a combination of smooth and textured
Standard planes of typical floorsLarge area, viewed from 20–50 mSmooth, plain concrete or light sandblasted; a fine pattern cannot be read from a distance
Cornices, string courses, window surroundsWater runs, shadows from the profileSmooth or lightly etched: the profile itself creates the modelling
Top of the building, atticWind, precipitation, distant viewingBold relief and enlarged details, surfaces that are easy to maintain

The transition between zones is best tied to architectural divisions such as a string course, a cornice or a change of material, rather than run across the middle of a plane.

Relief on wall planes is conveniently created by the products themselves: GFRC rustication and pilasters divide the facade with shadows and at the same time conceal the joints between elements.

How texture relates to the mould and the thickness of the product

Not every pattern can be cast in a thin shell: the depth and character of the relief are limited by the forming method and the skin thickness. This is worth bearing in mind at sketch stage, so that the texture does not have to be changed once tooling is under way.

The main technological constraints:

  • Depth of relief. The skin thickness must be maintained across the whole pattern, so a deep texture means either a thicker product or a shell that follows the relief on the rear side.
  • Draft angles and undercuts. A product will leave a rigid mould only if draft angles are provided; undercuts require an elastic insert or a split mould.
  • Sharp arrises. Thin projecting edges fill poorly with fibre and chip in transport, so they are rounded to a radius of a few millimetres.
  • Face coat. Etching and sandblasting remove part of the surface, so a face coat of sufficient thickness with a selected aggregate is provided for them.

How the tooling material affects detail and service life is explained in the article on moulds for GFRC. Separate rules apply to three-dimensional patterns and parametric relief; they are covered in the article on 3D GFRC panels.

Layout, joints and pattern repeat

A stone or timber texture is taken from a finite number of samples, so on a large plane the pattern inevitably repeats. To keep the repeat from catching the eye, the layout of textured products is thought through as carefully as the layout of natural stone slabs.

Techniques used by designers and production engineers:

  • several mould variants of one texture, usually three to five, alternated across the facade;
  • rotating products through 180° where the pattern allows it;
  • inserts with a neutral surface between large textured fields;
  • aligning joints with the natural lines of the pattern: rustication joints, the joints between ‘blocks’, mouldings;
  • separate corner elements in which the texture returns on to the edge without a break.

The joints themselves are also part of the pattern. A wide open joint emphasises the module and suits contemporary facades; a narrow filled joint suits classical ones, where the look of solid masonry is wanted. The sealant colour is chosen from a sample together with the texture, otherwise a light or dark grid of joints will alter the overall impression of the surface.

For textures with a pronounced direction, such as wood grain, bedded stone or fluting, the drawings indicate the orientation of the pattern on every product. Otherwise some elements will be installed upside down and bands of a different tone will appear on the plane.

GFRC texture alongside natural stone and other materials

On many projects GFRC works together with natural stone, porcelain stoneware or render, and the texture of the products is matched to its neighbours. Two strategies are possible: to achieve similarity or, on the contrary, to build the facade on a contrast of surfaces.

When working tone-on-tone, a mould is taken from the stone and its pattern is reproduced in the ornament, so that GFRC cornices and surrounds continue the stone cladding of the walls. It is important to compare samples with the stone finish specified in the design: polished, honed and bush-hammered surfaces of the same stone look like different materials. In a contrasting scheme, smooth concrete is placed next to split-faced or relief stone, and textured panels next to glass and metal.

The boundary between materials calls for a constructional solution: a joint, a step in plane or a dividing profile. An attempt to butt two similar but not identical surfaces in one plane without a joint usually emphasises the difference between them.

How does a textured surface change over time?

Any GFRC texture acquires a patina over time, but different surfaces age differently: smooth ones show runs and stains, while relief ones collect dust in the recesses and yet look more even. The rate of change is determined by how water is shed and by the condition of the water-repellent protection.

Points to watch in design and in service:

  • horizontal ledges of the relief and the tops of projecting details must fall outwards and have a drip, otherwise water leaves dark tracks on the plane below;
  • on north-facing and shaded areas a porous texture stays damp for longer, and biological growth is possible there;
  • etched and sandblasted surfaces fade and soil evenly, so ageing is less noticeable on them;
  • intensive high-pressure washing can alter the texture, removing the cement skin and lightening the area.

Washing methods are matched to the type of surface; general recommendations are collected in the article on cleaning stone and concrete facades. After cleaning, the water-repellent impregnation is renewed, particularly on open porous textures.

Texture and light: daylight and architectural lighting

Relief can be read only in side light: in overcast weather and under frontal lighting a textured panel looks almost flat. The depth of the pattern is therefore chosen with regard to the orientation of the facade and to how the building will be lit in the evening.

On south and west elevations even fine relief gives expressive shadows through the day. On north elevations, which receive almost no direct sun, fine texture is lost, and what works there is bold division, contrast between smooth and rough areas, or a difference in tone. In the evening the picture is set by the luminaires: raking light from below or above strengthens the pattern and at the same time reveals any unevenness and any difference in plane between adjacent products.

If architectural facade lighting is planned for the building, texture samples should also be checked under directional light. The same applies to installation accuracy: in areas with raking light the steps between elements are held to tighter limits. Large illuminated GFRC facade panels are usually checked on a control mock-up.

Typical mistakes in choosing a texture

Mistakes with texture are seldom related to casting quality; more often the surface has been chosen without reference to scale and service conditions.

  • Fine pattern at height. A delicate texture above the third or fourth floor cannot be made out from the ground, yet it increases the cost of the mould.
  • Deep relief near the ground. Dirt from splashes collects in the recesses and the lower tier darkens quickly.
  • Too many textures on one facade. Each surface needs its own moulds and samples; two or three are usually enough.
  • One mould for a large plane. The pattern repeat becomes noticeable within as few as ten panels.
  • Judging a sample lying on a table. A vertical surface in outdoor light looks different from a horizontal one indoors.

Most of these mistakes can be avoided if, before tooling starts, a mock-up of several products is assembled on site or in the workshop and viewed from the real distance at different times of day.

FAQ

What kind of stone is travertine?

A sedimentary rock formed from mineral spring deposits, with characteristic pores and a banded texture. It is used for facade and interior cladding.

Is travertine suitable for facades?

Yes, travertine is used for facade cladding; its properties depend on the quarry, so frost resistance and water absorption are checked against the batch documents. TECHNOFORMA Group supplies travertine.

Can GFRC be made to look like travertine?

Yes: the mould is taken from natural stone, and the face coat and pigments are matched to the required shade. The result is a lightweight product with a travertine texture.

How does flexible travertine-look stone differ from GFRC?

Flexible stone is a thin layer of stone chippings or a stone veneer on a flexible backing, used mainly for surface finishing. GFRC produces rigid products of any shape: panels, cornices, columns.

Which GFRC texture should I choose for a facade?

For restrained architecture — smooth or concrete; for classical styles — acid-etched or stone-look. The decision is confirmed on samples viewed outdoors in daylight.

Can different textures be produced on one product?

Yes. Smooth and relief areas are combined in one mould, and after demoulding individual zones are additionally etched or sandblasted through a mask. The boundary between textures is usually aligned with a rustication joint or a step in the profile so that the transition looks neat.

Which GFRC texture shows dirt least?

Soiling is most visible on smooth light surfaces. A fine etched or sandblasted texture masks dust and runs better, while deep relief collects dirt in the recesses. In any case, soiling is reduced by water-repellent treatment and by shedding water correctly.

How long does it take to develop a new texture?

The time depends on the complexity of the pattern: a model has to be made, a mould taken from it and trial samples cast for approval. The manufacturer states specific times after studying the references; ready textures from the catalogue go into production faster.
