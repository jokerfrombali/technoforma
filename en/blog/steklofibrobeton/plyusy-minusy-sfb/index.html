Pros and Cons of GFRC for Facades

Updated: 2026-09-27 · TECHNOFORMA editorial team

The main advantages of glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) for facades are freedom of form, low weight with a thin wall, non-combustibility and weather resistance. The main disadvantages are the strong dependence of quality on the manufacturer, the cost of custom moulds in small runs and the demanding design of fixing details.

In other words, the material itself rarely fails; the mix design, the production process and the installation do. That is why the decision "GFRC or not" almost always comes down to two questions: does the architecture justify the cost of moulds, and can the manufacturer be trusted.

This guide is useful for architects and developers choosing cladding for a residential complex or a public building, and for private clients with a classical facade design. For a general overview of the material, see GFRC: material and technology.

Advantages

The advantages of GFRC show above all where the facade is rich in architectural detail:

  • Freedom of form. Panels of any curvature, complex cornice profiles, columns, capitals, reliefs and ornaments. The shape is defined by the model, not by the limits of formwork and rebar.
  • Low weight. Thanks to fibre reinforcement, the wall of an element is typically 10–20 mm. Hollow columns and cornices weigh several times less than solid equivalents, which reduces the load on the substructure, anchors and wall.
  • Non-combustibility. According to TECHNOFORMA Group, its GFRC is rated non-combustible (NG class under the Russian classification). This matters for high-rise housing and public buildings.
  • Frost and weather resistance. The mineral material is not affected by UV, moisture or temperature swings; according to the company, frost resistance is improved with admixtures.
  • No corrosion. GFRC contains no steel reinforcement that could rust if the cover is damaged.
  • Wide range of textures and colours. Smooth surface, stone imitation, acid etching, integral pigment.
  • One material for the whole facade. Panels, decorative elements and small architectural forms share one style and colour.
  • Serial production. One mould yields dozens or hundreds of identical pieces, which pays off on residential complexes with repeating sections.

The textures most often ordered for facades are smooth (for painting or with integral pigment), stone-look (sandstone, limestone, travertine), etched with exposed aggregate, and relief to a custom model. The choice of texture affects not only appearance but also soiling: smooth light surfaces at pedestrian level need a protective impregnation and regular washing, while textured ones hide dust better.

Disadvantages and risks

The disadvantages of GFRC are more often linked not to the material but to how it is produced and used:

  • Dependence on process. Ordinary glass fibre instead of alkali-resistant fibre, the wrong fibre content, excess water or drying out in the first day reduce strength. Defects may appear months or years later.
  • Flexural strength decreases over time. This property is accounted for in design by using long-term values with a safety margin.
  • Mould cost. A unique element needs a model and a mould; with a run of just a few pieces, this noticeably increases the unit price.
  • Demanding fixing details. Thin panels are sensitive to errors in the design of fixings, movement joints and water drainage. A wrong detail can lead to cracks.
  • Efflorescence and soiling. Like any cement-based material, GFRC without protective treatment can show efflorescence and absorb dirt.
  • Repair. Chips and cracks can be repaired, but matching colour and texture perfectly is harder than replacing a standard tile.
  • Not a structural material. GFRC is cladding and decoration, not structure.

Some myths that come up in discussions of the material:

  • "GFRC is just thin concrete." No: the flexural strength of the thin wall comes from the fibre; without it an element of that thickness would not work. Properties depend on the content and type of fibre, not only on the cement.
  • "All GFRC is the same." Products from different manufacturers of the same thickness can differ noticeably in strength, which is exactly why test reports per batch are needed.
  • "The glass fibre will dissolve over time." Ordinary glass does degrade in a cement matrix; that is why alkali-resistant fibre is used and the residual loss of strength is built into the design.
  • "Since the material is light, the fixings can be simplified." Wind load on a panel does not depend on its weight; fixings are designed for wind, self-weight and thermal movement.

A real disadvantage that is rarely mentioned is lead time: a custom mould and curing take time, so GFRC is poorly suited to a situation where "the cladding is needed in two weeks" unless the elements are standard.

Where GFRC is not the best choice

GFRC is not a universal solution. It loses to other materials in several situations:

  • Large flat surfaces without decoration. If the facade consists of plain planes, porcelain stoneware or fibre cement boards are usually simpler and cheaper: they are mass-produced without custom moulds.
  • One-off unique elements in small numbers. One or two non-standard pieces are cheaper to make from another material or to cut from stone.
  • When genuine natural material is required. If the architect or client wants actual granite, travertine or marble with a natural structure, GFRC remains an imitation.
  • Abrasion loads. Steps, floors and paving with heavy traffic are jobs for stone or special concretes.
  • Interior decoration in dry rooms. For ceiling mouldings, gypsum or polyurethane is lighter and cheaper; GFRC is justified where strength and moisture resistance matter.

The optimum is often a combination: porcelain stoneware or stone for the planes, GFRC for the architectural details.

How to reduce the risks

Most risks are removed at the stage of choosing the contractor and designing the facade:

  1. Check the production. An in-house mould shop, quality control at every stage, test reports on samples for flexural strength and frost resistance.
  2. Request documents for the fibre confirming its alkali resistance.
  3. Commission a ventilated rainscreen facade design. Panel layout, fixing details, movement joints, drainage from horizontal elements, structural calculation of the substructure.
  4. Approve a reference sample of colour and texture before serial production starts.
  5. Specify water-repellent treatment of exposed faces: it reduces water absorption and soiling.
  6. Entrust installation to one company. When design, production and installation are done by one contractor, responsibility for the result is not diluted.

TECHNOFORMA Group works on a full cycle: design department, in-house mould shops, production, logistics and its own installation teams of more than 100 specialists.

Large panels are never left hanging on a single fixing point.

A short checklist for making the decision in practice:

  1. Estimate what share of the facade is architectural detail. If it is small, GFRC makes sense only for those details.
  2. Assess repetition: how many identical elements one mould will produce. Dozens or hundreds of repeats are a strong argument for GFRC.
  3. Check the load on the wall and substructure: if heavy stone requires strengthening, lightweight GFRC may turn out cheaper for the system as a whole.
  4. Compare not the price per square metre of cladding but the cost of the whole facade: elements, substructure, installation, schedule.
  5. Ask the manufacturer for test reports, a texture sample and examples of completed projects.

It makes no sense to estimate GFRC cladding "per metre" without a design: the price depends on the complexity of shapes, the number of element types, the texture and the scope of installation. Pros and cons of GFRC on different types of building

The strengths and limitations of GFRC show themselves differently depending on the type of building. Where there are many repeating details and strict fire safety requirements, the material comes fully into its own, whereas on a small project with unique decor the cost of tooling comes to the fore. It should therefore be assessed in relation to a specific task.

Type of projectWhat works in its favourWhat to take into account
Multi-storey residential complexRepetition of elements floor by floor, low load on the frame, non-combustibilityCoordination of deliveries with the installation programme, a large number of fixing points
Business centreLarge-format panels, complex modelling, precise geometryHigh demands on colour consistency across large surfaces
Hotel, resortRich decor, stone imitation, moisture resistanceBespoke moulds and sample approval lengthen the preparation
Public building, university, cultural venueNon-combustibility, durability, reproduction of historic profilesStrict design documentation and staged acceptance
Private houseLightweight decor without strengthening walls, any architectural styleSmall run: the cost of the mould is spread over a small number of items

On high-rise buildings weight becomes decisive: a light shell reduces the load on the subframe and floor slabs and simplifies lifting. On low-rise projects this advantage is less noticeable, but detailing and texture, which are viewed at close range, matter more. In the refurbishment of historic facades, the ability to take a mould from a surviving element and repeat the profile without adding weight to old walls is valued.

Flat areas are usually clad with items of a regular format — such solutions are described on the page GFRC facade panels — while crowning and inter-storey profiles are made as GFRC cornices. Dividing the facade into planes and details helps to see more precisely where the material gives the greatest return.

There is also a general indicator by which feasibility can be assessed in advance: the share of architectural details in the facade area and the number of repeats of each element. If one cornice profile runs around the whole perimeter and window surrounds are identical on dozens of floors, the tooling pays for itself quickly and the strengths of the material prevail. If, however, each element is unique and occurs once or twice, it is more sensible to simplify the decor, reduce the number of types or consider natural stone for individual accent details.

How strengths and weaknesses appear at each stage of a project

The advantages and disadvantages of the material are distributed unevenly across the stages: most of the time is spent on preparation, while most of the return comes during installation and service. Understanding this helps to build the programme correctly.

  1. Design. Pro — the facade can be divided into elements of almost any shape. Con — the layout, details and embedded parts must be worked out before production starts; changing them later is difficult.
  2. Tooling. Pro — the mould reproduces the model precisely and is used many times. Con — before the first casting, time is needed for the model, the mould and sample approval.
  3. Production. Pro — factory conditions give consistent thickness and texture. Con — the item has to gain strength, and this process cannot be accelerated without loss of quality.
  4. Logistics. Pro — a lorry carries more square metres of cladding than when transporting stone or ordinary concrete. Con — thin-walled elements require rigid packaging and careful unloading.
  5. Installation. Pro — light elements are fixed from cradles and scaffolding, often without a heavy crane. Con — a team familiar with sliding fixings and tolerances is needed.
  6. Service. Pro — the material does not burn, rot or corrode. Con — light surfaces without protection collect dirt over time.

The conclusion for the client is simple: the decision to use GFRC is best taken at concept or design stage rather than when the frame has already been erected and only a few weeks remain for finishing. With an early start the drawback associated with lead times practically disappears, because tooling and the first batches are prepared in parallel with construction of the building.

In practice, programme overruns are more often caused not by production itself but by late changes. Typical situations: the architect adjusts a profile after the mould has been made, the client changes the shade after the sample has been approved, deviations of the frame not allowed for in the layout are discovered on site. Each such correction sends the project back one or two stages. Before tooling is launched it is therefore useful to carry out an as-built survey of the building, fix the profiles and sign off the colour master sample — these three steps remove most of the risks to the programme.

GFRC and other materials for facade decor: which to choose?

Among materials for architectural details, GFRC stands out for its combination of non-combustibility, low weight and the durability of a mineral base; each alternative has an area in which it is more practical. The comparison below is a guide, not a substitute for design calculation.

MaterialWhere it outperforms GFRCWhere it falls short
Natural stoneNatural pattern, prestige, a very long service lifeMass, labour-intensive complex profiles, format limitations
Coated expanded polystyreneLow cost, quick adhesive fixingCombustible core, low impact resistance, short-lived coating
PolyurethaneLightness, crisp fine relief, ready-made catalogueCombustibility, sensitivity to ultraviolet, a plastic look at close range
GypsumFine modelling, simple restoration indoorsNot water-resistant: of limited use outdoors
Polymer concreteDense surface, low water absorptionPolymer binder, greater mass of the item
Precast architectural concreteMassiveness, its own load-bearing capacityThick wall, heavy elements and fixings

Detailed analyses are given in separate articles: GFRC or natural stone and the comparison with polystyrene decor. The practical conclusion is this: on projects with fire safety requirements and a design service life of decades, lightweight polymer solutions usually drop out, and the choice comes down to stone, architectural concrete and GFRC.

Between these three options the decision is most often determined by geometry. The more curved surfaces, deep profiles and repeating relief there are on the facade, the more evident the benefit of a thin-walled moulded item.

Quite often the materials do not compete but complement one another. The plinth and entrance areas, which are subject to impact and abrasion, are clad in granite or dense limestone, while the upper floors with cornices, string courses and capitals are made of GFRC with a texture imitating the same stone. In this way a single image of the building is preserved, the load on the structure is reduced and less expensive stone is used where its merits can no longer be seen from the ground.

How climate affects the strengths and weaknesses of the material

In any climate GFRC remains a mineral material with high frost resistance, but the emphasis in the design shifts. In the south colour fastness and thermal movement matter more, in the north water run-off and joint quality, and by the sea protection of metal fixings.

  • Hot climate and strong insolation. Dark surfaces heat up noticeably, so long elements are divided into parts with movement joints, and lightfast pigments are selected.
  • Coastal areas. The material itself is resistant to salt mist; the embedded parts and subframe are vulnerable, so stainless steel or a reliable anti-corrosion coating is chosen for them.
  • Areas with frequent freeze-thaw cycles. The main thing is to prevent water standing on horizontal ledges and in joints: falls, drips and sealing are required.
  • Seismic regions. The low mass of the cladding reduces inertial loads on the fixings, which is one of the advantages of lightweight suspended systems.
  • Industrial and traffic-laden cities. Dust and soot settle on textured surfaces, so water-repellent treatment and access for washing are provided for in advance.

Numerical values — flexural strength, water absorption, frost resistance grade — depend on the mix and the forming method and are confirmed by test reports. Reference figures are given in the article on GFRC properties.

Climate also affects the organisation of work. Casting and strength gain take place in the workshop and do not depend on the weather, which is one of the advantages of factory production. Installation on mechanical fixings is possible all year round in most cases, whereas joint sealing, repair compounds and the application of protective coatings are carried out at the temperatures specified by the manufacturer of those materials. In the programme such operations are scheduled for the warm season, or temporary enclosure is provided for them.

How to recognise a quality item: signs at acceptance

Many of the so-called drawbacks of the material are in fact defects of a particular batch, and most of them are visible at acceptance. Inspecting the items before installation takes little time and avoids disputes on the facade.

  • Wall thickness. At the ends and at technological openings it must correspond to the drawing and be uniform, without thinning at the corners.
  • Face surface. Small pores characteristic of the texture are acceptable; cracks, voids with exposed fibre and delamination are not.
  • Colour. Items of one batch are compared with the approved master sample in daylight; slight shade variation is inherent in cement-based materials and is agreed in advance.
  • Geometry. Length, diagonals, straightness of edges and the fit of adjacent elements are checked.
  • Embedded parts. Position as per the drawing, metal protected against corrosion, no cracks around the embedded parts.
  • Marking and packing. Each element bears a number from the layout, is laid on spacers and protected along the edges.
  • Documents. A batch certificate and test reports for samples accompany the batch.

If an item raises doubts, it is better to set it aside before installation: replacing an element on the ground is incomparably easier than after it has been fixed and the joints sealed.

For complex details — corners, the junction of a cornice with a pilaster, joints between curved panels — a trial assembly of several elements at the factory or a facade mock-up on site is useful. On such a mock-up the alignment of relief at the joints, the joint width, the performance of the fixings and the overall appearance in natural light are checked. Comments arising from the mock-up are dealt with in the tooling before the whole series is produced, and the mock-up itself then serves as the reference at acceptance.

What to include in the specification and the contract

Risks associated with lead times and quality are removed by the wording of the specification: the more precisely the result is described, the fewer discrepancies arise at acceptance. For a facade with complex decor this matters more than for standard cladding.

  • scope of work and limits of responsibility: design, production, delivery, installation;
  • a master sample of texture and colour signed by the parties;
  • tolerances for dimensions, gaps and shade variation;
  • requirements for embedded parts and the subframe — material and anti-corrosion protection;
  • a programme by stage: tooling, sample, batches of items, installation by section;
  • the procedure for acceptance and replacement of defective items;
  • storage conditions on site and responsibility for safekeeping;
  • warranty obligations and care recommendations.

When design, production and installation are assigned to a single contractor, the chain of responsibility is shorter: there is no dispute over whose error caused an element not to match a fixing detail. With separate contracts it is particularly important that the installation company receives the working drawings of the layout and details before deliveries begin.

A separate clause should cover a reserve of items in case of damage during transport and installation, as well as the period for which the moulds are kept after deliveries are complete. Retained tooling makes it possible to produce a replacement element several years later without repeating the cost of the model. For projects built in phases it is also a way of maintaining a uniform appearance of blocks erected at different times.

What happens to a GFRC facade after years in service?

With the correct mix and installation, a GFRC facade ages in the same way as quality concrete or natural stone: it is not the load-bearing capacity that changes but the appearance of the surface. The main processes are soiling, possible efflorescence in the first seasons and gradual weathering of the water-repellent protection.

Maintenance comes down to a few actions: periodic inspection of joints and sealant, washing as dirt accumulates, renewal of the water repellent and local repair of chips. One of the advantages is repairability: an individual element can be removed and replaced without dismantling the whole facade, if the fixing details allow it. One of the drawbacks is that a new part may differ in shade from its neighbours, so it is useful to order a small reserve of items from the same batch during production.

What durability depends on and how to extend it is examined in detail in the article on the service life of GFRC.

FAQ

What are the pros and cons of a ventilated rainscreen facade?

Pros: moisture removal through the ventilated cavity, external insulation, a wide choice of cladding, easy repair. Cons: higher cost than a render-on-insulation facade and the need for an accurate design of the substructure and details.

What are the pros and cons of shell limestone (coquina)?

Shell limestone is light, porous, easy to work and has a recognisable texture. The downsides are modest strength, high water absorption and properties that depend on the quarry, so on facades it is usually protected with water repellents.

What are the pros and cons of Dagestan stone?

Dagestan limestones and dolomites are valued for their warm tones and good workability. Properties depend on the specific quarry, so density, strength and frost resistance must be checked against the batch documents. TECHNOFORMA Group has extensive experience with Dagestan stones.

What is the main disadvantage of GFRC?

The strong dependence of quality on the manufacturer: the mix design, the alkali resistance of the fibre and the curing regime determine durability, and defects do not show up immediately.

Is GFRC cost-effective for a private house?

Yes, if the facade is rich in decoration such as cornices, columns and surrounds: GFRC gives a stone look without a heavy load on the walls. For a simple flat facade, other cladding options are more sensible.

What are the most common mistakes when installing GFRC?

Rigid fixing without sliding supports, joints packed with mortar, no fall or drip on horizontal elements, cutting elements to fit on site and installing elements that have not reached strength. All of these lead to cracks and chips; they are prevented by a proper ventilated facade design and by following the manufacturer's fixing scheme.

Is GFRC suitable for high-rise buildings?

Yes, the low mass of the cladding is one of the main advantages of the material at height: the load on the subframe and floor slabs is reduced and lifting is simpler. At the same time the calculation of fixings for wind load and supervision of installation become more important, so the details are developed as part of the facade design rather than selected on site.

Does the colour of a GFRC facade change over time?

Integral pigments are, as a rule, lightfast, and the colour itself lasts a long time. Visually the surface may change owing to soiling and efflorescence, especially in the first seasons. Water-repellent treatment and well-considered water run-off reduce this effect, and washing restores the original appearance.

At what stage of a project should the decision to use GFRC be taken?

Ideally at concept or design documentation stage. The layout of elements, fixing details and loads are then taken into account in the building design, and models and moulds are prepared in parallel with construction. With a late decision the material can still be used, but the tooling lead time directly postpones the start of installation.
