GFRC vs Fibre-Reinforced Concrete: What Is the Difference

The difference between fibre-reinforced concrete and glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) is that of general and specific. Fibre-reinforced concrete is any concrete reinforced with dispersed fibres: steel, basalt, polypropylene or glass. GFRC is a type of fibre-reinforced concrete that uses alkali-resistant glass fibre and a fine-grained matrix without crushed stone.

The type of fibre determines the purpose. Steel fibre increases the crack resistance and impact strength of massive structures — floors, slabs, tunnel linings. Polypropylene mainly reduces shrinkage cracking. Alkali-resistant glass fibre makes it possible to produce thin, light elements with high flexural strength — facade panels and decoration.

This article is for those choosing a facade material who want to understand the difference between offers on the market. A general overview is in GFRC: material and technology.

Types of fibre

Fibres are short filaments evenly distributed in concrete that act as three-dimensional reinforcement. Several main types are used on the market:

  • Steel fibre — lengths of wire with hooked ends, crimped or milled. Usually 30–60 mm long. Used in structural concrete.
  • Polypropylene microfibre — fine synthetic fibres a few millimetres long. Reduces plastic shrinkage and cracking of screeds, renders and thin layers.
  • Polymer macrofibre — thicker synthetic fibres that partly replace steel fibre in floors and shotcrete.
  • Basalt fibre — mineral fibre made from molten basalt, used in renders, screeds and some structural concretes.
  • Glass fibre — for concrete, only alkali-resistant (zirconia AR glass); ordinary glass breaks down in the alkaline cement environment.

Concretes with these fibres are called steel fibre reinforced concrete, basalt fibre reinforced concrete, polypropylene fibre reinforced concrete and glass fibre reinforced concrete respectively.

Besides the fibre material, its length, diameter, shape and dosage affect the result. Fibres that are too short hold cracks poorly; fibres that are too long ball up during mixing. So manufacturers select fibre for the specific technology: for spray-up, glass fibre is chopped from roving directly in the gun; for vibration casting, ready-chopped fibre is used.

Glass fibre vs steel and polypropylene fibre

Each type of fibre changes the properties of concrete in its own way:

  • Steel fibre has a high modulus of elasticity and strength and resists crack opening well in thick sections. Its drawbacks for facades are the heavy weight of the material, the risk of fibre corrosion near the surface (rust spots) and the impossibility of thin walls: steel fibre needs coarse aggregate and a thickness of several centimetres.
  • Polypropylene fibre is light and does not corrode but has a low modulus of elasticity. It hardly increases the flexural strength of hardened concrete and works mainly in the first hours of curing against shrinkage cracks. In addition, polypropylene is a combustible polymer.
  • Alkali-resistant glass fibre combines high tensile strength with a mineral nature. In a fine-grained matrix at a content of a few percent, it gives high flexural strength at a thickness of 10–15 mm. The material does not corrode and, according to TECHNOFORMA Group, its GFRC is classed as non-combustible (NG).

For thin-walled facade elements it is glass fibre that provides the right combination of strength, lightness and durability. Polypropylene microfibre is sometimes added to GFRC as well, but it cannot replace glass fibre.

What is used where

The applications of different fibre-reinforced concretes barely overlap:

  • Steel fibre reinforced concrete — industrial floors, road and airfield pavements, tunnel linings, shotcrete, foundation slabs, structural repairs.
  • Concrete with polypropylene fibre — screeds, renders, paving slabs, small elements where reducing shrinkage cracking matters.
  • Basalt fibre reinforced concrete — screeds, render and repair mortars, some structural applications.
  • GFRC — facade panels and decoration, columns, cornices, window surrounds, plinth cladding, small architectural forms, interior elements, permanent formwork, noise barriers.

When people talk about fibre-reinforced concrete for facades, they in fact mean GFRC: the other fibre types cannot produce a thin, light and detailed element.

Choosing for a facade

For rainscreen cladding and facade decoration, the choice is usually GFRC. The reasons:

  1. Thin wall and low weight — less load on the substructure and wall.
  2. Accurate reproduction of shape and texture thanks to the fine-grained mix.
  3. No corrosion — no risk of rust stains on the face.
  4. Non-combustible cladding material.

When choosing a supplier, check exactly which fibre is used. Under the name "fibre concrete for facades", mixes with polypropylene fibre or ordinary glass fibre are sometimes offered — they are cheaper but do not deliver the strength and durability of genuine GFRC. Ask for documents on the alkali-resistant fibre and flexural test reports.

If the project needs both facade decoration and massive elements — for example, entrance steps or car park floors — it makes sense to use different materials for different purposes rather than one compromise.

How fibre dosage and orientation change the properties of fibre-reinforced concrete

The properties of fibre-reinforced concrete depend not only on the type of fibre but also on its quantity, length and position within the section. The same material behaves differently at different dosages: at a low content the fibre merely restrains shrinkage cracking, while at a high content it takes up tensile forces after the first crack has formed.

In GFRC the fibre content is, as a rule, higher than in other fibre-reinforced concretes. With the spray method it is usually around 4–5% of the mix by weight, and with premix casting about 2–3.5%; the exact figures are set by the manufacturer's mix design. In structural concrete, polypropylene microfibre is added in far smaller amounts — hundreds of grams per cubic metre — so it has almost no effect on the strength of the hardened material.

The second variable is orientation. When sprayed, the fibres lie in layers in the plane of the skin, which is exactly where the element works in bending. In a cast mix the fibre is distributed randomly through the volume, and only part of it works in the required direction. For the same composition, sprayed GFRC therefore usually shows higher flexural strength than cast GFRC; the two methods are covered in more detail in the article on GFRC production technology.

Fibre-reinforced concrete, fibre cement and reinforced concrete: what is most often confused

Commercial offers use similar names for different materials. To compare them properly it is enough to understand what the material is reinforced with and how a product is made from it.

Fibre cement boards are a cladding class of their own: they are flat, cut to size and fixed to a substructure, but relief and three-dimensional form cannot be obtained from them. The features of this material are discussed in the article on fibre cement panels for ventilated facades.

The difference between fibre-reinforced concrete and reinforced concrete lies in the reinforcement principle. Bar reinforcement works in the design zones and needs a concrete cover of several centimetres, so a thin skin is impossible with it. Dispersed fibre is distributed across the whole section and needs no cover — this is what makes it possible to form shells 10–20 mm thick.

How to specify GFRC in the design and the technical brief

The wording “fibre-reinforced concrete” in a specification leaves the contractor too wide a choice. To make sure the material delivered to site is the one the facade was designed for, the brief should fix several parameters.

  • Fibre type: alkali-resistant glass fibre, confirmed by a document from the fibre manufacturer.
  • Forming method: spraying, vibration casting or a combination of the two for different product types.
  • Skin thickness and permitted tolerances, presence of stiffening ribs and embedded parts.
  • Required values: flexural strength, density, water absorption, frost resistance — with the test method stated.
  • Texture and colour to an approved reference sample, and the colouring method.
  • Weight of the products by type — needed to design the substructure and anchors.

The specific figures for strength and frost resistance are set by the designer on the basis of calculation and regional conditions. Typical ranges are summarised in the article on the properties of GFRC, and the mix design is covered separately in the article on the mix composition and the role of each component.

Which tests confirm that the material is GFRC?

It is difficult to tell GFRC from a mix with polypropylene fibre by the appearance of the finished product: the face surface may look the same. The difference is shown by sample testing and production control.

  1. Flexural testing of coupons cut from a test board made during the same shift. With GFRC the load keeps rising after the first crack appears, whereas a concrete without working reinforcement fails at once.
  2. Checking the fibre content of the fresh mix — the wash-out test used with the spray method.
  3. Determining density and water absorption: excessive water absorption points to poor compaction or surplus water.
  4. Inspecting a fracture surface: in GFRC the fibres are visible through the whole thickness of the skin, not as isolated strands.

In practice it is enough for the client to ask for test reports on the latest batches and to see how control is organised at the factory. A list of questions for the manufacturer is given in the article on how to choose a GFRC manufacturer.

What the client risks by replacing GFRC with a cheaper fibre-reinforced concrete

Substituting the material at the procurement stage usually looks like a saving, but it changes the facade structure. A product made of fine-grained concrete with polypropylene fibre cannot carry the design loads at the same thickness, so it is made thicker and heavier.

  • Weight increase. A 30–40 mm skin instead of 10–15 mm makes the element two to three times heavier, and a substructure designed for GFRC no longer has sufficient load-bearing capacity.
  • Cracking from thermal movement. Without working reinforcement, long cornices and string courses crack at points of stress concentration.
  • Brittle failure. Under overload the element gives no warning through cracking; it breaks off.
  • Losses in transport and installation: chipped edges and breakage during lifting.
  • Re-approval of the design: a change in weight and fixing details requires a new calculation.

Where the budget is limited, it is more sensible to reduce the number of unique moulds and simplify the profiles than to change the material. For large flat areas an economical solution is standard GFRC facade panels in a repeating format.

How fibre-reinforced concretes behave over time: corrosion, ageing, maintenance

The durability of fibre-reinforced concrete depends on how the fibre withstands the alkaline environment of cement and exposure to the weather. Each type of fibre has its own weak point here, and it is useful to know it before choosing the material.

  • Steel fibre inside dense concrete is protected by the alkaline environment, but fibres reaching the surface rust. This does not affect load-bearing capacity, yet it leaves rust spots and streaks on the face surface.
  • Polypropylene fibre is chemically inert and does not age in concrete, but it makes no contribution to strength either: the behaviour of the product years later is determined by the matrix itself.
  • Ordinary glass fibre gradually breaks down in the cement matrix, and within a few years the material becomes brittle. For this reason only alkali-resistant fibre is used in concrete.
  • Alkali-resistant glass fibre remains serviceable for decades, but flexural strength decreases somewhat over time. This is allowed for in the calculation by adopting long-term values rather than the results of tests at 28 days.

Maintenance of a GFRC facade comes down to periodic inspection of joints and fixings, cleaning, and renewal of the water repellent at the interval recommended by the manufacturer. The products need no special corrosion protection provided the embedded parts and fixings are made of corrosion-resistant steel or have a protective coating.

Where other types of fibre-reinforced concrete are appropriate on a project

GFRC covers cladding and decor, but on the same project other fibre-reinforced concretes often work alongside it. The division by purpose usually looks like this.

  • Car park floors, ramps and podium slabs — steel fibre reinforced concrete or concrete with macrofibre, where crack resistance of a thick section matters.
  • Screeds on terraces and balconies, render coats — mortars with polypropylene or basalt microfibre.
  • Steps, kerbs and paving slabs — dense concrete or natural stone: a thin shell is not needed here, abrasion resistance is what matters.
  • Wall cladding, cornices, columns, opening surrounds and planters — GFRC.

This division is worth fixing in the design in advance. Then, at tender stage, items are not lumped together under the general term “fibre-reinforced concrete”, and offers for different materials are not compared with one another on price per square metre.

FAQ

What is fibre-reinforced concrete?

Concrete reinforced with dispersed fibres — steel, polymer, basalt or glass. The fibres increase crack resistance and, depending on the type, flexural strength and impact resistance.

What are the properties of fibre-reinforced concrete?

They depend on the fibre: steel increases the crack resistance of massive structures, polypropylene reduces shrinkage cracking, and alkali-resistant glass gives thin elements high flexural strength.

Which fibre-reinforced concrete is suitable for facades?

For rainscreen cladding and decoration — GFRC with alkali-resistant fibre: it allows thin, light elements with complex shapes and textures.

What is the difference between limestone and shell limestone?

Shell limestone is a type of limestone composed mainly of shells and shell fragments; it is more porous and lighter. Limestone in general can be dense and uniform.

How does travertine differ from marble?

Travertine is a sedimentary rock with characteristic pores, formed from mineral spring deposits. Marble is a metamorphic rock, dense and crystalline, and takes a better polish. TECHNOFORMA Group supplies both.

Can glass fibre be added to ordinary concrete with coarse aggregate to obtain GFRC?

No. Coarse aggregate makes it impossible to form a 10–20 mm skin and damages the fibres during mixing. GFRC is made with fine sand, a high cement content and alkali-resistant fibre, and the dosage is selected to suit the forming method.

Do GFRC products need reinforcement bars?

Bar reinforcement is not used in the thin skin: the fibre performs that role. Large panels are strengthened with stiffening ribs and a steel frame, which is connected to the skin by flexible anchors and serves for fixing to the substructure.

Are fibre-reinforced concrete and fibre cement the same thing?

No. Fibre cement is a factory-made sheet material with cellulose and synthetic fibres, produced as flat boards. Fibre-reinforced concrete is a general name for concretes with dispersed reinforcement; of these, GFRC is the one used for three-dimensional facade decor.
