Service Life of GFRC on Facades

The service life of glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) on a facade is measured in decades and is determined not by one parameter but by a combination: the quality of the alkali-resistant fibre and the mix, correct element and fixing design, drainage and maintenance. International experience with GRC spans more than half a century, and elements made to the proper technology are still in service.

The main durability factor is water: wherever it stands and freezes, any concrete deteriorates faster. So when choosing a manufacturer, look not only at specimen strength but also at how well falls, drips, joints and surface protection have been thought through.

This article is for clients, technical supervision and facility managers. Basic information about the material is in GFRC: material and technology.

What service life depends on

The service life of GFRC elements is affected by three groups of factors:

Climate deserves separate mention. A southern climate with frequent freeze-thaw transitions and wet winters can be harder on a facade than steady frost. A sea coast adds chlorides, which are dangerous primarily for metal cast-in fixings and fasteners. All these conditions are taken into account when choosing the mix, the cover thickness and the material of the fixings.

Service life benchmarks usually quoted in the industry are shown below. These are not warranty commitments but estimates for comparing materials, assuming correct design, production and maintenance; the actual life depends on climate, design and care.

For the facade as a whole, service life is set by the weakest element of the system. GFRC cladding can outlast a galvanised steel substructure in an aggressive environment, or joint sealants that need replacing after 10–15 years. So the design life is set for the whole system, not just the cladding.

Ageing and carbonation

Two main processes take place in GFRC as it ages.

Ageing of the composite. Cement paste continues to hydrate, and hydration products gradually fill the spaces between the glass filaments in the strands. As a result, the ductility of the material, its ability to carry load after the first crack, decreases over time, and flexural strength approaches the limit of proportionality. The effect has long been known, so element design relies on long-term rather than initial properties, and modern mixes with modifying admixtures slow the process down.

Carbonation. Carbon dioxide in the air reacts with the calcium hydroxide in the cement paste, and the alkalinity of the surface layer drops. For glass fibre this is largely neutral, but for steel cast-in fixings located in a thin carbonated layer it means a risk of corrosion. That is why cast-in fixings and fasteners in GFRC are made of stainless or protected steel, and the cover is specified with a margin.

Both processes are slow and predictable; they are accounted for in design rather than fixed after installation.

The practical conclusion for the client: comparing suppliers by 28-day strength is not enough. A better question is how the mix design and calculations account for the long-term properties of the material, which fibre type is used and what steel the cast-in fixings are made of.

How water and frost destroy the concrete matrix: water that has entered the pores expands by about 9% on freezing, creating tensile stresses in the pore walls. Each cycle leaves micro-damage that accumulates. That is why a low water-cement ratio, a dense matrix and no places where the element stays saturated for long are important for durability. Dry concrete is not affected by frost; the damage comes from the combination of water and temperatures crossing zero.

The rate of carbonation depends on matrix density and moisture: the denser the material, the more slowly the carbonation front moves into the wall.

The role of water-repellent treatment

Water-repellent treatment is an impregnation that makes surface pores water-repellent while keeping the material vapour-permeable. It extends service life through three effects:

  • the material becomes less saturated, so there is less freeze-thaw damage;
  • salt migration is reduced, so there is less efflorescence and staining;
  • the surface gets less dirty and is cleaned better by rain.

The effect of the impregnation declines over time under UV and rain, so it is renewed at intervals specified by the product manufacturer. The facade is cleaned before re-treatment, and on painted elements compatibility of the impregnation with the paint is checked.

Important: water-repellent treatment does not correct design mistakes. If water stands on the horizontal surface of a cornice or a joint lets it into the element, the impregnation will only slow the damage. Falls, drips and correct joints come first, surface protection second. Some products slightly deepen the colour of the element, so they are tested on a sample before treating the whole facade.

For painted elements, vapour-permeable facade paints (silicate, silicone, mineral-based acrylic) are used instead of or together with impregnation. Film-forming vapour-tight coatings are undesirable on GFRC: moisture that gets into the material cannot escape, and the coating blisters and peels. Painted facades need repainting at intervals that depend on the product and exposure, whereas through-coloured elements need no repainting.

Field experience

Worldwide experience with GFRC dates back to the 1970s; since then the material has been used on facades in different climate zones, from the United Kingdom to the Middle East. The conclusions the industry has drawn over this time:

  1. Most problems are caused not by the material itself but by the design: rigid fixing that ignores movement, no drainage, insufficient thickness around cast-in fixings.
  2. Production quality is decisive: the wrong fibre content, excess water and poor curing show up as cracks within the first few years.
  3. Regular inspection makes it possible to spot local defects in time and fix them before they develop.

TECHNOFORMA handed over its first GFRC projects in 2017: the Golden Keys residential complex and the Bogatyreva residential complex in Makhachkala. The company has worked with the material for more than 10 years.

Typical signs on a facade worth looking for during inspection: hairline cracks near cast-in fixings and at corners, delamination of the textured layer, efflorescence and streaks below horizontal elements, traces of fixing corrosion, and failed sealant in joints. Most of them do not threaten safety if found in time and are dealt with by local repair.

Signs of a quality element that can be checked at acceptance, without waiting years of service:

  • uniform wall thickness without thin spots, especially at ribs and corners;
  • no voids or dry zones with unimpregnated fibre on the back face;
  • cast-in fixings of stainless or protected steel, firmly embedded, with no cracks around them;
  • consistent colour and texture within a batch and between batches;
  • falls and drips built into horizontal faces;
  • element marking and a data sheet with control specimen test results.

If even one of these points is not met, the defect is likely to show within the first few years rather than decades.

Why GFRC outlasts many alternatives under the same conditions: the mineral matrix does not fade in the sun or age under UV like polymer decoration; the material does not burn; and the absence of steel reinforcement rules out the main cause of failure in thin reinforced concrete, corrosion of steel with spalling of the cover. Service life is limited mainly by design decisions and workmanship, not by the material itself.

How to extend service life

The service life of a GFRC facade can be extended at every stage:

  • Design: falls on top faces of at least a few per cent, drips, flashings, open or properly sealed joints, freedom for thermal movement, corrosion-resistant fixings.
  • Production: consistent mix, control of fibre content, correct curing of elements.
  • Installation: mounting without overstressing, protection from damage, closing top edges before the rains start.
  • Service: inspecting the facade at least once a year and after strong winds, cleaning drainage, renewing water-repellent treatment and sealants, local repair of chips with repair mortars.

For systematic maintenance, as-built documentation is useful: a layout drawing with element marking and the fixing details. It speeds up finding and replacing a damaged element.

The facility manager should keep an inspection log with photographs: this makes it easier to track how a defect develops and decide whether to repair or replace an element. A replacement element is made in the same mould and colour-matched, so it makes sense to keep the moulds for standard elements.

Mistakes that most often shorten service life:

MistakeConsequenceHow to avoid it

Rigid fixing at every pointCracks from thermal movementOne fixed support, the rest sliding

Where GFRC elements last especially long: on vertical surfaces with good drainage, in areas without an aggressive industrial environment, with protected top faces. The most stressed zones are parapets, cornices and the plinth, where wetting and mechanical impact are higher; they are checked separately at the design stage.

Do GFRC elements need maintenance? Minimal: an annual inspection, cleaning of drainage and flashings, washing with low-pressure water without aggressive acid products, and renewing sealants and water-repellent treatment according to the manufacturers' schedules. Acid cleaners used to remove efflorescence from brick are dangerous for cement products: they dissolve the surface layer of the matrix and expose the fibre. Efflorescence on GFRC is removed with a dry brush once the surface has dried or with products approved for concrete. An element is replaced if a crack runs through the full wall thickness or a cast-in fixing has failed.

Service life of the facade as a system: what wears out before the panel?

GFRC cladding is almost never the first element to require intervention. The joint sealant, the protective coating, individual fixings and thin sheet-metal flashings reach the end of their life sooner. When discussing the service life of a facade, therefore, it is correct to consider not the panel material but the whole construction, from the anchor in the wall to the outer surface.

Below is an indicative comparison of the elements of a ventilated facade system by what limits their life. Actual periods depend on the design, climate and quality of installation, so the table gives not an age but the order in which the elements usually require attention.

ElementWhat limits its lifeWhat is done during maintenance
Water-repellent coatingUltraviolet, precipitation, abrasion by dustRenewed periodically
Joint sealantLoss of elasticity, debonding from the edgesThe joint is raked out and refilled
Sills, flashings, parapet cappingsCorrosion at cut edges, loosening of fixingsTightened; damaged sections replaced
Fixings and embedded partsCorrosion under constant wettingChecked by selective opening-up
SubframeMaterial and coating of the profiles, contact between dissimilar metalsInspected at points of leakage
InsulationWetting, wind washing, settlementChecked when the cladding is opened up
GFRC panel or decorative elementStanding water, mechanical damageLocal repair of chips and cracks

The practical conclusion for the client: the service life of GFRC on a facade is set when the details are designed, not when the wall thickness is chosen. A frame of corrosion-resistant materials and well-considered drainage achieve more than any reserve in the product itself. How the material of profiles and brackets is selected is described in the article on the ventilated facade subframe.

The same reasoning leads to a requirement for maintainability. Elements with a short life must be accessible for replacement without dismantling the cladding: a joint can be raked out and refilled, a sill removed and a new one fitted. If a short-lived component is hidden behind a panel, its failure means dismantling a whole section of the facade, and the overall life of the construction is in effect reduced to the life of that component.

How the durability of GFRC is tested in the laboratory

Durability cannot be measured directly, so it is assessed from a set of indirect indicators and from the results of accelerated tests. Specimens are subjected to exposures that simulate years of service within weeks, and their properties before and after are compared. The result does not give an exact number of years, but it does allow mix designs to be compared with one another and a weak one to be identified.

The programme usually includes the following checks:

  • Frost resistance. Specimens are saturated with water, repeatedly frozen and thawed, and the loss of mass and strength is then assessed. The more cycles withstood without damage, the higher the grade.
  • Water absorption. An indicator of matrix density: the lower it is, the less water enters the pores and the more slowly all deterioration processes proceed.
  • Flexural strength after ageing. Specimens are kept in hot water, which accelerates hydration and simulates many years of ageing of the composite, and are then tested in bending. What matters is how much strength and ductility has been retained.
  • Alternate wetting and drying. Shows the tendency to shrinkage cracking and warping of thin panels.
  • Resistance of coating and colour. Painted and impregnated specimens are exposed to ultraviolet in a climatic chamber and compared with a reference.

When requesting test reports, it is worth looking not only at initial strength but also at the values after ageing: these are the ones used when a product is designed for wind load in the long term. A manufacturer with a well-proven mix design shows only a small difference between the two. Which properties are regarded as basic and how to read them is covered in a separate article on the properties of GFRC.

Laboratory data have a limitation: they relate to specimens, not to a particular product on the wall. Actual life is additionally determined by how closely series production reproduces the composition of the test boards, so it is reports on current batches that have value, not a single test carried out many years ago.

A separate group of checks relates to finished products: control of wall thickness, inspection of the locations of embedded parts, a pull-out test of an embedded part on a control sample. These data show that the properties of the material have actually been achieved in a specific element and not only in a laboratory board.

Sea air, de-icing agents, urban dust: how the environment affects service life

An aggressive environment shortens the life not so much of the GFRC itself as of the metal next to it. The cement matrix is resistant to salts and to fluctuations in humidity, whereas embedded parts, fixings and the subframe corrode noticeably faster in a saline or polluted atmosphere. A facade design for such conditions differs above all in its requirements for the metal and for surface protection.

  • Coastal locations. Marine aerosol carries chlorides, which settle on the facade and penetrate the joints. For seaside projects stainless fixings and embedded parts are generally specified, and the water-repellent treatment is renewed more often.
  • Road splash zone. In winter the plinth and ground floor are exposed to de-icing agents. Low water absorption of the product, a gap above the paving and the possibility of washing the surface down in spring are important here.
  • Industrial and urban atmosphere. Soot and dust do not destroy the material but quickly alter the appearance of light-coloured facades. Impregnation makes cleaning easier and slows soiling.
  • Continental climate with many freeze–thaw transitions. The main action is the repeated freezing of damp material, so the removal of water from horizontal surfaces becomes decisive.
  • Hot southern regions. Daily swings in surface temperature are large, and the fixing must allow free thermal movement of the panel.

Consideration of the environment begins with the initial data: the location of the building, the orientation of its facades, the proximity of the sea and of main roads. On this basis the subframe material, the type of impregnation and the design of the junctions are chosen. The general principles of this choice are set out in a separate article on how the facade relates to the climate of a region.

Conditions can differ greatly on a single building. Upper floors and corners receive more wind and driving rain, the north facade stays damp for longer, and the plinth works in the splash zone. Requirements for surface protection and for the metal are therefore sometimes set by facade zone and not uniformly for the whole building.

Embedded parts and fixings: why the durability of a panel depends on the metal

A GFRC panel is held on the wall by embedded parts and a steel frame, so its actual life is equal to the life of that metal. If an embedded part has rusted through, the condition of the concrete itself is irrelevant: the product has to be removed. Corrosion is also dangerous because rust occupies a greater volume than steel and splits the thin wall from within.

The durability of the metal parts is affected by several decisions taken at the design stage:

  1. The material of embedded parts and anchors — stainless steel, or steel with a zinc coating of sufficient thickness, depending on the aggressiveness of the environment.
  2. Protection of the panel frame — hot-dip galvanising or painting on a prepared surface, with the coating made good at welds.
  3. The thickness of concrete around the embedded part — a thin layer carbonates quickly and ceases to protect the steel.
  4. Avoidance of contact between dissimilar metals, for example aluminium profiles and uninsulated steel, which form a galvanic couple in a damp environment.
  5. Freedom of movement in the connection — flex anchors between the skin and the frame allow the panel to expand and contract freely without accumulating stress.

The same principle applies to three-dimensional decorative elements. GFRC cornices and other projecting elements work in the harshest conditions: they receive the most water, and the fixing is loaded not only by weight but also by the moment from the projection. The fixing detail for such products is designed with a margin and with provision for inspection.

When concealed works are accepted, it is worth photographing the fixing details before they are covered by the cladding. Years later these photographs make it possible to understand exactly what is installed behind a panel without dismantling it.

The anchors in the wall are assessed separately. Their load-bearing capacity depends on the substrate material and may fall over time if the substrate becomes damp. For this reason leaks behind the cladding are dangerous not only because the insulation gets wet but also because the fixings gradually weaken.

Joints between panels: how they affect the durability of the cladding

The joint is the most vulnerable point of any prefabricated cladding: through it water reaches the space behind the panels, the edges of the products, the embedded parts and the insulation. How the joint is detailed determines how dry the fixings and the back of the panels remain, and hence their life. Two approaches are used in ventilated systems — the open joint and the closed joint.

ParameterOpen jointClosed joint with sealant
How it worksSome water passes behind the cladding and drains down the air gapThe sealant keeps water from getting behind the panels
MaintenancePractically none requiredPeriodic replacement of the sealant
Design requirementsProtected insulation, corrosion-resistant subframe, free outlet for water at the bottomCorrect joint width, backing for the sealant, compatibility with the impregnation
Typical riskStreaking on the facade where the layout is poorly chosenDebonding of the sealant and concealed leaks

Joint width is determined by calculating thermal movement: a panel several metres long changes size by millimetres over the year, and the joint must accommodate this movement. Too narrow a joint results in the edges of adjacent products bearing against each other and spalling, while the sealant in it tears in the very first winter.

For closed joints the sequence of work is important. The water-repellent impregnation is applied so that it does not reach the edges to which the sealant must bond, or else compatible materials and a primer are used. Otherwise the sealant comes away from the edge within a season, and water enters a joint from which it cannot escape. The layout and width of the joints are established at the design stage, when the GFRC panels for the facade are produced and their format is determined.

After how many years will maintenance be needed: an indicative schedule

Maintenance of a GFRC facade does not involve great expense, but it must be regular and tied to the calendar. Below is an indicative schedule, to be adjusted according to inspection results. The intervals are given as ranges: they depend on the climate, the orientation of the facade and the type of protective coating.

PeriodWhat is checked and done
First year after handoverInspection after the first winter: joints, junctions, traces of leaks, efflorescence; rectification of defects under warranty
AnnuallyVisual inspection, cleaning of sills and rainwater elements, checking the sealant in visible areas
Every few yearsWashing the facade, checking the water-repellent effect, renewing the impregnation if necessary
Roughly every 10–15 yearsOverhaul of joints with replacement of sealant, selective opening-up to inspect fixings and insulation
Following a surveyLocal repair of chips, repainting, replacement of individual elements

A simple way to assess the condition of the impregnation is to wet an area of the wall with water. If the drops bead on the surface and run off, the protection is working; if the water is absorbed quickly and the surface darkens, it is time to renew the impregnation. The check is carried out on several facades: the south and west sides usually lose protection sooner.

The washing procedure, choice of products and frequency of work are described in detail in the article on care of GFRC products. The conditions under which the manufacturer and the installer are liable for defects are set out in the warranty for the facade works, which, as a rule, stipulates the owner's obligation to carry out inspections.

It is useful to record the results of each inspection in a single document, with the date, photographs and a list of the work carried out. Such a history of the facade makes it possible to notice that a defect is developing, and simplifies discussions with the contractor during the warranty period and afterwards.

Repair or replacement: how to tell that an element has reached the end of its life

The overwhelming majority of GFRC defects are remedied in place without dismantling. Replacement is required only when the load-bearing capacity of the product or its fixing is affected. A simple rule helps to distinguish these cases: surface damage is repaired, while damage associated with metal and through-cracks is investigated first.

Repairable defects usually include:

  • chips at edges and corners that do not expose the frame;
  • hairline shrinkage cracks with no opening or displacement of the edges;
  • efflorescence, stains, local loss of colour;
  • failure of sealant and pointing in the joints.

Grounds for a survey with possible replacement are:

  • rust streaks from joints or along the line of embedded parts;
  • through-cracks, especially those running from fixing points;
  • displacement of a product relative to its neighbours, a change in joint width;
  • delamination of the face coat over a significant area;
  • damage after impact by vehicles or objects falling from the roof.

Restoration techniques and the matching of repair compounds for colour and texture are described in the article on repair of GFRC. If defects recur in the same places, the cause is, as a rule, constructional — a leak from above or a rigid fixing — and it is sought in the course of an instrumental check, the procedure for which is set out in the article on surveying a building facade.

The advantage of unit products is that a single element can be replaced without affecting its neighbours. For this the manufacturer must retain the moulds or 3D models, and the owner the as-built documentation with product marking. When ordering a facade it makes sense to clarify in advance how long the tooling is kept and whether a few spare elements can be made together with the main batch.

The decision to replace is taken on the basis of a specialist's report, not on outward impression: a product with an unsightly stain may be entirely sound, whereas a neat-looking panel with a rust streak at the joint may require urgent opening-up.

FAQ

What is the service life of GFRC?

Decades, with the right mix, design and maintenance; worldwide experience with the material spans more than 50 years.

What does GFRC durability depend on?

On the alkali resistance and content of the fibre, the mix design, element design, drainage, fixings and maintenance.

Does GFRC need water-repellent treatment?

Yes, the impregnation reduces saturation, efflorescence and soiling; it is renewed periodically.

What is GFRC ageing?

A gradual reduction in the ductility of the composite as hydration products fill the fibre strands; it is accounted for in design calculations.

How often should a GFRC facade be inspected?

At least once a year and after strong winds or severe weather.

What shortens the service life of GFRC the most?

Standing water on horizontal faces, rigid fixing without freedom for thermal movement, corrosion of unprotected steel cast-in fixings and lack of regular inspections. Most of these problems originate at the design and production stages.

What wears out first on a GFRC facade?

Usually not the GFRC itself but the joint sealant and the water-repellent coating: they work under ultraviolet and precipitation and require periodic renewal. Next come sills and thin-metal flashings. Provided drainage is in order, panels and decorative elements last considerably longer than these components.

Can a single panel be replaced without dismantling the whole facade?

Yes, in most ventilated systems the products are fixed independently and a damaged element is removed on its own. To produce a replacement, the moulds or a model of the product and data on colour and texture are needed, so it is important to keep the as-built documentation and marking.

Does proximity to the sea affect the service life of GFRC?

The material itself is affected only slightly: the cement matrix is resistant to salts. Metal embedded parts, fixings and the subframe suffer more, so corrosion-resistant steels are used for coastal projects and the protective impregnation is renewed more often.
