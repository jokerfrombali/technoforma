GFRC Colour: Integral Pigmentation vs Facade Painting

The colour of glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) is set in one of two ways: the mix is coloured integrally with mineral pigments, or the finished element is coated with facade paint. Without pigment, GFRC has the natural grey or light colour of cement; with white cement and light sand it is almost white, close to limestone.

The main selection criterion is not the shade in a catalogue but how the colour behaves over the years: integral colour does not peel and does not show at chips, while paint gives an exact colour match and saturated tones but needs periodic renewal. Architects should decide which option to specify at the sketch stage, because it determines the mix design, mould layout and facade maintenance schedule.

General information on the material is collected in GFRC: material and technology; this article covers colour only.

Integral (through-body) colouring

With integral colouring, the pigment is added to the mix at the batching stage, so the colour runs through the entire thickness of the element rather than just the face layer. The standard GFRC mix is glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement and water; dry mineral pigments are added to it, most often based on iron oxides (yellow, red, brown and black tones), chromium oxide (greens) and titanium dioxide (lightening).

The final shade depends on:

  • cement colour — grey cement gives muted tones; clean light and pastel colours are only possible with white cement;
  • sand colour and grading — light quartz sand does not "dull" the pigment;
  • pigment dosage — usually within a few percent of the cement weight; beyond a certain threshold saturation barely increases, while strength may drop;
  • water-cement ratio and curing conditions — these determine tone consistency between batches.

The strength of this method is durability: a chip or scratch does not expose grey concrete, and the colour does not delaminate. The limitations are a mainly warm, natural palette (ochres, sand, terracotta, graphite), while saturated blues and bright colours are more expensive and less consistent. Integral colour also always has natural variation, which is considered an advantage for "stone" facades and a risk for uniform contemporary ones. For large projects, a reference sample and an acceptable deviation range are therefore agreed in advance.

Facade painting

GFRC is painted after it has gained strength and the surface has been prepared: the element is cleaned of dust and mould release residue and primed if necessary. Vapour-permeable facade coatings designed for mineral substrates are used:

  • silicate — bond chemically with the cement matrix, are highly vapour-permeable and give a matt finish;
  • silicone (silicone resin) — combine vapour permeability with water repellency and offer a wide range of tints;
  • acrylic facade paints — give saturated colours but are less vapour-permeable; not suitable for every service condition.

Paint for GFRC must be alkali-resistant, otherwise the pigments and binder break down in contact with fresh cement stone. Paint is usually applied in two coats; slender cornice and moulding profiles are painted before installation, joints and fixing points afterwards.

Painting is the better choice where an exact catalogue match (RAL, NCS), contrasting or bright colours are needed, as well as in restoration or when changing a building's appearance. The downside is that the coating fades and wears over time and will need renewing; the maintenance interval depends on the paint system and facade exposure and is specified by the paint manufacturer.

Pigments and fading

Fading of coloured GFRC is caused less by the pigment itself than by processes in the concrete and external exposure. The main causes:

EffectCauseHow the risk is reduced
Efflorescence (whitish bloom)Salts and calcium hydroxide carried to the surface by moistureProper curing, water-repellent treatment, sealers
Tone fadingUV radiation, organic pigmentsLightfast mineral pigments, durable paints
MottlingUneven curing, different moisture content between batchesConsistent mix design, control of batching and curing regime
SoilingPorous surface, dust, run-offWater-repellent treatment, drainage from cornices and drip sills

Inorganic oxide pigments are considered lightfast, so integral colour is stable. In the first year a facade may "lighten" slightly as efflorescence emerges — this is a surface effect, not a loss of pigment. Specific lightfastness and alkali resistance figures are given in the pigment and paint documentation.

Water-repellent treatment

Water-repellent (hydrophobic) treatment means applying impregnating sealers to the surface (usually silane-siloxane based) that penetrate the pores and make them water-repellent without blocking vapour permeability. This matters for colour for three reasons: less water gets into the element, so there is less efflorescence; dirt is absorbed less and is washed off by rain; and an integrally coloured surface looks more even and keeps its original tone longer.

The sealer is applied to a clean, dry surface. Some products slightly deepen the colour (a "wet stone effect"), so they are tested on a sample. If the element is to be painted, compatibility between sealer and paint is checked in advance: some water repellents prevent the coating from adhering. How often re-treatment is needed depends on the product and service conditions.

How to choose a colour for your project

Colour selection starts with the facade concept and the surrounding buildings, not with a pigment catalogue. A practical sequence:

  1. Define the role of GFRC on the facade: main background, accent or imitation of natural stone.
  2. Choose the colouring method: integral for "stone" solutions and areas where chip resistance matters; paint for exact colour matching and bright accents.
  3. Obtain physical samples: colour on screen and on a real texture differ, especially after water-repellent treatment.
  4. Assess the samples outdoors in natural light, on the side of the building where the facade will face.
  5. Record the reference sample and shade tolerance in the contract.

A common request is GFRC that looks like travertine. In photos, travertine ranges from creamy white through beige and walnut to golden, with characteristic pores and banding. In GFRC this effect is achieved by combining integral colour with a texture cast into the mould from real stone. If the project needs genuine natural stone, the company supplies travertine and other stones.

At TECHNOFORMA, facades are developed by the design department — 12 designers led by two architects — together with 3D visualisers who show the facade in colour before production starts.

Why the colour of GFRC varies from batch to batch

A slight colour variation between batches is a property of any cement-based material, not a sign of a defect: the shade depends on the raw materials, batching accuracy and curing conditions. The manufacturer's task is to keep deviations within an agreed range; the designer's task is to lay out the products so that the remaining difference cannot be read.

The main sources of tonal variation:

  • a change in the batch of cement or sand: even from one plant the colour of the clinker shifts slightly;
  • the amount of water in the mix: a wetter batch looks lighter once dry;
  • temperature and humidity in the workshop during curing;
  • the age of the product: fresh concrete is darker, and the final tone settles after several weeks;
  • the condition of the mould and release agent, which determine the density of the face skin.

In production this is controlled by organisation: raw materials for one project are bought as a single batch or with a reserve, pigment is batched by mass rather than by volume, and every casting is checked against the reference sample under the same lighting. At the layout stage, products from one batch are directed where possible to one elevation or one installation zone, and the boundary between batches is aligned with a corner of the building, a string course or a rustication joint.

Differences cannot be excluded entirely, and this is discussed openly before the contract is signed. For stone-effect facades a slight play of tone is perceived as natural; for strict single-colour planes a narrower tolerance is specified, and sometimes a finishing glaze that brings the batches to one shade.

How texture and lighting change the perception of colour

The same mix looks different on a smooth surface and on a treated one, so colour is approved only together with texture. A smooth face skin gives a denser and darker tone, acid etching and sandblasting expose the aggregate and lighten the surface, and deep relief adds shadows of its own.

Lighting has no less influence. A south elevation in the sun appears lighter and warmer, a north one cooler and a tone darker; under artificial light in an office a sample looks different from how it does outdoors. Wet concrete darkens, and after rain the areas under cornices and the exposed planes differ for a while. For this reason samples are viewed outside, in a vertical position, both dry and wetted, and from the distance at which people will see the building.

Adjacent materials also shift perception. Next to dark glass and graphite metal, beige concrete seems lighter and more yellow; next to white render, darker and greyer. The sample is therefore assessed alongside pieces of the other facade materials.

More on surface options can be found in the article on GFRC textures; ready combinations of shades and reliefs can be seen in the catalogue of textures.

Glazing, coloured face coat and other combined methods

Between integral colouring and opaque painting there are intermediate solutions that combine the merits of both methods. They are chosen when the lively, uneven tone of natural stone is needed, or when the shade has to be evened out without losing the pattern of the surface.

MethodResultStrengthLimitation
Integral colouringColour through the full thicknessDoes not peel, invisible on chipsRestrained palette, natural tonal variation
Coloured face coatPigment and decorative aggregate in the first layer onlySaves expensive components, stone effect after etchingThe backing layer shows on a deep chip
GlazingSemi-transparent mineral coating over coloured concreteEvens the tone, preserves the texture, allows patch toningRequires an experienced applicator and a reference sample
Opaque paintingContinuous film of the specified colourPrecise colour matching, bright tonesPeriodic renewal

To imitate travertine or limestone, two techniques are usually combined: the base tone is set in the mass, while veins and warm patches are added with a glaze. On large-format GFRC facade panels a coloured face coat is used more often: pigment consumption is lower and the outer layer comes out dense and uniform.

Which colouring method to choose for different products and facade zones

It is convenient to assign the colouring method not to the building as a whole but by product group: panels, ornament and landscape elements have different service conditions and different requirements for uniformity of tone.

  • Large planes. On large panels any blotchiness is noticeable, so a coloured face coat with light surface treatment, or painting, is chosen here.
  • Profiled ornament. Cornices, window surrounds and string courses cast their own shadows, which hide slight tonal variation; integral colouring works reliably.
  • Lower tier and entrances. Mid-tones that do not show dirt and resistance to washing are needed; chips from accidental knocks must not expose a grey base.
  • Landscape pieces and planters. There is constant contact with water and soil, so integral colouring with water-repellent treatment is preferable to film-forming coatings.
  • Interior elements. There is no weathering, the palette is wider, and decorative coatings and waxes are acceptable.

Where concrete and natural stone are combined on a facade, the shade of the products is matched to the stone already chosen, not the other way round: the colour of stone is fixed by the quarry, whereas the colour of concrete can be adjusted.

What to specify in the design to obtain the required colour

The wording ‘light beige’ in a specification is not enough: the colour of a concrete product is described through a reference sample and the conditions for assessing it. The manufacturing brief should record:

  • the colouring method for each group of products: integral, face coat, glazing or painting;
  • the colour reference: an RAL or NCS number as a starting point and an approved physical sample as the final standard;
  • the texture and type of treatment with which this colour has been approved;
  • the permissible range of deviation, defined by two limit samples, the lightest and the darkest;
  • the comparison conditions: daylight, dry surface, viewing distance;
  • the type of water repellent and whether a change of tone after impregnation is acceptable;
  • the procedure for keeping reference samples: one each with the client, the architect and the manufacturer.

For large projects a control mock-up is useful: several products installed on the wall or on a stand before the production run starts. It shows the colour at real scale, together with joints and adjacent materials, and removes most disputes at acceptance.

Reference samples change over time too: a sample that has spent a year in a dark cupboard differs from a product that has spent a year on the facade. When reordering, the new casting is therefore compared not only with the archived sample but also with the surface of the elements already installed.

How to care for a coloured surface and when it is renewed

Integrally coloured GFRC does not need repainting, but it does need periodic cleaning and renewal of the water-repellent impregnation; painted products are additionally repainted as the coating wears. The intervals depend on the climate, the orientation of the facade and air pollution, so they are set on the basis of inspections.

Practical rules:

  • wash the surface with water at moderate pressure and neutral agents; use acidic cleaners only locally and after a trial on an inconspicuous area;
  • do not paint over the whitish bloom of the first year but remove it; the causes and methods are described in the article on efflorescence on stone and concrete;
  • fill chips with a repair compound based on the same cement and pigment, left over from production or matched to a sample;
  • when repainting, use the same system as originally: silicate and silicone paints are not always compatible with each other.

A reserve of pigmented dry mix or paint from the same batch, left at the building, noticeably simplifies local repairs. General maintenance procedures are given in the article on care of GFRC products, and the technique for making good damage in the article on repairing chips and cracks.

What mistakes are most often made when choosing a colour?

Most complaints about colour arise not from production but from decisions taken without a sample or without regard to service conditions.

  • Choosing from a screen or printed catalogue. A monitor and paper do not convey a matt mineral surface; the decision is made only from a casting.
  • A very dark tone on a south elevation. A dark surface heats up more, thermal movement increases, and efflorescence and dust are more visible on it.
  • Pure white in the lower zone. At the plinth and entrances a white surface soils quickly; a tone one or two steps darker, or a different texture, is more appropriate here.
  • Approval on a small sample. A 10×10 cm tile does not show the natural unevenness that will appear on a panel of several square metres.
  • Reordering a year later without a reference. Hitting the earlier tone without a retained sample and a record of the mix is difficult, so make-up elements are best ordered together with the main batch.

FAQ

What colour is GFRC without pigment?

The grey colour of cement, or almost white if white cement and light sand are used.

What paint is suitable for GFRC?

Alkali-resistant, vapour-permeable facade coatings for mineral substrates — silicate or silicone; acrylics are used with care depending on service conditions.

Which is more reliable: integral colour or paint?

Integral colour does not peel and does not show at chips; paint gives an exact colour match and bright colours but needs periodic renewal.

Does integrally coloured GFRC fade?

Mineral oxide pigments are lightfast. A change of tone in the first months is more often caused by efflorescence, which is reduced by water-repellent treatment.

Can GFRC be made to look like travertine?

Yes: the shade is matched integrally and the texture of natural stone is cast into the mould. The result is approved on a physical sample.

Is it possible to obtain pure white GFRC?

A colour close to white is obtained with white cement, light sand and added titanium dioxide. Integral colouring does not give the absolute whiteness of paint: the tone remains slightly warm or greyish. If an exact catalogue white is required, the product is painted.

Does the colour change after water-repellent treatment?

Most silane-siloxane impregnations hardly alter the tone once dry, but some products make it slightly deeper. The reference sample is therefore treated with the same water repellent that will be used on the facade.

Can a GFRC facade be repainted in a different colour?

Yes. The surface is cleaned, its absorbency is checked, and an alkali-resistant, vapour-permeable facade paint is applied over a compatible primer. An integrally coloured product then moves to the maintenance regime of a painted surface, with periodic renewal of the coating.
