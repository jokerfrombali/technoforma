GFRC Moulds: Silicone, Plastic, Timber

Moulds for glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) are the forms in which the mix takes on the final geometry and texture of the element. They are made of silicone and polyurethane (for relief and texture), plastic, fibreglass and acrylic sheet (for smooth and large surfaces), laminated plywood and timber (for simple geometry and small runs), and metal for mass production.

The main criterion for choosing mould material is the combination of relief complexity and run size: the more undercuts and fine details, the more a flexible mould is needed; the more identical elements, the more cost-effective a durable mould with a long life.

This article is for architects, clients and technologists who need to understand what drives element lead times and cost. General information on the material is in GFRC: material and technology.

Types of moulds

The main types of moulds used for GFRC:

Mould materialFor which elementsProsCons
Silicone, polyurethaneRelief décor, capitals, stone textureReproduces detail precisely, allows undercuts, easy to releaseNeeds a rigid support jacket; limited life
FibreglassLarge panels, cornices, curved surfacesLight, rigid, large sizesUndercuts only with a split design
Plastic, acrylic sheetSmooth flat elementsGlossy or even surfaceLimited geometry
Laminated plywood, timberSimple profiles, prototypes, small runsFast and inexpensiveShort life, swells with moisture
MetalMass-produced standard elementsLongest life, consistent geometryExpensive, slow to make

In practice moulds are often combined: a rigid plywood or fibreglass frame with a textured silicone liner on the face. Form liners for concrete with a stone or wood texture are also made of silicone or polyurethane, taken from a natural sample.

A separate distinction is between open and closed moulds. An open mould has a single face surface into which the mix is sprayed or laid in layers; the back of the element is formed by hand, with ribs and inserts installed on it. A closed mould has a core and is filled completely — this is how elements visible on both sides are made, such as balusters, planters and railing elements.

Making the master model

The master model is the reference for the future element from which the mould is taken. It is made:

  • by CNC milling from a 3D model — in modelling foam, MDF or tooling board; high accuracy and repeatability;
  • by hand — sculptors and modellers shape or carve the model in clay, plasticine or plaster; needed for sculptural décor and restoration;
  • by a combination — a milled base refined by hand;
  • from a natural sample — for example stone, if its texture is needed.

The model is filled, sanded and sealed so that the mould surface is smooth and the element demoulds easily. The model design takes into account draft angles for release, shrinkage, the mould parting line and the position of inserts. TECHNOFORMA makes models and moulds in its own mould shops, so the design department and modellers work from the same 3D model.

For restoration and reproduction of historical décor, the model is taken from a surviving fragment: it is cleaned, losses are restored if necessary and a cast is taken. Lost elements are recreated from measured surveys, photographs and analogues. The accuracy of the model determines the accuracy of the whole run, so the model is approved by the architect before the mould is made — from photos, 3D scanning or on site.

Mould life (number of casts)

Mould life is the number of elements that can be cast from one mould before it has to be replaced or repaired. It depends on the mould material, relief complexity, forming method, care during demoulding and the release agents used. Industry guidelines:

  • plywood and timber moulds — tens of casts;
  • silicone — from several dozen to a hundred or more, depending on the silicone grade and relief;
  • fibreglass — hundreds with careful use;
  • metal — up to thousands.

These are guidelines, not guaranteed figures: mould life is assessed separately for each project. When the run exceeds the life of one mould or fast output is needed, several identical moulds are made and cast in parallel.

To extend mould life, it is regularly cleaned of cement laitance residue, release agents compatible with the mould material are used, impacts during demoulding are avoided and the mould is stored without distortion. Silicone moulds are sensitive to cuts and folding, timber to moisture, fibreglass to chipped edges. Mould wear shows on the elements: the relief loses sharpness, burrs appear and geometry drifts. That is why inspecting the first and last elements of a run is part of the normal quality control procedure.

Mould cost in the element price

The cost of a mould is spread over all the elements cast in it. Hence the main rule of GFRC economics: the more identical elements, the cheaper each one is. If a facade has 200 identical window surrounds, the mould cost is barely noticeable in the element price; if it has 200 different ones, moulds become the main cost item.

How to reduce the share of moulds in the budget:

  1. Standardise sizes and profiles at the design stage.
  2. Make elements from standard parts that can be assembled into different compositions (for example, a moulding run plus a corner piece).
  3. Use stop-end inserts so that one mould produces elements of different lengths.
  4. Choose the mould material for the actual run size, not "with a margin".

The company determines the exact cost of moulds and elements from the project drawings.

Besides the mould, the element price includes materials, labour for forming and finishing, cast-in inserts, packaging and delivery. On a large project the balance of these items depends heavily on the design, so offers from different manufacturers can only be compared fairly against the same element specification.

Lead times

Mould making is a separate stage in the production schedule that starts before the first cast. The sequence is: 3D model of the element → master model → mould → trial cast and inspection → series production. The duration of each stage depends on complexity and the number of unique elements, so lead times are set for each specific project.

To avoid losing time, moulds for standard elements are started first, and unique ones in parallel with the start of series production. Early approval of working drawings and the reference sample shortens the overall schedule more than speeding up any single stage.

Schedule planning also allows for the time cast elements need to gain strength before transport, and for the installation sequence: moulds for elements installed first (for example, plinth and lower bands) are logically made before those for the crowning cornice.

What requirements the mould places on product geometry

The product must leave the mould undamaged: this is the main condition that tooling imposes on the design. The architectural profile can almost always be kept, but individual details have to be adjusted: draft angles added, arrises rounded, the element divided into parts.

What production engineers check when reviewing the drawings:

  • Draft angles. Vertical walls in a rigid mould are given a slight draft, usually of a few degrees, so that the product does not jam on release.
  • Undercuts. Re-entrant angles and deep recesses are possible only in an elastic or split mould; each such place makes the tooling more expensive and slows the cycle.
  • Radii. Sharp internal corners fill poorly with fibre mix, and external ones chip. A radius of a few millimetres is invisible on the facade, yet rejects become fewer.
  • Dimensions and weight. The size of one element is limited by transport, the lifting capacity of the plant on site and ease of installation, so long GFRC cornices are divided into sections with joints at designed positions.
  • Rear side. An open mould needs room for ribs, embedded parts and the work of the operative, so deep narrow box sections are awkward.

A separate matter is the parting line of closed and split moulds. It leaves a mark on the product, so the parting is brought out on an arris, in the shadow of the profile or on the side facing the wall.

All such amendments are agreed with the architect before the model is made: changing a draft angle on a drawing is easy, whereas reworking finished tooling shifts the programme.

Release agents and preparing the mould for casting

The quality of the face surface depends not only on the mould material but also on how the mould is prepared before each cycle. The release agent forms a thin film between mould and mix: it eases demoulding and protects the tooling from cement laitance.

The type of agent is matched to the mould material and to the subsequent finishing of the product. Too much release agent gives stains, pores and streaks on the surface; too little gives sticking and pull-outs. Oil-based agents can impair adhesion of paint and impregnation, so for products to be painted, agents that leave no greasy trace are used, or the surface is additionally degreased after demoulding.

The standard preparation sequence:

  1. Cleaning off mortar residue with a soft tool, without scratching the working surface.
  2. Inspection: tears in the silicone, chips in the coating, gaps at the joints of split parts.
  3. Assembly and a geometry check against a template or control dimensions.
  4. Application of the release agent in a thin, even layer.
  5. Placing of embedded parts and locators according to the drawing.

Before a production run a trial casting is usually made: it is used to assess the surface, ease of release and the behaviour of the release agent on the given texture, and the preparation is adjusted if necessary.

How the mould affects the dimensional accuracy of the product

A casting reproduces the mould with all its inaccuracies, so the accuracy of the product cannot be higher than that of the tooling. The final dimension is influenced by the stiffness of the mould, the shrinkage of concrete during curing and wear of the working surface.

An elastic mould without a rigid casing deflects under the weight of the mix, and a long profile comes out bowed or with a varying section. Plywood tooling takes up moisture and changes dimensions over time. Glass-reinforced plastic and metal moulds are more stable, but they too are checked periodically: after dozens of cycles, warping and worn joints are possible. Shrinkage is allowed for when the model is made: it is produced slightly larger than nominal by an amount the manufacturer knows from experience with its own mix.

Tolerances on length, straightness and the difference between diagonals are stated in the working drawings. For critical junctions, for example the drums and half-shells from which GFRC columns and capitals are assembled, a trial assembly of the first cast elements is carried out at the factory. It shows whether the joints will meet before the run leaves for site.

The first product from every new mould is measured in full and compared with the drawing; only then is the mould released for series work. After that, checks are made by sampling, and the tooling itself is inspected after a set number of cycles.

Which mould should be chosen for a particular task?

The choice of tooling comes down to three questions: how many identical products are needed, how complex the relief is, and what the size of the element is. The table shows typical solutions; the final option is determined by the production engineer after studying the drawings.

TaskTypical solutionWhy
A dozen simple profiles for a private housePlywood or composite mouldA small run does not pay back expensive tooling
Capitals, rosettes, ornamentSilicone or polyurethane in a rigid casingUndercuts and fine detail
Hundreds of identical window surrounds for a residential complexSeveral duplicate glass-reinforced plastic mouldsHigh number of uses and parallel casting
Large-format and curved panelsGlass-reinforced plastic on a frame, with a textured insert where requiredStiffness at large dimensions
Panels with a repeating three-dimensional patternA master milled from a 3D model, and a mould taken from itExact matching of the pattern at the joints
Recreating historic ornamentA silicone impression from the original, then a working mouldPreserving the original modelling

The number of duplicate moulds is calculated from the installation programme: if an installation zone needs a certain number of elements every day and one mould gives one casting per day, the tooling is replicated. The requirements for masters for three-dimensional patterns are described in the article on 3D panels and parametric facades.

Storage, repair and reuse of moulds

A mould remains an asset after delivery is complete: it is used to cast make-up elements, to replace products damaged during installation and to repair the facade years later. The fate of the tooling is therefore worth settling in advance.

Points worth fixing in the contract:

  • who owns the models and moulds once the work is finished;
  • how long the manufacturer keeps the tooling and on what terms;
  • whether the moulds may be used for a second phase of construction or for other blocks;
  • whether the master model and the digital 3D model, from which a mould can be remade, are retained.

Silicone and polyurethane moulds are stored in their casings, without folds, away from direct sun and heaters; plywood ones in a dry room. Minor damage is repaired: tears in elastic moulds are bonded, and chips in the gelcoat of glass-reinforced plastic are filled and polished. When the service life is exhausted, a new mould is taken from the retained master model, which is faster and cheaper than making the model again.

Tooling is marked in the same way as products: the mould number is linked to the standard size and the project and is stated in the batch certificate. If a defect is then found, it is easy to establish which mould the product came from and to check the other castings from it.

What to prepare in order to start mould making

Tooling is started only on approved product drawings: any change of profile after that means a new model. For the stage to pass without rework, the following are needed by the start:

  • an approved facade layout with a schedule of standard sizes and the number of products of each type;
  • working drawings of the products with sections, draft angles and the positions of embedded parts and ribs;
  • 3D models for elements of complex modelling, or sketches and templates for hand modelling;
  • approved colour and texture samples;
  • the installation programme by zone, on which the number of duplicate moulds depends;
  • a decision on tolerances and on trial assembly of critical junctions.

The content of the working documentation and its link with production are covered in the article on facade shop drawings. If the ornament is developed from an original design, the procedure for approving the model is described in the article on bespoke ornament to an architect's sketch.

A typical mistake at this stage is to start moulds for one-off elements before the questions on standard ones are closed. Series products set the rhythm of installation, and a delay with them costs more than the late delivery of a single decorative accent.

Common mistakes when designing products for moulds

Most problems with tooling arise not in the mould shop but earlier, when a product is designed without regard to how it will be cast and released.

  • Many almost identical standard sizes. A difference of a few millimetres between profiles on adjacent elevations turns into separate moulds; it can usually be removed without harm to the architecture.
  • Excessively long one-piece elements. They are hard to demould, transport and lift; a joint at a designed position is more reliable than a crack at a random one.
  • Relief without draft. Strictly vertical walls of a deep pattern tear the silicone and scratch the product.
  • Changes after the model is approved. Adjusting a profile ‘by a couple of millimetres’ means a new model and a new mould.
  • Judging a mould by price alone. Cheap tooling with a short life costs more on a large run because of replacements and unstable geometry.

FAQ

Which moulds are best for GFRC?

Silicone for relief and texture, fibreglass for large smooth elements, plywood for simple profiles and small runs.

How long do silicone moulds for GFRC last?

From several dozen to a hundred or more casts, depending on the silicone, relief and care in use.

What are form liners for concrete?

Moulds or inserts that set the surface texture, for example stone or wood; usually silicone or polyurethane.

Why are unique GFRC elements more expensive than standard ones?

The cost of the master model and mould is spread over fewer elements.

Who makes the moulds for TECHNOFORMA elements?

The company makes models and moulds in its own mould shops.

Can a GFRC product be cast from an existing gypsum or stone original?

Yes. A silicone impression is taken from the original, a model is obtained from it and refined if necessary, and a working mould is made. This is how historic ornament is reproduced and make-up elements are matched to an existing facade.

What happens to the moulds after the project is finished?

It depends on the terms of the contract. Moulds may remain in storage with the manufacturer for an agreed period or be handed over to the client. If a second construction phase or a reserve of products for repairs is planned, the arrangements for storing the tooling are best fixed in writing.

Is a separate mould needed for every product size?

Not always. Straight profiles of different lengths are cast in one mould with movable stop ends, and panels of similar sizes in a mould with inserts. Separate tooling is required when the section, radius or relief pattern changes.
