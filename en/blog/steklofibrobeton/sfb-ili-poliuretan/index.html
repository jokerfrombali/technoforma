GFRC or Polyurethane: Which to Choose for a Facade

Polyurethane facade decoration consists of lightweight elements (cornices, mouldings, window surrounds, pilasters, rosettes) cast from rigid polyurethane foam and painted after installation. Its main advantages are low weight, simple installation with adhesive and fixings, and an affordable price for small batches.

The key criterion when choosing between polyurethane and GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) is service conditions and fire safety requirements. Polyurethane is a combustible polymer that is sensitive to UV and temperature changes; GFRC is a mineral material which, according to TECHNOFORMA Group, is classified as non-combustible (NG class under Russian standards). For multi-storey and public buildings the choice usually falls on GFRC, while polyurethane can be justified for interiors and small private houses.

For a general overview of GFRC, see GFRC: material and technology.

How polyurethane behaves outdoors

Polyurethane decoration is made by casting a foaming compound into moulds. The surface of the product is a dense skin with a cellular structure inside. This makes the elements very light and allows them to be installed without a substructure, using adhesive and additional fixings.

Outdoors, polyurethane faces several factors:

  • UV radiation. Unprotected polyurethane degrades in sunlight over time: the surface darkens, chalks and loses strength. Painting with facade paints is therefore mandatory and must be renewed periodically.
  • Thermal movement. Polymers expand on heating much more than wall materials. Long cornices and string courses on the sunny side move, and gaps and cracks appear in the filler and paint at the joints unless expansion gaps are provided.
  • Substrate. The elements are fixed to render or insulation; if the substrate cracks or delaminates, the decoration goes with it.
  • Mechanical impact. The light material is easily damaged at accessible heights — by knocks, ladders or birds.

Durability and painting

The service life of polyurethane facade decoration depends primarily on surface protection. The product is painted immediately after installation and then repainted at intervals determined by paint quality, climate and facade orientation. Once the paint layer breaks down, UV quickly reaches the polymer.

GFRC does not need painting for UV protection: the mineral matrix does not age in sunlight. Colour can be set by integral pigmentation — pigments in the mix — so the product needs no painting at all, and chips and scratches do not reveal a different colour underneath. If painting is chosen, vapour-permeable facade coatings for mineral substrates are used.

Fire safety

Fire safety is the main argument against polyurethane on multi-storey buildings. Polyurethane foam is a combustible polymer. Fire-retardant additives can reduce combustibility but do not make the material non-combustible. When burning, polymer decoration can release smoke and flaming droplets, helping fire spread across the facade.

For residential buildings above a certain height, schools, hospitals, hotels, shopping and office centres, the current fire safety codes impose strict requirements on the combustibility of cladding and rainscreen facade systems. On such projects combustible decoration may fail design review or require additional justification.

GFRC consists of mineral components and, according to TECHNOFORMA Group, is classified as non-combustible (NG). This removes the question of combustibility of the decorative elements themselves. However, the fire safety of a facade is determined by the whole system, including insulation, wind barrier and fire breaks, so it must be designed as a whole.

Life-cycle cost

When comparing costs, it is important to look not only at the price of the products but at the costs over the entire service life of the facade:

  • Purchase and installation. Polyurethane is usually cheaper upfront for small volumes and easy to install. GFRC requires fixing design, but with serial production on large projects the difference narrows.
  • Painting. Polyurethane must be painted immediately and repainted periodically — costs for materials, scaffolding or an aerial platform, and labour. Integrally pigmented GFRC needs no painting.
  • Repairs. Cracks at polyurethane joints, detachment from the substrate and mechanical damage require regular maintenance.
  • Replacement. Worn polyurethane decoration is eventually replaced entirely.
  • Fire requirements. On multi-storey projects combustible decoration may require additional justification or a change of design solution.

TECHNOFORMA Group calculates the specific cost of GFRC products from drawings or after a site survey.

When polyurethane makes sense

Polyurethane decoration is reasonable in several cases:

  • Interiors. Ceiling cornices, mouldings and rosettes in dry rooms — here polyurethane is light, simple and not exposed to UV or frost.
  • A small private house with a limited budget, where the decoration is in areas protected from the sun and the owner is prepared to repaint regularly.
  • Temporary solutions — seasonal pavilions, exhibition stands, stage sets.
  • Restoration of light elements on a weak substrate where the load cannot be increased — after calculations and approvals.

For multi-storey residential complexes, public buildings and projects designed for long service without frequent repair, GFRC remains the more reliable solution.

A combined approach is also common: GFRC elements on the facade and polyurethane mouldings of the same pattern in the interior hall or lobby. This keeps a consistent style while each material works in its own conditions.

How does installing polyurethane differ from installing GFRC?

Polyurethane decor is glued to the finishing layer of the wall, whereas GFRC products are mechanically fixed to the load-bearing substrate. Everything else follows from this difference: who does the work, what calculations are needed and what happens to the decor if the substrate starts to deteriorate.

The ease of installing polyurethane is also its weak point. The adhesive joint works only as long as the render beneath it is intact, and its condition under the decor cannot be monitored. A GFRC product is held by anchors in concrete or masonry, and the state of the finishing layer does not affect its stability.

Which elements can be made of polyurethane, and which only of GFRC

The boundary is drawn by the size of the element, the installation height and accessibility for maintenance. The larger the section and the harder it is to reach the part from a platform, the less reason there is to choose a material that needs regular repainting.

  • Thin mouldings and window surrounds on floors 1–2 of a private house. Polyurethane is acceptable if the facade does not face south and the owner is prepared to look after the paintwork. The option without repainting is GFRC mouldings and bands.
  • Architraves and pediments on an apartment building. GFRC: the elements are repeated in hundreds, sit at height and must be non-combustible. Typical solutions are collected on the architraves and surrounds page.
  • Crowning cornices with a large projection. Only GFRC or other mineral materials with engineered fixing: snow lies on the ledge, water runs over it, and long polymer parts change length noticeably when heated.
  • Columns and pilasters. Polyurethane half-columns are used indoors; outdoors GFRC shells are more resistant to impact in the lower zone.
  • Balustrades and railings. Only strong mineral materials: a balustrade takes loads from people.
  • Rustication, plinth bands, entrance surrounds. GFRC or stone: a zone of mechanical impact, splashing and de-icing agents.

More on what replaces traditional plaster and polymer mouldings outdoors can be found in the article on facade mouldings.

How the decor looks close up: joints, element length, edges

From a distance of a few dozen metres, painted polyurethane and GFRC are hard to tell apart. The difference is noticeable close up and after several years in service, so it should be taken into account at entrances, ground floors and terraces — wherever the facade is viewed at close range.

  • Joints. Polyurethane profiles are produced in lengths of standard size, usually about two metres, and are butt-jointed with adhesive. Every joint is a potential crack in the paint. GFRC elements are made longer and with a designed joint, which remains a straight line rather than a defect.
  • Edges and corners. A mineral casting has crisp, hard arrises. With a foamed polymer the edge is softer and is easily dented during installation or by accidental impact.
  • Building corners and breaks in the profile. Polyurethane is mitred on site, and the quality depends on the installer. In GFRC the corner elements are cast in one piece.
  • Surface. Under a coat of paint, polyurethane looks like a painted part. GFRC retains a mineral texture close to stone or render.
  • Ageing. Repeated repainting gradually rounds off fine relief; on GFRC the pattern remains unchanged.

If the budget is limited, a sensible compromise is to concentrate GFRC decor in the zones that are seen close up and are most exposed, and to reduce the number of small details on the upper floors.

Weight of the decor and load on the wall: what needs to be calculated

Polyurethane is lighter, but for the load-bearing capacity of the building the difference is, as a rule, insignificant: both materials count as lightweight decor. What matters more is where exactly the load is transferred — to the finishing layer or to the structure.

A polyurethane profile of medium section weighs in the order of a few kilograms per linear metre and places no appreciable load on the wall. A similar GFRC element with a 10–20 mm skin is several times heavier, yet remains considerably lighter than solid concrete or stone. This weight is taken by brackets, and their spacing and the type of anchors are selected from the calculation and, where necessary, anchor pull-out tests.

Three values are essential for the designer: the weight of the element, the offset of its centre of gravity from the wall, and the wind pressure at the given height. It is the offset, not the weight as such, that determines the force in the upper anchor of a cornice. The assessment procedure is set out in the article on how much GFRC weighs.

On an insulated facade there is an additional consideration. Polyurethane decor is glued to a thin reinforced layer over the insulation, and that layer takes its entire weight together with wind suction. Brackets for GFRC pass through the insulation to the wall, so the insulation is not loaded.

How do you replace polyurethane decor with GFRC during refurbishment?

Replacement is almost always possible, but it requires a design: GFRC products cannot simply be set on the former adhesive positions. The work usually proceeds in the following sequence:

  1. Survey of the facade: condition of the render and insulation beneath the decor, material of the load-bearing wall, actual dimensions of the profiles.
  2. Measurement and, if the appearance is to be retained, taking profiles from the existing elements.
  3. Development of the fixing details and the layout of elements along their length, allowing for joints and openings.
  4. Making of models and moulds, production of the items with embedded parts.
  5. Removal of the old decor, repair of the substrate, reinstatement of the finishing layer.
  6. Installation of brackets, hanging of the elements, sealing of abutments and fitting of flashings.

Replacement is most often prompted by cracked joints, detachment together with the render, fading and traces of repeated repainting that obscures the relief. If the cladding or insulation is being replaced at the same time, the decor is designed together with the new system — this is covered in the article on facade refurbishment.

On buildings with a ventilated facade system, the fire-stopping details are checked as well: fire breaks, opening surrounds, materials in the air cavity. The requirements for them are described in the article on fire barriers.

Typical mistakes in choosing and installing lightweight decor

Problems with polyurethane on a facade are more often caused not by the material itself but by its use outside a reasonable range. The most frequent miscalculations are:

  • using interior products outdoors — they have a different density and are not designed for moisture and frost;
  • butt-jointing long bands without expansion gaps and elastic sealant;
  • installation on fresh or weak render, or on a painted surface without removing the paint;
  • no flashings or drips above projecting profiles, so that water runs behind the decor;
  • painting with products not intended for polymer substrates and exterior work;
  • fitting large cornices on adhesive alone, without mechanical fixing;
  • ordering decor before the fire requirements for the building have been checked.

GFRC has its own list of risks, and it is linked to the design: incorrectly specified joints, rigid restraint of all points with no allowance for thermal movement, no fall on horizontal surfaces. These matters are resolved at the working documentation stage, before the moulds are made.

What to prepare for pricing GFRC decor in place of polyurethane

For a preliminary estimate, the materials that the client or architect usually already has are sufficient:

  • facade drawings or photographs with the overall dimensions of the building and number of storeys;
  • catalogue numbers or sections of the polyurethane profiles specified in the design;
  • information on the wall: load-bearing material, type and thickness of insulation, type of finish;
  • preferences for colour and texture — smooth surface, stone imitation, through-colouring;
  • the address of the site and the desired programme.

From these data the engineer determines which profiles can be reproduced unchanged and which should be enlarged or merged in order to reduce the number of moulds. Standardisation has a marked effect on the budget: each unique part requires its own model and mould, whereas repeating elements are cast many times in the same tooling.

FAQ

How long does polyurethane facade decoration last?

Service life depends on product quality, painting and climate. Without a protective coat of paint polyurethane degrades under UV, so the paint must be renewed regularly.

Can polyurethane decoration be used on a multi-storey building?

Polyurethane is combustible, and current fire safety codes impose strict requirements on the cladding of multi-storey buildings. Such projects usually use non-combustible materials such as GFRC.

Does GFRC need painting?

Not necessarily: colour can be set by integral pigmentation. Painting is used at the architect's request, with vapour-permeable coatings for mineral substrates.

Which is heavier — polyurethane or GFRC?

Polyurethane is much lighter and is glued to the wall. GFRC products are heavier, but with a 10–20 mm wall and hollow shells they are still lightweight cladding and are installed on fixings or a substructure.

Can GFRC decor be fixed with adhesive, like polyurethane?

No. GFRC products are fixed mechanically — on embedded parts, brackets and anchors in the load-bearing substrate. Adhesives and sealants are used only to close abutments, not as a load-bearing fixing.

Can the profile of a catalogue polyurethane cornice be reproduced in GFRC?

Yes. A model and mould are made from the profile section, and the product is manufactured with embedded parts for fixing. The profile is sometimes adjusted in the process: a fall is added to the top ledge and a drip is formed so that water does not run onto the wall.

Will an insulated wall support GFRC decor?

The load from the decor is transferred not to the insulation but through brackets to the load-bearing wall or floor slab. Whether fixing is feasible is established by calculation and, where necessary, by testing anchors in the specific substrate.
