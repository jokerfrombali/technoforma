Coated Foam Decoration or GFRC

Foam facade decoration consists of elements cut from expanded polystyrene (EPS) and covered with a protective layer: reinforcing mesh with adhesive, render or a special polymer-cement coating. It is cheap, light and quick to install, which is why it is popular in private construction and on facades insulated with an external thermal insulation composite system (ETICS).

The main limitations are the combustibility of the core, vulnerability to mechanical damage and the service life of the protective layer. For multi-storey residential complexes, where decoration must last for decades without constant repair and meet fire safety requirements, the alternative is GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) — a mineral material which, according to TECHNOFORMA Group, is non-combustible (NG class under Russian standards).

For a general overview of GFRC, see GFRC: material and technology.

How foam decoration is made

The technology for producing exterior EPS decoration is simple, which explains its popularity:

  1. Blank. Blocks of expanded polystyrene of the required density are used.
  2. Profile cutting. A heated wire on a CNC machine cuts the profile of a cornice, window surround, string course or pilaster to a template. Three-dimensional elements — capitals, brackets, rosettes — are milled.
  3. Protective coating. A reinforcing layer is applied to the blank: an adhesive or polymer-cement compound with glass mesh, sometimes with sand for texture. The coating is usually a few millimetres thick.
  4. Installation. Elements are glued to the wall and additionally fixed with anchors, joints are filled, then the decoration is skimmed and painted with facade paint.

EPS facade decoration looks good in photos right after installation: straight edges, a clean profile, fresh paint. It is often made DIY too, by cutting simple profiles with a hot wire. Problems appear later, when the protective layer starts working in real conditions.

GFRC decoration is made differently: a mix of cement, sand and alkali-resistant glass fibre is formed in moulds, and the finished product has a strong mineral wall through its full thickness rather than a thin skin over foam.

Typical damage

Coated foam decoration is vulnerable in several places:

  • Coating cracks. Polystyrene and the protective layer expand differently when heated and cooled. Cracks appear on long elements and at joints, letting water get under the coating.
  • Delamination. Water under the coating tears the layer off the core as it freezes. Blisters appear first, then the coating falls off in pieces.
  • Mechanical damage. The thin layer is easily punctured by a ball, a ladder or during snow clearing. At ground-floor level such damage is inevitable.
  • Birds and insects. Through damaged areas birds and insects destroy the soft core.
  • Water on horizontal surfaces. The top surfaces of cornices and string courses without metal flashings quickly lose protection.
  • Fading and soiling. Paint on a polymer coating needs regular renewal.

GFRC is much less susceptible to these defects: the material is solid through its thickness, strong in impact and bending, and colour can be set by integral pigmentation.

Fire restrictions on high-rise buildings

Expanded polystyrene is combustible. The protective coating shields it from direct flame, but in a fire it cracks, and the core starts to melt and burn, releasing smoke and flaming droplets. Decoration running in continuous bands across the facade can become a path for fire to spread between floors.

For multi-storey residential buildings, public buildings, hotels and places of mass occupancy, requirements for the combustibility of cladding and facade systems are set by the current fire safety codes. On such projects combustible decoration may fail design review or require additional technical justification.

According to TECHNOFORMA Group, GFRC is classified as non-combustible (NG). This does not remove the need for a holistic approach — the fire safety of a facade is determined by the whole system, including insulation and substructure — but it removes the question of combustibility of the decorative elements themselves.

Service life compared

The service life of decoration is determined less by the core than by the durability of the face layer and the details. Below is a qualitative comparison; specific life spans depend on materials, climate, facade orientation and maintenance.

Foam decoration is cheaper upfront but requires regular investment in repair and painting. GFRC costs more to buy but is designed for long service without constant maintenance.

Conclusion for residential complexes

For a residential complex the arguments favour GFRC:

  • Fire requirements. A multi-storey residential building is a project where cladding combustibility is checked strictly.
  • Scale. On a residential complex decoration runs to thousands of square metres and linear metres. Regular repair of foam elements at height requires aerial platforms, rope access workers and costs that fall on the management company and residents.
  • Repetition. Repeating sections allow the same moulds to be used for a large GFRC run, reducing unit cost.
  • Developer reputation. Peeling decoration a few years after handover is a visible defect that reduces the value of the property.

Foam decoration remains an option for small private houses with a limited budget, where the owner is prepared for regular maintenance.

How polystyrene and GFRC decor are fixed: adhesive versus hung fixing

Polystyrene decor is bonded to the surface of the wall or insulation and secured with disc-head plugs; it is held by the adhesion of the adhesive layer. A GFRC element is hung on mechanical fixings that transfer the load to the load-bearing wall — a fundamentally different way of working.

ParameterCoated polystyreneGFRC
Main fixing methodAdhesive compound and disc-head plugsAnchors, brackets, embedded parts
Where the load goesTo the outer layer of the wall or insulationTo the load-bearing wall or the building frame
Substrate requirementsEven, sound surface and a compatible adhesiveLoad-bearing capacity of the wall for the anchor, pull-out testing
Joints between elementsFilled with adhesive and filler, reinforced with meshSealed or open joints, flashings
Removal and replacementThe element is cut off and the substrate repairedThe element is taken off its fixings

The weak point of the adhesive approach is its dependence on the condition of the surface: if the decor is bonded to the render coat of an insulation system, it is no more reliable than that coat. Mechanical hanging requires calculation and more skilled installation, but the behaviour of every fixing point is predictable and can be checked.

Hence the difference in quality control. With bonded decor, once it has been painted, neither the contact area of the adhesive nor the number of plugs can be checked. With a hung element the fixing points are visible before the joints are closed: they can be inspected, photographed and signed off as concealed work.

Can GFRC decor be installed on an insulated facade?

Yes, but the fixing must pass through the insulation to the load-bearing wall. The product must not bear on the insulation boards or on the thin render coat: they are not designed for such a load.

On a rendered facade the brackets for cornices, string courses and surrounds are installed before the insulation and then worked round with thermal insulation; thermal break pads are used to reduce heat loss. Light elements of small section are sometimes fixed with through anchors passing through the insulation with spacer sleeves. On a ventilated facade the decor is included in the overall substructure: the brackets and rails are designed from the outset for its weight and projection.

At the same time, every bracket passing through the thermal insulation is a point thermal bridge. Their number and spacing are determined by calculation, and the penetration is carefully packed with insulation and sealed so that water does not get into the wall. When properly executed, the effect of such penetrations on the thermal performance of the facade is small, but it has to be allowed for at design stage.

The decision on the type of facade system and on the decor material is best taken at the same time, because the embedded parts and the sequence of work depend on it. The differences between the systems are discussed in the article on rendered and ventilated facades.

What polystyrene facade decor loses in detail

Hot-wire cutting gives a profile of constant section, and a protective coat a few millimetres thick rounds off every arris. On simple string courses this goes unnoticed, but on classical cornices with fine mouldings, on capitals and on brackets the design comes out softened, especially after several repaints.

  • Sharp arrises and thin fillets. In polystyrene they have to be thickened, otherwise the coating chips; in GFRC they are formed from the model.
  • Ornament and relief. Milled polystyrene conveys the large form but not fine texture; a mould for concrete reproduces the model down to the tool marks.
  • Surface texture. Polystyrene decor is always painted, either smooth or with a render texture; GFRC can have the texture of stone or exposed aggregate.
  • Large projections. A polystyrene cornice of large section remains light but vulnerable to wind and birds; in GFRC it is made hollow with a calculated fixing.

How to replace polystyrene decor with GFRC: the sequence of work

Replacement on a building in use is possible and is usually combined with facade repairs. In general terms the sequence is as follows.

  1. Facade survey: the condition of the decor, render coat, insulation and wall, with damp areas recorded.
  2. Measuring the existing profiles and deciding whether the design is retained or adjusted.
  3. Checking the load-bearing capacity of the wall for anchors, with pull-out tests where necessary.
  4. Preparing working drawings of the products and fixing details with the insulation taken into account.
  5. Making the models and moulds and approving a sample.
  6. Removing the old decor, repairing the substrate, installing brackets, reinstating the insulation and finish around them.
  7. Installing the products and forming flashings and joints.

On residential buildings the work is planned in sections so that opened-up areas of facade are not left for long. The extent of removal and substrate repair becomes clear only after opening up, so a contingency is usually included in the estimate for such work.

If the facade design is retained, the old profiles are used as the basis for the models, but the section is, as a rule, adjusted: falls are added on the top surfaces, along with drip edges and places for fixings.

What makes up the cost of ownership of facade decor

Comparing materials on purchase price alone is incorrect: with polystyrene decor a substantial part of the cost falls in the years of use, whereas with GFRC it falls on manufacture and installation. A proper comparison includes the same items over the same horizon — as a rule, 15–25 years.

  • Products and consumables: the decor itself, adhesive or fixings, flashings, sealants.
  • Installation, including access equipment.
  • Painting and its periodic renewal.
  • Local repairs: filling cracks, reinstating the coating, replacing elements.
  • Access at height for every repair: scaffolding, towers, rope access technicians.
  • Indirect losses: warranty claims, reduced appeal of the property.

For a private house of one or two storeys the calculation may work out differently: access to the decor is simple, repairs are carried out from a ladder or tower, and the quantity is small. The conclusion about which material is more advantageous is therefore always tied to a specific building.

The taller the building and the greater the quantity of decor, the more the last three items affect the total. The factors that determine the price of concrete products are discussed in the article on what the cost of GFRC decor depends on.

If polystyrene is chosen: how to extend the service life of decor on a private house

For a one- or two-storey house, expanded polystyrene decor remains a workable option provided a few rules are followed. They do not make it the equal of mineral materials, but they noticeably postpone the first repair.

  • Choose blanks made of dense facade-grade polystyrene with a factory-applied reinforced coating, not ones coated on site.
  • Cover the tops of cornices, string courses and sill elements with metal flashings with a drip edge.
  • Do not place such decor at plinth and ground-floor level next to paths, entrances and parking.
  • Divide long profiles into lengths and reinforce the joints, avoiding continuous rigid lines tens of metres long.
  • Inspect the decor every spring and fill cracks before water gets under the coating.
  • Use vapour-permeable facade paints and renew the coating as it wears.

Which elements are really justified on a small house, and which are better omitted, is covered in the article on facade decor for a private house. A comparison with another common lightweight material is given in the article on GFRC and polyurethane.

What data is needed to estimate GFRC decor

Facade drawings or dimensioned photographs are enough for an estimate. If the decor is replacing existing polystyrene decor, it is useful to attach its profiles and details of the wall construction.

  • Facade elevations with levels and the position of the decor relative to the openings.
  • Profile sections with projection and height dimensions.
  • Wall construction: material of the load-bearing layer, type and thickness of insulation, type of finish.
  • Number of storeys and the region of construction.
  • Colour and texture requirements: integral colour or a surface for painting.
  • Desired time frame and sequence of delivery by block or section.

From this data the number of moulds, the weight of the elements and the fixing layout are determined; the final calculation is made after a site survey.

FAQ

How long does EPS facade decoration last?

Service life is determined by the integrity of the protective coating and the regularity of maintenance. Once cracks and chips appear, water gets under the coating and the elements need repair or replacement.

Can EPS facade decoration be made DIY?

Simple profiles are cut with a hot wire and coated with a reinforcing compound, but durability depends on the quality of the coating and details. On multi-storey buildings such decoration is restricted by fire requirements.

Does coated foam decoration burn?

The core — expanded polystyrene — is combustible. The coating delays exposure to fire, but in a fire it cracks, and the core melts and burns.

Why is GFRC better than foam for a facade?

GFRC is non-combustible (NG class according to TECHNOFORMA Group), impact-resistant, does not require painting and is designed for long service without regular repair.

Can GFRC elements be bonded in the same way as polystyrene ones?

Not on a facade. GFRC products are considerably heavier, and an adhesive layer is not regarded as a load-bearing fixing. They are installed on anchors, brackets and embedded parts, with the load transferred to the load-bearing wall; adhesive and sealant play a secondary role.

How much heavier is GFRC decor than polystyrene decor?

Noticeably: a polystyrene profile weighs just a few kilograms per linear metre, whereas a hollow GFRC element of the same section weighs several times more, depending on the skin thickness and developed width. The exact weight of each product is given in the working drawings and is used to design the fixing.

Can polystyrene decor and GFRC be combined on the same house?

On a low-rise house this is sometimes done: GFRC is used at the bottom, where impacts are possible, and light elements higher up. The profiles, colour and paint texture must be coordinated, otherwise the difference will be noticeable. On multi-storey buildings the use of combustible decor is restricted by fire safety requirements.
