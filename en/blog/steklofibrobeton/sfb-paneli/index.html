GFRC Facade Panels

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) panels are thin-walled facade elements made from a cement-sand mix reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre. Thanks to dispersed reinforcement, with a face skin of around 10–20 mm they withstand wind and thermal loads while weighing several times less than conventional concrete or natural stone of the same area.

The main selection criterion is not the price per square metre but the panel's compatibility with the fixing system and facade geometry: size, thickness, cast-in fixings and permissible deformations must be calculated together with the substructure. The material is chosen by architects and developers who need a facade with relief, large formats or a "stone" texture, while achieving non-combustible class NG under Russian standards.

For the structure and properties of the material itself, see GFRC: material and technology.

Panel types

What are GFRC facade panels in practice? The term covers several structural types:

  • Flat cladding panels — sheet elements without ribs that act as cladding on a ventilated rainscreen facade. Suitable for small formats.
  • Ribbed panels — stiffening ribs of the same material are formed around the perimeter and/or across the field. They allow larger formats without increasing skin thickness.
  • Stud frame panels — a thin GFRC skin is connected to a light steel frame with flexible anchors. The classic solution for large-format facade panels, including storey-height ones.
  • Volumetric and 3D panels — relief, curved, deeply sculpted panels; window surrounds, cornices and other decorative elements are closely related.
  • Sandwich panels — two GFRC skins with insulation between them; less common and require a separate thermal calculation.

GFRC facade panels can have a smooth surface, a natural stone texture (travertine, limestone, sandstone), a ribbed or perforated finish, and can be integrally coloured or prepared for painting. Choosing the type is a trade-off between format, relief, weight and mould cost.

Sizes and thicknesses

GFRC panels have no strict "standard" sizes: each project is laid out individually, and limits are set by strength, the casting process, transport and installation. Industry reference values:

ParameterTypical rangeDepends on
Skin thickness10–20 mm, more at fixing zonesLoads, mix design, relief
Flat panel without ribsup to ~1.5 m on the longer sideDeflection, wind load
Ribbed or framed panelup to storey height and moreFrame, transport, lifting capacity on site
Skin weightaround 20–40 kg/m²Thickness, mix density

These values are industry guidelines: for a specific element, thickness and weight are determined by calculation and mix design, and exact values are stated in the product data sheet and test reports. The question "what size are ventilated facade panels" is answered at the layout stage: the format is matched to the substructure grid, openings and floor levels to minimise the number of panel types and therefore moulds.

Panel fixing

Panels are fixed to the load-bearing substructure of the rainscreen facade, and the substructure is fixed to the wall or floor slabs via brackets. Several schemes are used for GFRC:

  • Concealed fixing to cast-in inserts — inserts (sockets, plates, anchor loops) are cast into the panel body, and hook brackets are attached to them.
  • Fixing via a frame — for stud frame panels: the frame is hung on brackets, and the skin is connected to it by flexible anchors that accommodate thermal movement.
  • Clip and visible fixings — used on small flat elements.

The key principle is separation of supports: some points carry the dead load (gravity supports), others only the wind load (restraints), and the scheme must let the panel move freely as it heats and cools. Calculations cover wind and dead loads in line with current Russian codes of practice (SP) on loads and actions.

Joints and geometry

The appearance of a panel facade is largely defined by its joints. Open (unfilled) joints are typical of ventilated facades: moisture that gets behind the cladding drains through the air gap, and the panels move freely. Closed joints are filled with elastic sealant — this requires maintenance and a precise joint width.

Joint width is set taking into account thermal movement and manufacturing and installation tolerances; projects typically allow for joints of around 8–15 mm. Panel geometry is checked at demoulding: deviations in length, width, diagonals and flatness are compared with the tolerances agreed in the working drawings. On complex facades the joint often becomes part of the pattern — it is aligned with floors, openings and decoration, and the layout is checked in a 3D model before moulds are made.

Interfaces are designed separately: building corners (L-shaped corner panels or mitred joints), reveals and sills of openings, transitions to the plinth and parapet. These are where leaks and visual defects most often occur, so the details are worked out in the working drawings before moulds are made.

Where they are used

GFRC facade panels are used where complex sculpting or a "stone" look is needed under weight constraints:

  • multi-storey residential complexes with classical or neoclassical facades;
  • business centres and offices with large-format or relief cladding;
  • public buildings — universities, administrative buildings, hotels;
  • renovation, where existing walls and slabs are not designed for heavy cladding.

Another reason to choose GFRC is fire safety: according to the manufacturer, the material is non-combustible (Russian class NG), which is important for high-rise buildings.

There are limitations too. For a simple flat facade without relief, GFRC may lose on price to porcelain stoneware or fibre cement boards: the material's advantage shows where form is required. That is why many projects combine GFRC with other claddings — for example, GFRC on decoration and piers, porcelain stoneware on plain wall areas.

How do you choose the type of GFRC panel for the task?

The type of panel is determined by three parameters: the required format, the depth of relief and the way it is hung on the building. It is best to start not from a catalogue but from the facade: the grid spacing, storey height and joint pattern indicate where a flat sheet is sufficient and where a frame is needed.

TaskSuitable typePoints to watch
Flat cladding with closely spaced jointsFlat panel without ribsMany fixing points, close subframe spacing
Large format between windows, piersPanel with stiffening ribsRib positions are coordinated with embedded parts
Storey-height panel, ribbon glazingSkin on a steel frameCorrosion protection of the frame, crane installation
Deep relief, curved surfaceThree-dimensional 3D panelCost of the mould, number of repeats
Sun screens, enclosures for plant areasPerforated panelShare of openings and residual strength of the webs

Two or three types are usually combined on one facade. The designer’s task is to reduce them to the minimum number of moulds without losing the architectural intent. Examples and available textures are shown on the GFRC facade panels page.

What input data are needed to design the panels

Panel layout requires architectural facade drawings, information on the load-bearing structure and the requirements for appearance. The more accurate the input data, the fewer the revisions once mould-making has begun.

  • Elevations, plans and sections with the levels of floor slabs, openings and parapets.
  • Material and thickness of the external walls, position of in-situ concrete bands and slab edges — this is where load-bearing brackets are placed.
  • Insulation thickness and the required air cavity, which determine the bracket projection.
  • Construction region and building height for determining wind load; for seismic regions — the design seismicity of the site.
  • A colour and texture reference, requirements for the joint pattern.
  • Site constraints: availability of a crane, facade hoists and storage areas.

On the basis of these data the layout, details and shop drawings of the products with embedded parts are issued. The role of detail drawings in the overall chain is described in the article on facade detail drawings. At TECHNOFORMA Group this is handled by a design department of 12 designers and 2 architects.

Production: spraying or casting

Large panels are more often made by spraying, while small elements with complex relief are made by premix casting. The method affects fibre content, flexural strength and the achievable skin thickness.

In spraying, the mix and chopped glass fibre are delivered onto the mould simultaneously, and the layers are compacted with rollers. The fibres lie in the plane of the skin, so the material works well in bending — exactly what a thin, large-format panel needs. In casting, the fibre is added to the mix beforehand and the mix is poured into the mould with vibration; the method is convenient for parts with deep relief and a closed contour.

After moulding, the product is kept in humid conditions until it reaches demoulding strength, then removed from the mould, checked for geometry and left to mature. Dispatch is usually planned once the strength specified in the process regulations has been reached. Both methods are examined in more detail in the article on GFRC production technology.

Transport, storage and lifting of panels

A thin-walled panel is designed for loads in its final position, so most damage occurs not on the facade but on the way to it. The transport and slinging scheme is determined in advance and stated in the documentation.

  • Panels are transported on edge or at a slight incline in racks and on A-frames, with soft packers; stacking flat is acceptable only for small flat elements.
  • Support is provided at the points indicated by the manufacturer, usually near ribs or frame nodes.
  • The face is protected from abrasion and soiling, and the edges with corner protectors.
  • On site the products are stored on a level base, under a canopy or cover, without allowing water to stand in the relief.
  • They are lifted by lifting loops or embedded sockets; slinging by edges and corners is ruled out.

Each panel carries a mark corresponding to the layout. Deliveries are made up by work sections and storeys, so that products do not have to be re-stacked on site in search of the required mark.

Installing facade panels: sequence and checks

Installation begins with a geodetic survey of the walls: the actual deviations of the building are compared with the design and compensated by adjusting the brackets. Only then are the subframe axes set out.

  1. As-built survey of the facade and setting-out of axes.
  2. Pull-out tests of anchors on representative areas of the substrate.
  3. Installation of brackets with thermal break pads.
  4. Installation of insulation and, if specified, a wind barrier membrane.
  5. Installation of rails and alignment of their plane.
  6. Hanging of panels from the bottom up, checking joint widths and levels.
  7. Fitting of fire breaks, reveals, sills and parapet cappings.

Requirements for profiles and fixings depend on the frame material; the options are compared in the article on subframes for ventilated facades. The specifics of a ventilated system with this particular cladding are described in the article on the GFRC ventilated facade.

At acceptance, the following are checked: conformity of marks to the layout, width and straightness of joints, absence of chips and cracks, tightening of fixings, and the existence of concealed work certificates for anchors, insulation and fire breaks.

What makes up the estimate for a GFRC panel facade

The cost of a facade is determined by more than its area: two projects with the same square metres may differ markedly in budget because of the number of moulds and the complexity of the details. The estimate is usually divided into several blocks, and each should be checked separately.

  • Design. Layout, fixing calculations, shop drawings of the products and subframe.
  • Tooling. Models and moulds. The higher the repetition of panels, the smaller the share of tooling in the price per square metre.
  • Products. Materials, moulding, embedded parts or frame, surface finishing, packing.
  • Subframe and fixings. Brackets, rails, anchors; the subframe material is chosen with regard to the environment and service life.
  • Insulation and membrane, if they are within the scope of work.
  • Logistics. Distance, number of trips, packaging requirements for large-format panels.
  • Installation. Access equipment, cranes or hoists, labour content of abutment details.

The main levers for saving lie in the design: standardising panel sizes, replacing unique corner pieces with standard ones, a sensible depth of relief, flat panels on areas not visible from the main viewpoints. The skin thickness or the number of fixing points must not be reduced for the sake of price — these parameters are set by calculation.

When comparing contractors’ offers, it is useful to make sure that they cover the same scope: with or without the subframe, with or without insulation, with reveals and sills or the wall plane only. Prices are not published on the company’s website; pricing is carried out from the drawings of the specific project.

Typical mistakes in design and operation

Most problems are built in at the design stage and show up after several seasons. The most common are:

  • too many panel sizes — the number of moulds and the lead time grow;
  • rigid restraint of the panel at all points, so that thermal movement causes cracks at the embedded parts;
  • joints that do not coincide with storey levels and fire breaks;
  • horizontal ledges and relief without a fall and a drip;
  • no access for inspecting fixings at abutments;
  • replacement of the specified subframe with an ‘equivalent’ without recalculation.

In service the panels are inspected as part of scheduled facade surveys: condition of joints, integrity of edges, traces of leaks at sills. Washing is done with water at moderate pressure and neutral detergents, and water-repellent treatment is renewed in line with the recommendations of the product manufacturer. Practical advice is collected in the article on care of GFRC products.

FAQ

What are GFRC facade panels?

Thin-walled panels made of glass fibre reinforced concrete — a cement-sand mix reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre; they are mounted on the substructure of a ventilated rainscreen facade.

How thick are GFRC panels?

The skin is usually 10–20 mm, thicker at fixing zones; the exact value is determined by calculation and stated in the product data sheet.

What sizes are GFRC ventilated facade panels?

Custom: flat panels without ribs up to 1.5 m on the longer side, framed panels up to storey height.

How are GFRC facade panels fixed?

To cast-in inserts or via a steel frame to the rainscreen substructure, with separate gravity and wind supports.

Do GFRC panels burn?

According to the manufacturer, GFRC is non-combustible (Russian class NG).

Which is better for large panels — spraying or casting?

Spraying is usually used for thin, large-format panels: the fibre lies in the plane of the skin and increases flexural strength. Premix casting is more convenient for small parts with deep relief. The method is chosen by the manufacturer on the basis of the product’s shape and loads.

How are GFRC panels transported?

On edge or at a slight incline, in racks or on A-frames with soft packers and edge protection. Support and slinging are carried out only at the points indicated by the manufacturer.

Can GFRC panels be cut on site?

Trimming small flat elements is technically possible but undesirable: the position of the embedded parts changes and an untreated edge is exposed. Panels are manufactured to the design size, and make-up pieces are provided for in the layout.
