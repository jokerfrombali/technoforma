GFRC Composition: Cement, Sand and Alkali-Resistant Fibre

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) consists of four basic components: cement, fine sand, alkali-resistant glass fibre and water. Plasticisers and modifying admixtures are added to control the workability of the mix, the rate of strength gain and frost resistance.

The key component is the fibre: it must be alkali-resistant, otherwise the cement environment gradually destroys it and the element loses flexural strength. So when choosing a supplier, the question is not just what goes into the mix, but which materials the manufacturer actually uses, in what proportions, and how it controls them.

This article is for architects, technical supervision engineers and clients comparing offers from GFRC manufacturers. For a general overview of the material, see GFRC: material and technology.

Mix components

According to TECHNOFORMA Group, its GFRC is made from four components. The role of each:

ComponentRole in the materialWhat to look for
High-grade cementBinder; forms the matrix and compressive strengthGrade and batch-to-batch consistency; white cement is used for light-coloured and through-coloured elements
SandAggregate; reduces shrinkage and forms the surface textureFine fraction, free of clay and silt particles
Alkali-resistant glass fibreDispersed reinforcement; carries bending and tension, provides crack resistanceFibre type (zirconia-containing AR glass), length and proportion by weight
WaterCement hydration, workability of the mixLow water-cement ratio; excess water reduces strength

Unlike ordinary concrete, GFRC contains no coarse aggregate such as crushed stone or gravel. This makes thin walls possible and allows the mould relief to be reproduced sharply.

In industry practice, the requirements for the components come down to a few points. Cement: high-grade Portland cement without mineral additions that reduce early strength; for light-toned architectural elements, white Portland cement. Sand: graded quartz sand, usually with a maximum grain of about 1–2 mm, washed and dried, because sand moisture directly changes the actual water-cement ratio. Water: drinking quality or meeting the requirements for concrete mixing water, free of salts and organic matter that cause efflorescence and stains on the face.

Besides the base components, the mix may include pigments, decorative fillers (marble or granite chips for a textured face layer), polymer dispersions and pozzolanic additives. Their share is small, but they largely determine the appearance and durability of a particular element.

Why alkali-resistant glass fibre is essential

Hardened cement paste is a highly alkaline environment. Ordinary glass (E-glass) corrodes in it over time: the fibres lose strength and the element gradually becomes brittle. That is why GFRC uses alkali-resistant glass fibre (AR glass) containing zirconium dioxide. This fibre is what distinguishes GFRC from any mix that simply has 'glass fibre for concrete' added.

There is no universal answer to what GFRC is made of and in what proportions. In industry practice, fibre content is usually a few per cent of the mix weight (roughly 3–5% for spray-up and less for vibration casting), the cement-to-sand ratio is close to 1:1 and the water-cement ratio is low, around 0.3–0.4. The actual mix design depends on the forming method, the required properties and the manufacturer's materials; exact values are set out in the production procedure and confirmed by test reports.

Fibre comes as roving (a continuous strand chopped by the spray gun during spray-up) or as ready-made chopped fibre for premix. Fibre length is usually measured in centimetres and chosen to suit the production method.

How much fibre to add is determined by the required flexural strength and the technology. In spray-up the fibre lies in the plane of the element and works more efficiently, so its share is higher and the wall is thinner. In premix the fibre is distributed randomly throughout the volume, and with too much fibre the mix loses workability, forms lumps and fills the mould relief poorly. Raising fibre content above the optimum does not give a proportional gain in strength, but it makes the element more expensive and worsens the surface.

Admixtures and plasticisers

Admixtures do not replace the main components; they adjust the properties of the mix and the finished element:

  • Plasticisers and superplasticisers reduce the amount of water without loss of workability. Less water means higher density and strength and lower water absorption.
  • Polymer additives (for example, acrylic dispersions) improve curing conditions for thin walls, increase crack resistance and reduce water absorption.
  • Frost-resistance admixtures: according to TECHNOFORMA, the frost resistance of GFRC is improved with admixtures, which is especially important in climates with frequent freeze-thaw transitions.
  • Pigments for through-colouring; dosed strictly so as not to reduce strength.
  • Pozzolanic additives (for example, metakaolin, silica fume) bind part of the free lime and lower the alkalinity of the matrix, giving the fibre additional protection.

The set and dosage of admixtures are part of the manufacturer's mix design, so they are worth discussing when approving elements.

For facade elements, three groups of admixtures matter most: those that reduce water absorption (integral water repellents and polymers), those that increase frost resistance, and those that protect the fibre from the alkaline environment. For interior elements, pigments and admixtures that improve surface quality come to the fore.

AdmixtureWhat it givesRisk of overdosing
SuperplasticiserLess water at the same workabilitySegregation of the mix, delayed setting
Acrylic dispersionCrack resistance, curing without wet care, lower water absorptionHigher cost, lower compressive strength
Metakaolin, silica fumeDenser matrix, fibre protection, less efflorescenceHigher water demand of the mix
PigmentsThrough-colouringLower strength, uneven colour
Integral water repellentLess capillary suctionPoorer bond between layers

Mineral pigments are used: iron, chromium and titanium oxides, which resist the alkaline environment and UV. Their dosage is usually limited to a few per cent of the cement weight: above that the gain in colour saturation is minimal and strength starts to drop. Decorative fillers such as marble, granite or quartz chips go into the face layer, which is then acid-etched or sandblasted to expose the grain.

How composition affects strength

The strength of GFRC depends not on a single component but on their balance:

  • Fibre content and distribution are the main factors in flexural strength. Too little fibre reduces crack resistance; too much makes placing harder and can form lumps.
  • Water-cement ratio: the lower it is while keeping workability, the denser and stronger the matrix.
  • Sand quality: contaminated or overly coarse sand weakens the bond with the fibre and worsens the surface.
  • Compaction: in spray-up the layers are rolled; in vibration casting the mix is compacted by vibration; air voids reduce strength.
  • Curing regime: thin elements lose moisture quickly, and without humidity control they do not reach full strength.

For comparison, the properties of natural materials are set by their mineral composition. Shell limestone (coquina), for example, is a limestone composed mainly of calcite from shells, and its strength depends on the porosity of the particular quarry. With GFRC, strength can be designed through the mix. In a ventilated rainscreen facade, the GFRC panel is only the cladding layer; the system also includes the substructure, fixings, insulation and wind barrier, and the strength of the whole assembly is determined by calculating the connections.

How the composition shows up in the properties of the finished element is summarised in the table. The numerical properties of GFRC depend on the mix design and technology; exact values are given in the product data sheet and test reports.

The main difference from ordinary concrete is how it works in tension. In heavy concrete, tension is taken by steel reinforcement, and the concrete itself cracks early in bending. In GFRC, the dispersed fibre takes tensile stresses throughout the volume, so a wall 10–20 mm thick carries a load for which ordinary concrete would need several times the thickness plus a reinforcement cage with cover.

What to ask the manufacturer about the mix design

Before ordering elements, it is worth asking the manufacturer a few specific questions:

  1. What glass fibre is used: alkali-resistant AR fibre, who makes it, and is it documented?
  2. What is the fibre content by weight and which forming method is used (spray-up, vibration casting, premix)?
  3. What cement and sand are used, and how is batch consistency controlled?
  4. Which admixtures are added for frost resistance and lower water absorption?
  5. Are control specimens made from every batch, and are there test reports for flexural strength, frost resistance and water absorption?
  6. How is curing of thin-walled elements organised?

TECHNOFORMA Group controls quality at every production stage and makes its own models and moulds in its in-house mould shops. Specific properties of elements for a project are discussed with the company's engineers.

Typical client mistakes when assessing composition:

  • comparing manufacturers only by price per square metre without requesting flexural and frost resistance test reports;
  • taking fibre documents as documents for the element: a certificate for AR glass does not confirm that the right amount goes into the mix;
  • approving colour from a single sample without checking elements from different batches;
  • not specifying in the contract that control specimens are made and kept until acceptance;
  • accepting a change of forming method (spray-up to premix) without recalculating thickness and cast-in fixings.

A separate issue is storing elements before installation. Finished elements are stored on timber bearers in a position close to their design position and protected from direct soaking and dirt. Chips are repaired with repair mortars colour-matched to the matrix, which is one more reason to know the element's composition.

Face coat and backing layer: how do their compositions differ?

In a sprayed product the composition of GFRC is not uniform through the wall thickness: a thin fibre-free face coat is applied to the mould first, followed by the reinforced backing layer. The face coat is responsible for appearance, the backing layer for load-bearing capacity. For the client this means that questions about the mix design are best asked for each layer separately.

The face coat is typically a few millimetres thick. Fibre is omitted from it or kept to a minimum so that fibre bundles do not reach the surface and show through after sandblasting. This is the layer that receives the pigment, white cement and decorative aggregate, so it determines the colour of the finished product and its texture.

The backing layer is built up in several passes, each compacted with a roller. All of the reinforcement is concentrated here, and the fibre content is kept to the mix design. The backing layer must be applied before the face coat has set; otherwise a weak interface forms between the layers, along which delamination can occur under freezing.

ParameterFace coatBacking layer
PurposeColour, texture, sharpness of reliefFlexural strength, stiffness, crack resistance
FibreNone or minimalFull content as per mix design
Pigment and decorative aggregateYesUsually not
ThicknessA few millimetresThe remainder of the wall thickness

Vibration-cast products usually have no such division: the mix is uniform across the whole section and the fibre is distributed throughout the volume. Cast elements with an exposed texture therefore call for more careful selection of fibre length and compaction regime.

At acceptance the ratio of the layers can be assessed from a cut through a test board or from the edge of the product: the face coat is visible as a thin, uniform band without fibres. An excessively thick face coat is undesirable — it is unreinforced and prone to fine crazing as it shrinks, so its thickness is controlled in production in the same way as the overall wall thickness.

Cement and pozzolanic additions: why metakaolin is added to the mix

Pozzolanic additions — metakaolin and silica fume — are added to the mix to bind the free calcium hydroxide released during cement hydration. The less of it remains in the matrix, the more slowly the composite ages and the longer the product retains its ductility in bending. This is one of the main differences between modern mix designs and those used in the first decades of the material's application.

The mechanism is simple. Over time calcium hydroxide crystallises inside the glass fibre bundles, bonds the individual filaments together and deprives the bundle of the ability to pull out of the matrix when a crack forms. An active mineral addition reacts with calcium hydroxide and converts it into additional calcium silicate hydrates, which densify the cement paste. At the same time the alkalinity of the pore solution falls and the tendency to efflorescence is reduced.

In practice the following binder options are used:

  • Grey Portland cement — for products that will be painted and for elements where the body colour is unimportant.
  • White Portland cement — for light and through-coloured products; it gives clean shades but requires consistent batches, otherwise the tone will vary from delivery to delivery.
  • Cement with metakaolin — for facade products with higher durability requirements; the addition is light in colour and has almost no effect on shade.
  • Cement with silica fume — densifies the matrix but has a dark tint, so its use in white and light mixes is limited.

The dosage of active additions is part of the mix design and usually ranges from a few per cent to about a fifth of the cement mass, depending on the type of addition. Overdosing increases the water demand of the mix, so it is always combined with a superplasticiser. For more on how the mix design is reflected in the declared properties, see the article on the characteristics of GFRC.

The grade of cement is a separate matter. For thin-walled products a high-activity cement without factory-blended mineral additions is used, so that the manufacturer controls the amount and type of active addition directly. The freshness of the cement also matters — material stored for a long time loses activity, and the design strength may not be reached by the time of demoulding.

Which sand is suitable for GFRC

The composition of GFRC includes washed, dry, fine silica sand with a low content of silt and clay particles. Shrinkage, water demand and the quality of the face surface all depend on the aggregate, so the requirements are stricter than for sand used in ordinary mortar.

The main requirements for the sand are as follows:

  • Grading. For spraying, the upper particle size limit is usually about one millimetre: coarser grains block the gun nozzle and damage the fibre. A somewhat coarser aggregate is acceptable for vibration casting.
  • Mineral composition. Sand with a high quartz content is preferred: it is hard, chemically inert and does not change volume when wetted.
  • Cleanliness. Clay and dust coat the grains, impair bond with the cement paste and increase water consumption. Organic impurities retard hardening.
  • Moisture content. The sand is supplied dry, or its moisture content is checked before each batch; otherwise the actual water–cement ratio will differ from the design value.
  • Particle shape. Rounded grains give a more workable mix at the same water content than crushed, angular ones.

In decorative mixes part of the sand is replaced with marble, granite or limestone chippings. After sandblasting or acid etching the grains are exposed and the surface takes on the look of natural stone. These finishing options are described in the article on textures of GFRC products. When the aggregate is replaced the mix design is recalculated: chippings differ from silica sand in density and water demand.

In production every new batch of sand is checked before it is put to use: sieve analysis, fines content, moisture. A change of quarry or supplier is a reason to produce test samples again, since even an aggregate with a similar specification can alter the shade of the face surface and the workability of the mix.

Polymer admixture and curing

An acrylic polymer dispersion is added to the mix to retain water in a thin-walled product while it hardens. A wall 10–15 mm thick dries out within hours, and without protection the cement does not have time to hydrate — strength and density end up below the design values. The polymer forms a film in the pores that slows evaporation.

There are two approaches to curing. The first is moist curing: after demoulding the products are kept in a humid environment for several days, under film or in a chamber. The second is to add polymer at a few per cent of the cement mass, which allows curing in air under ordinary workshop conditions. The second method is more convenient for large panels that are difficult to place in a chamber, and gives a more predictable result at high production volumes.

Besides retaining water, the polymer has side effects that are useful for a facade:

  • it reduces water absorption and the permeability of the matrix;
  • it reduces shrinkage microcracking at the surface;
  • it improves the workability of the mix and the bond between layers during spraying;
  • it makes the surface less prone to dusting and efflorescence.

It is important that the dispersion is one intended for cementitious systems and is UV-stable. An unsuitable polymer may cause yellowing on light products or soften in a wet environment. The frost resistance of facade materials depends directly on the density and water absorption of the matrix, so the curing regime is regarded as just as much a part of the mix design as the components themselves.

Workshop temperature is also part of the curing regime. At low temperatures hydration slows and demoulding strength takes longer to develop; at excessively high temperatures and in dry air the surface dries out before the polymer film has formed. For this reason products are not taken outdoors immediately after demoulding but are kept in the workshop until they reach release strength.

How the composition of GFRC is tailored to the product

There is no single mix design for all cases: the composition is adjusted to the forming method, the geometry of the product, the surface requirements and the service conditions. The basic ratio of cement, sand and water changes only slightly, while the main means of adjustment are fibre content and length, the type of admixtures and the composition of the face coat.

TaskWhat is changed in the mix designWhy
Large-format thin panelSpraying, maximum fibre content, polymer admixtureHigh flexural strength at low weight
Decorative element with fine reliefVibration casting, shorter fibre, higher workabilityFilling fine mould details without voids
Through-coloured productWhite cement, mineral pigment, consistent aggregateUniform tone within a batch and between batches
Natural stone textureStone chippings in the face coat, subsequent exposure of the grainThe look and pattern of natural material
Products near ground level and in the splash zoneReduced water–cement ratio, air-entraining admixture, water-repellent treatmentResistance to freezing and de-icing agents

For facade cladding the mix design is usually approved together with a reference sample: the client agrees the colour and texture, and the manufacturer fixes the composition to which the entire batch will be produced. This is how GFRC facade panels are made for large projects, where the shade must match across different sections of the works.

The approach is different for landscaping elements. Planters, benches and barriers are subject to impact loads and constant contact with moisture, so in small architectural forms the wall is made thicker and particular attention is paid to matrix density and surface protection.

Regions with hot summers and seismic activity are a separate case. The mix design is essentially the same, but more attention is paid to the reserve of flexural strength and to the lightfastness of pigments: on southern facades the surface heats up considerably, and differences in pigment quality become noticeable within a few seasons. Any adjustment to the composition is confirmed by new test samples, not by calculation on paper.

How is the mix design verified in production?

Compliance of the actual composition with the mix design is checked at three stages: when the components are batched, in the fresh mix and on hardened samples. None of these checks replaces another, as each detects its own kind of deviation.

  1. Batching. Cement, sand, water and admixtures are measured by mass, not by volume. The weighing equipment is verified periodically, and every batch is recorded in a log with the raw material lot numbers.
  2. Slurry workability. Before spraying, the spread of the mix is measured on a smooth plate. A deviation from the norm indicates an error in the amount of water or plasticiser.
  3. Gun calibration. The quantities of slurry and fibre delivered by the equipment over a fixed time are weighed separately. The fibre content is calculated from the ratio of these values and the feed is adjusted if necessary.
  4. Wash-out test. A weighed sample of freshly sprayed material is washed on a sieve; the cement paste is removed and the fibre remains. After drying it is weighed to give the actual fibre content.
  5. Testing of samples. Test boards are sprayed at the same time as the products, and specimens are cut from them for flexural testing and for determining density and water absorption.

The test results are documented in reports that are attached to the batch certificate. The list of properties and methods referred to by manufacturers is discussed in the article on standards for GFRC. Once on site, the mix design can no longer be verified at acceptance, so it makes sense for the client to request the reports before dispatch and, on large projects, to be present when the test boards are made.

The frequency of checks depends on output. Slurry workability and equipment calibration are, as a rule, checked every shift, while flexural specimens are tested for each batch or at a set interval. If results drift from the norm, production establishes the cause before work continues: raw materials, batching or the spraying regime.

Common mix errors and what they lead to

Most of the defects that appear on a facade after two or three years are built in on the day of mixing. Below are the deviations from the mix design most often encountered among small manufacturers, and their typical consequences.

  • Extra water for ease of forming. The mix is easier to place, but the matrix becomes porous: water absorption rises, strength and frost resistance fall.
  • Reduced fibre content. The product looks normal, but cracks appear during transport and installation, and the strength reserve under wind load is lower than designed.
  • Replacing alkali-resistant fibre with ordinary fibre. There is no difference in the first months, then flexural strength gradually declines.
  • Unwashed or damp sand. The water–cement ratio fluctuates, and stains and shade variation appear on the surface.
  • Organic pigments instead of mineral ones. The colour fades on the sunny side within a few seasons.
  • Excess accelerator in winter. The product reaches demoulding strength quickly, but the risk of efflorescence and shrinkage cracks increases.
  • No curing. The thin wall dries out, the surface dusts and holds impregnation less well.

None of these deviations can be reliably identified from the appearance of a new product. When comparing offers, therefore, what matters is not a declaration of composition but which checks the manufacturer carries out on each batch and whether it is prepared to show the results.

There is also the opposite error — excessive reinforcement. Too high a fibre content impairs compaction: the mix does not roll out well, voids remain within the thickness, and strength falls instead of rising. A mix design works only when balanced, so an arbitrary «improvement» of one component usually gives a worse result than strict adherence to a proven composition.

FAQ

What is GFRC made of?

Cement, fine sand, alkali-resistant glass fibre and water, plus plasticisers and modifying admixtures. GFRC contains no coarse aggregate (crushed stone).

What are typical GFRC mix proportions?

The cement-to-sand ratio is usually close to 1:1, fibre content is a few per cent of the mix weight and the water-cement ratio is low. Exact proportions depend on the technology and are set out in the manufacturer's production procedure.

Can ordinary glass fibre be used in concrete?

Not for durable elements. Ordinary glass breaks down in the alkaline cement environment; GFRC uses alkali-resistant fibre containing zirconium dioxide.

Why are plasticisers added to GFRC?

They reduce the amount of water while keeping the mix workable, which increases density and strength and lowers water absorption.

What does a ventilated facade with GFRC panels consist of?

Substructure brackets and rails, fixings, insulation, a wind barrier membrane where needed, and GFRC cladding panels installed with a ventilated cavity.

How much glass fibre goes into GFRC?

In industry practice, roughly 4–5% of the mix weight for spray-up and 2–3% for vibration casting and premix. The exact share depends on the required strength and technology and is set in the manufacturer's procedure.

Does the composition of GFRC differ for spraying and for vibration casting?

Yes. In spraying, the fibre is fed from roving and chopped in the gun; its content is higher and the mix contains less water. Vibration casting uses pre-chopped fibre of shorter length and a more fluid slurry so that it fills complex moulds. The basic components are the same in both cases.

Why is metakaolin added to GFRC?

Metakaolin binds the calcium hydroxide that forms as cement hardens and, over time, makes the composite more brittle. With this addition the material retains its flexural strength for longer, and the matrix becomes denser and less prone to efflorescence.

Is it possible to tell from a finished product what it is made of?

Not from its appearance. The type of fibre, its content and the water–cement ratio can only be established by laboratory methods and from production records. It is therefore worth asking the manufacturer for test reports on control samples and details of the raw materials used before dispatch.
