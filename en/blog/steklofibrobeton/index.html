GFRC: material and technology

What is GFRC? GFRC is glass fibre reinforced concrete, also known as GRC; in Russia it is called SFB (steklofibrobeton). According to TECHNOFORMA, it consists of four components — glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement and water; it is non-combustible (class NG under Russian standards), highly frost-resistant and can be cast into almost any shape.

Articles in this section explain how the material works and how it is made — by spray-up and vibration casting, its strength, density and weight and how these affect the load on a facade, and where its strengths and limitations lie. A separate series compares GFRC with fibre-reinforced concrete, polymer concrete, polyurethane, foam plastic, gypsum, architectural concrete and natural stone, so you can make an informed choice.

Where GFRC is used: facades, interiors and landscaping

Glass fibre reinforced concrete is used wherever a complex shape is needed at a low component weight: in facade cladding and ornament, in the interiors of public areas and in landscaping. A thin wall and fibre reinforcement throughout the section make it possible to produce large elements without a heavy steel frame inside.

  • Facade. Facade panels, cornices, string courses, window surrounds, columns and rustication — on a ventilated facade subframe or fixed to the load-bearing wall.
  • Interior. Wall panels, portals and ceiling elements in lobbies and atria; see the article on GFRC in interiors.
  • Landscaping. Planters, benches, balustrades and other small architectural forms that stay outdoors all year round.

There are limitations too. The material is not intended for load-bearing structures, large components need embedded parts and calculated fixings, and every new mould adds time to production preparation. GFRC therefore delivers the greatest benefit on projects with repeating elements, where one mould serves dozens or hundreds of casts, and where the weight of the cladding is limited by the capacity of the walls or the subframe.

Which GFRC properties matter for a facade design?

Four groups of properties matter to the designer: component weight, flexural strength, resistance to frost and moisture, and fire performance. Exact values depend on the mix design and the forming method, so the design should rely on the manufacturer's documents rather than on averaged figures from reference books.

Spraying or premix casting: how the technologies differ

Spraying is used for thin-walled large-format components, premix vibration casting for solid and small parts with crisp relief. In spraying, the mix and chopped fibre are applied to the mould in layers and the fibre lies in the plane of the wall; this is how panels and shells of large area are made. In casting, the fibre is blended into the mix beforehand and the mould is filled with compaction — a method well suited to balusters, brackets and small ornamental elements.

On a single project the two technologies are usually combined, and the manufacturer makes the choice based on geometry, batch size and the requirements for the face surface. Details are in the article on production technology.

FAQ

Can GFRC be used on high-rise buildings?

Yes. The material is non-combustible (NG class) and, thanks to its thin wall, places a moderate load on the subframe. A high-rise building requires fixings to be calculated for wind load and a subframe design that takes into account the weight of the specific components.

How does GFRC differ from ordinary concrete?

Ordinary concrete relies on coarse aggregate and steel reinforcement, so components turn out thick and heavy. GFRC contains no crushed stone, and the reinforcement is distributed throughout the section as alkali-resistant glass fibre, which allows a wall thickness of roughly 10–20 mm.

Do GFRC components need to be painted?

Not necessarily. Colour can be achieved by integral pigmentation of the mix, or facade paint can be applied after installation. The choice depends on the required uniformity of tone and on how the building will be maintained.
