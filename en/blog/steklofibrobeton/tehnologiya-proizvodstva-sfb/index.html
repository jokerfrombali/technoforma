GFRC Production Technology: Spray-Up and Vibration Casting

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) production comes down to two main forming methods: spray-up, in which the mortar and chopped glass fibre are sprayed into the mould simultaneously, and vibration casting, in which a ready mix containing fibre is poured into the mould and compacted by vibration. These are followed by curing, demoulding, surface finishing and quality control.

The method is chosen to suit the element: spray-up for large thin-walled panels with high flexural strength requirements, vibration casting for three-dimensional decoration and small, intricate elements. The result depends on production discipline as much as on the method.

This article is for designers and clients who want to understand what happens at the plant and what to check. A general overview is in GFRC: material and technology.

Two main methods

Two main methods are used in GFRC production — spray-up and vibration casting (premix). They differ in how the fibre is introduced, its proportion in the mix and, consequently, the properties of the element. Both are often used on one project: panels by spray-up, decoration by casting.

ParameterSpray-upVibration casting
Fibre introductionChopped from roving in the gun and sprayed together with the mortarChopped fibre is added to the mixer in advance
Fibre contentHigher, fibres oriented in the plane of the wallLower, fibres randomly distributed
Flexural strengthHigherLower
Typical elementsFacade panels, large shells, cornicesBalusters, capitals, rosettes, small decoration, landscape elements
Operator skill requirementsHigh, manual control of layer thicknessLower, the process is easier to standardise

Spray-up

In spray-up, the mortar is pumped to the gun, where a continuous strand of alkali-resistant glass fibre is chopped into lengths and mixed into the mortar spray. The usual sequence is:

  1. A release agent is applied to the mould, and if required a thin fibre-free face coat for precise texture reproduction.
  2. The mix with fibre is sprayed in layers of a few millimetres.
  3. Each layer is rolled to remove air and lay the fibres in the plane of the wall.
  4. Thickness is checked with a gauge; the final wall of a facade panel is usually 10–15 mm.
  5. Where required, fixings and stiffening ribs or a steel frame on flexible anchors are embedded.

The method gives the highest flexural strength at minimum thickness, which is why it is used for large-format panels.

Vibration casting

In vibration casting, the components — cement, sand, water, admixtures and chopped fibre — are blended in a mixer until uniform. The ready mix is poured into a mould on a vibrating table and compacted by vibration until the air is released.

The advantage of the method is precise reproduction of fine relief and three-dimensional forms: balusters, capitals, brackets, rosettes, planters. The process is easier to standardise and repeat. The limitation is that, because of the lower fibre content and its random orientation, walls are made thicker than in spray-up and flexural strength is lower. That is why vibration casting is rarely used for large thin-walled panels.

Mould preparation

The mould defines the element's geometry and texture, so mould preparation is a separate production stage:

  • Model (master pattern). Made from drawings or a 3D model — from timber, plastic or MDF, or CNC-milled.
  • Mould material. Polyurethane or silicone moulds for complex relief; fibreglass, steel or combined moulds for flat and large elements.
  • Texture. The surface relief is built into the mould: smooth, stone-effect, wood-effect, rusticated.
  • Preparation before casting. Cleaning, applying release agent, checking geometry and embedded items.

At TECHNOFORMA Group the models and moulds are made in-house, in the company's own mould shops. This removes dependence on third-party contractors and makes it faster to incorporate the architect's comments.

Curing and demoulding

After forming, the element gains strength in the mould. Thin walls lose moisture quickly, so the first day is especially important: the element is covered with film or kept in a chamber with controlled humidity and temperature. Polymer admixtures in the mix partly address the problem but do not replace control.

Demoulding takes place once the element has reached stripping strength — usually the day after forming; the exact time depends on the mix design, temperature and element size. Demoulding too early is risky: cracks and chips can occur. After demoulding the element continues to cure, undergoes surface finishing (grinding, acid etching, sandblasting, water-repellent treatment) and only then is shipped.

Quality control in production

GFRC quality cannot be judged by eye alone: a mix design defect shows up months later. So control is organised by stage:

  • Incoming inspection of raw materials — cement, sand, fibre, admixtures.
  • Mix control — dosing, workability, fibre content (for example, by the wash-out test).
  • Forming control — layer thickness, compaction, position of embedded items.
  • Control samples — made from the same mix and tested in bending; where required, also for density, water absorption and frost resistance.
  • Acceptance of elements — geometry, absence of cracks and voids, colour and texture matching the approved reference sample.

According to the company, TECHNOFORMA Group carries out quality control at every production stage.

What affects production lead times

Production lead times depend on several factors:

  1. Design and layout. Developing element drawings and fixing details from the architectural design.
  2. Number of unique moulds. Each new profile needs a model and a mould; repeat elements are faster to produce.
  3. Volume and mould turnover. One mould yields a limited number of elements per day, so several sets are made for large volumes.
  4. Sample approval. Colour and texture are approved before the production run starts.
  5. Season and curing regime. Temperature affects the rate of strength gain.
  6. Logistics to the site.

Specific lead times for a project are set after costing. TECHNOFORMA Group's GFRC production capacity is more than 20,000 m² per month (company data).

Mix preparation: where GFRC production technology begins

Every forming method starts in the same way — with the preparation of a cement–sand slurry in a high-shear mixer. Everything that follows depends on how accurately the components are measured and in what order they are loaded: the workability of the mix, the operation of the pump and gun, the uniformity of colour. A mistake at this stage cannot be corrected during forming.

A typical mixing sequence is as follows:

  1. Water and liquid admixtures — plasticiser and polymer dispersion — are poured into the mixer.
  2. Pigment is added, if the product is through-coloured, and mixed until uniform.
  3. Sand is loaded, followed by cement and active mineral additions.
  4. The batch is mixed at high speed until a smooth, lump-free slurry is obtained.
  5. Workability is checked by the spread test and adjusted if necessary.
  6. For vibration casting, chopped fibre is added at low speed; for spraying, the fibre-free slurry is transferred to the pump.

All components are measured by mass, and the data for each batch are entered in a log: if a defect is found, this makes it possible to establish from which raw materials and when the product was made. Water is dosed with a correction for the moisture content of the sand; otherwise the water–cement ratio will vary from batch to batch.

Fibre for a casting mix is added last and mixed only briefly. Prolonged or excessively vigorous mixing breaks the bundles down into individual filaments and damages them, as a result of which the mix stiffens and the reinforcing effect is reduced. The prepared slurry is used within a limited time: a mix that has begun to set is not diluted with water but discarded. In warm weather the pot life of the slurry is noticeably shorter, so the batch size is matched to the pace of forming.

What forming methods are used besides spraying and casting?

In addition to the two basic methods, production makes use of their combinations and variants. The choice is determined by the shape of the product, the requirements for the face surface and the production run. It is useful to know about them when agreeing the technical brief: the method of manufacture affects the permissible wall thickness and the texture.

MethodPrincipleWhere it is used
Spraying with a face coatA thin fibre-free layer is applied first, then the reinforced backing layerPanels and decorative elements with an exposed texture
Sprayed premixA mix with short fibre already added is delivered through a special nozzleSmall products of complex shape, serial decorative elements
Casting in a closed mouldA fluid mix is poured into two-sided toolingBalusters, parts with two face sides
Combined methodThe face coat is sprayed, while thickenings and ribs are topped up or built up by handThree-dimensional elements with stiffening ribs

Several methods are usually combined on one project: wall surfaces are covered with sprayed panels, while small repeating details are cast. The manufacturer assigns a method to each item in the schedule, and this is worth clarifying when samples are agreed, since the texture of a sprayed and a cast surface may differ slightly after identical finishing.

Relief cladding elements are, as a rule, sprayed into flexible form liners: the thin wall follows a deep pattern without thickening. The possibilities and limitations of such relief are described in the article on three-dimensional GFRC panels. For bodies of revolution — shafts, bases and capitals — split moulds made up of several parts are more often used; this is how GFRC columns are made, which are then assembled on site around a load-bearing core.

Embedded parts, ribs and frame: how the load-bearing part of the product is formed

A thin wall cannot by itself transfer load to the subframe, so the elements by which the product will be fixed are installed in it while it is still in the mould. This is done before setting is complete, while the backing layer is still plastic. The position of each embedded part is specified in the working drawing and checked against a template.

Three main solutions are used:

  • Embedded parts. Sockets, plates and anchors are set into local thickenings of the wall and oversprayed with an additional layer. Suitable for small products and decorative elements.
  • Stiffening ribs. Ribs of the same material are formed on the back, often over cores of lightweight filler. The ribs limit deflection and prevent the panel from warping.
  • Steel frame. For large panels a welded frame is fabricated, suspended over the freshly sprayed skin and connected to it by flex anchors through GFRC bonding pads. The frame carries the weight and the wind, while the skin can deform freely as the temperature changes.

The frame is protected against corrosion before installation, and the connection points are distributed so that the panel is not restrained around its perimeter. The quality of this operation cannot be seen from the face side, so it is checked before demoulding: the pads are counted and their dimensions and the gap between frame and skin are verified. The choice between ribs and a frame is determined by the format — more on this on the page describing GFRC facade panels.

Finishing: what is done to the product after demoulding

After demoulding the product is not yet ready for dispatch: the surface is brought to the agreed texture, minor process defects are remedied and protection is applied. The list of operations depends on the type of finish approved from the sample.

  • Trimming and dressing of edges. Flash along the mould parting line is removed, the edges are trued and the geometry is checked against a template.
  • Sandblasting. Removes the cement laitance and exposes the aggregate grain; the depth of treatment sets the degree of roughness.
  • Acid etching. Gives a finer, velvety texture; afterwards the surface must be rinsed and neutralised.
  • Grinding and polishing. Used for smooth surfaces with an exposed pattern of stone chippings.
  • Filling of pores. Small blowholes on smooth products intended for painting are filled with a repair compound based on the same cement.
  • Painting or water-repellent treatment. Carried out after strength gain and drying, on a clean surface.

Minor defects made good in production are acceptable provided the repaired area does not stand out in colour and texture. It is convenient to agree the criteria for such acceptance in advance on a sample, so that the assessment of finished products does not depend on subjective impression.

Finishing is carried out under a single regime for the whole batch; otherwise adjacent products will differ in tone: the depth of sandblasting alters the shade more than might be expected. The reference sample is therefore kept by both manufacturer and client, and finished products are compared with it under the same lighting. Types of finish and their behaviour on the facade are discussed in the article on GFRC textures.

Marking, storage and dispatch of finished products

The last stage of production is preparing the products for transport. Thin-walled elements are more often damaged in storage and loading than on the facade, so the rules for stacking are regarded as part of the technology.

Each product receives marking that corresponds to the installation layout: element number, facade, level. This allows dispatch to be made up in the order in which the products will be installed, without re-handling on site. Panels are stored and transported in a vertical or near-vertical position on A-frames, with spacers of soft, non-staining material. Three-dimensional decorative elements are placed on pallets with the face side arranged so that it does not touch adjacent products.

Accompanying documents are handed over with the batch: the certificate, a schedule of products with marking and mass, and a slinging diagram for large elements. On acceptance at site the products are inspected before unloading, in order to distinguish transport damage from production defects.

Before dispatch the products are kept until they reach release strength. Sending out «green» elements is a frequent cause of cracks during installation. Packaging must be breathable: under impermeable film condensation forms on the surface and leaves marks on light-coloured products.

Production defects: where they come from and how they are prevented

Most production defects have an identifiable cause and are prevented by keeping to the process regime, not by subsequent repair. Knowing the typical defects helps at acceptance: the type of deviation indicates which stage was at fault.

DefectProbable causeHow it is prevented
Blowholes on the face surfaceEntrapped air, insufficient compaction or vibrationSelection of workability, rolling of the face coat, vibration regime
Fibres showing throughThin or absent face coatControl of face coat thickness
Shade variation within a batchFluctuations in sand moisture, pigment dosage, finishing regimeBatching by weight, a single lot of raw materials, a reference sample
Uneven wall thicknessOperator error when spraying over complex reliefMeasuring thickness with a depth gauge at control points
DelaminationA break between layers, poor rollingApplying layers without interruption, compaction layer by layer
Warping of the panelUneven drying, early demoulding, storage laid flatCuring, correct storage

FAQ

What is the difference between GFRC spray-up and vibration casting?

In spray-up the fibre is chopped in the gun and applied in layers together with the mortar, giving thin panels high flexural strength. In vibration casting the fibre is pre-mixed and the mix is compacted by vibration, which is more convenient for three-dimensional decoration.

How is a ventilated rainscreen facade with GFRC panels installed?

Installation includes setting out, fixing brackets, laying insulation, a wind barrier where required, installing rails and hanging the panels on fixings with movement joints and a ventilated cavity.

What insulation is used behind a ventilated facade?

Mineral (stone) wool boards fixed to the wall with insulation anchors are usually used; the insulation must meet the fire performance requirements and the project's thermal calculation.

Can a facade be insulated with stone wool and clad with GFRC?

Yes, this is a typical ventilated rainscreen build-up: mineral wool insulation on the wall, a substructure and GFRC panels with an air gap. TECHNOFORMA Group installs insulation and ventilated facade substructures as part of its construction works.

How long do GFRC elements cure before demoulding?

Demoulding is usually done the day after forming, but the exact time depends on the mix design, temperature and element size. The element continues to gain strength after demoulding.

What equipment is needed to produce GFRC?

A high-shear mixer, a slurry pump, a spray gun with a roving chopper and a compressor — for spraying; a mixer and a vibrating table — for casting. In addition, a pattern and mould shop, weighing equipment and the means to test the mix and samples are required.

Can a product be made with two face sides?

Yes. This is done by casting in a closed two-sided mould, or by making two halves and joining them before hardening is complete. Balusters, barriers and elements visible from all sides are made in this way.

When can a GFRC product be installed after manufacture?

Once it has reached release strength, which the manufacturer confirms on control samples. The period depends on the mix design and curing conditions, so it is stated in the batch documents and not judged by eye.
