3D Panels and Parametric GFRC Facades

3D facade panels are cladding elements with volumetric relief — waves, facets, pyramidal and curved surfaces that change the facade pattern depending on the light. Combined with digital design, they are used to build parametric facades in which every panel can differ in shape from its neighbour.

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) suits such tasks because it is a mouldable material: the mix follows any mould surface, and the thin reinforced skin keeps weight low even with deep relief. The main selection criterion is how many unique moulds the project will require: this affects lead times and cost more than the area does.

The solution appeals to architects and developers who want an expressive facade that is non-combustible (Russian class NG). Basic information on the material is in GFRC: material and technology.

What is a parametric facade

A parametric facade is one whose geometry is defined not by drawing each element but by an algorithm with a set of parameters: grid spacing, facet rotation angle, relief depth, a smooth transition from one module type to another. By changing the parameters, the architect gets dozens of design options in minutes and can link form to functional data — insolation, views from windows, wind.

In practice, the parametric approach takes three forms:

  • a repeating module with variation — one to three panel types arranged by different rules;
  • a gradient — the relief gradually increases or decreases along the height or length of the facade;
  • a fully unique surface — every element is individual, as in curved shells.

The closer a project is to the third option, the more complex and expensive it is to build, so the engineering task is to keep the visual effect with a minimum of unique moulds.

Parametric design is especially natural for GFRC: the material needs no machining, and the form is defined by the mould surface. What in metal would require bending and welding, and in stone milling from a solid block, is achieved in GFRC with a single cast. The only constraint is mould cost, so the designer thinks in advance about which elements can be made identical.

Design and moulds

Design of volumetric facade panels starts with a digital facade model. At this stage the team decides on:

  1. Rationalisation — the surface is split into panels so that the number of types is minimal and the joints follow the logic of the pattern.
  2. Manufacturability — draft angles for demoulding, minimum radii, absence of undercuts and thickness at projections are checked.
  3. Fixing — positions of cast-in inserts and the connection to the substructure are determined, bearing in mind that the centre of gravity of a relief panel is offset.
  4. Visualisation — renders and facade fragments in the light of different times of day and seasons.

TECHNOFORMA has a design department of 12 designers led by two architects, plus 3D visualisers. This makes it possible to work out complex geometry before moulds go into production.

Typical mistakes at this stage are edges that are too sharp and chip during demoulding and transport, and thin projecting details without sufficient reinforcement. It is advisable to round edges with a small radius, keep wall drafts and check how the relief will look in raking light: on a 3D facade, shadows become the main means of expression, and relief that is too shallow simply cannot be read at height.

The design output is a set of working drawings with the layout, panel marking, fixing details and drawings of each element type, from which master models are made.

Producing complex geometry

Producing complex GFRC geometry depends on moulds. A master model is made from the digital model, and from it a production mould: rigid (for simple facets) or flexible silicone (for relief with undercuts and texture). The company makes models and moulds in its own mould shops, which shortens the approval chain between design and production.

The mix is applied by spraying or casting. Spraying gives a higher fibre content and a thin skin, which is important for large 3D panels; casting is convenient for small elements with fine relief. Once the element has gained strength it is demoulded and its geometry and surface are checked. Quality control is carried out at every stage.

Installation

Installing 3D panels is more complex than flat cladding. Each panel has its own mark and position on the facade according to the installation plan; installing one element incorrectly breaks the pattern. The offset centre of gravity is accounted for in the bracket design, and three-axis adjustment allows the surface to be set without steps at the joints.

The sequence of work is standard for a rainscreen facade: setting out and survey, installation of brackets and substructure, insulation, hanging the panels, joint inspection. TECHNOFORMA's own installation division of more than 100 specialists carries out the full cycle: substructure, insulation and panel installation.

For complex-shaped panels, packaging and logistics matter a great deal: relief elements are transported in individual cassettes or with spacers so that projections are not damaged. On site they are stored in installation order. The company's warehouses stretch from Novorossiysk and Makhachkala to Novosibirsk and Yekaterinburg, which simplifies delivery to projects in different regions.

After installation, surfaces and pattern lines are checked; if necessary, chips are repaired locally with colour-matched repair compounds and a water-repellent sealer is applied.

Typical applications

Volumetric GFRC facade panels are used:

  • on entrance areas and ground floors, where the facade is seen up close;
  • on the gable ends of residential buildings as a large graphic element;
  • on business centres and retail buildings for a recognisable image;
  • on public buildings — theatres, museums, universities — where sculptural quality matters;
  • as sun-shading screens and perforated grilles in front of glazing.

In many cases two or three module types arranged by different rules are enough to create the effect of a complex surface.

If the budget is limited, the parametric effect is created by combination: flat cladding over the main area and volumetric panels on the principal facade or selected zones. Rotating identical modules by 90 or 180 degrees works the same way — one mould gives several pattern variants.

Sizes and parameters of GFRC 3D panels

The size of a three-dimensional panel is governed not by what can be moulded but by transport, installation and the joint pattern. In practice, GFRC 3D facade panels range from small modules lifted by two installers to large-format units on a steel frame set in place by crane.

ParameterTypical rangeWhat it depends on
Dimensions of a frameless moduleUsually up to 1–1.5 m on the longer sideDepth of relief, fixing method, weight
Dimensions of a framed panelAs a rule up to 3–4 m, more on individual projectsTransport restrictions, lifting capacity of the plant
Shell thicknessUsually 10–20 mmMoulding method, size, loads
Depth of reliefFrom 20–30 mm to several hundred millimetresArchitectural intent, demoulding conditions
Density of the materialAbout 1,900–2,100 kg/m³Mix design
Joint widthUsually 8–15 mmPanel size, thermal movement, installation tolerances

The values given are for guidance; the final parameters are set by the designer on the basis of calculation. The weight per square metre depends on the shell thickness, the depth of relief and whether there is a frame; how to estimate it is explained in the article on how much GFRC weighs.

The scale of perception is also taken into account when choosing the module size. Fine relief reads from a distance of a few metres and is lost on the upper storeys, so on tall buildings the module and depth are usually increased with height, or a larger pattern is used across the whole plane.

How relief works with light: facade orientation, depth and lighting

A three-dimensional facade is perceived through light and shade, so the same relief looks different on the south and north sides. Designing 3D panels without a daylight analysis means leaving the main effect to chance.

  • On sunny facades a moderate depth is enough: raking light in the morning and evening casts sharp shadows even on shallow facets.
  • On north-facing and shaded facades the light is diffuse and the shadows soft; here the relief is made deeper or emphasised by a contrast of textures.
  • Horizontal ribs respond more strongly to a high sun, vertical ribs to low side light.
  • Light shades of concrete show relief better than dark ones: on a dark surface the shadows merge with its own tone.

At concept stage it is useful to produce renderings for several times of day and different seasons, and for critical areas to make a full-size mock-up and view it outdoors. The evening appearance is shaped by artificial light: grazing light from below or from the side strengthens the relief, while frontal floodlighting flattens it. Positions for luminaires and cable routes are built into the panels and the subframe in advance; more on this in the article on architectural facade lighting.

Joints, corners and abutments in three-dimensional cladding

The most vulnerable part of a parametric facade is not the panel itself but the places where the relief meets a window, a building corner or an adjacent material. If these details are not resolved in the design, the complex surface breaks off arbitrarily and the effect is lost.

The main decisions taken at documentation stage:

  • Joints. They are either made part of the pattern, aligned with the ribs and folds of the relief, or hidden in the shadow of the facets. A joint crossing a smooth convex surface is always visible.
  • External corners. One-piece corner units or a mitred joint are used; a simple butt joint exposes the thickness of the shell.
  • Window openings. The relief is run out smoothly to the plane of the reveal, or the opening is framed with a neutral surround against which the pattern stops.
  • Plinth and parapet. At the bottom the relief usually ends in a smooth band resistant to dirt and impact; at the top, water is led away from the upper edge of the panels.
  • Junctions with other materials. Glass, metal and stone have different tolerances and movements, so a movement gap is left between the systems.

The general principles of such solutions for rainscreen systems are covered in the article on ventilated facade abutment details.

Colour and texture of three-dimensional facade panels

On a relief surface, texture and colour behave differently from a flat one: light strikes the facets at different angles, and a single-colour panel is perceived as several shades. For 3D panels, restrained solutions are therefore chosen more often: one integral colour and one texture across the whole pattern.

A smooth surface gives crisp facets and graphic shadows, but pores and tonal variation are more noticeable on it. A matt acid-etched or sandblasted texture conceals small differences and scatters light more softly. A stone-effect texture suits large, calm forms and competes with fine relief. The finishing options are described in the overview of GFRC textures.

The sample for colour approval should be three-dimensional, with the same relief as the production panel: a flat tile of the same mix looks lighter and more even.

If the concept calls for several colours, the boundary between them is best aligned with a joint or a change of panel type. A tonal transition within a single unit is technically possible, but it is hard to reproduce from batch to batch and complicates repair.

How do GFRC 3D panels differ from metal, composite and stone?

A three-dimensional facade can be made from several materials, and the choice depends on the nature of the geometry. GFRC wins where double-curvature forms, a monolithic stone-like appearance and non-combustibility are needed; metal wins where the relief is formed by flat facets and minimum weight is important.

MaterialGeometry availableCharacteristics
GFRCAnything mouldable: facets, double curvature, deep relief, textureNon-combustible, mineral surface; mould costs, economical with repetition
Aluminium cassettes and sheetFolding, perforation, pleated formsLow weight; double-curvature surfaces are difficult and expensive
Aluminium composite panelsFlat planes and simple foldsFast installation; use is restricted by fire requirements that depend on the panel type
Natural stoneRelief milled from the solidNatural texture; high weight and material consumption with deep relief
Architectural concreteThe same forms as GFRCConsiderably greater thickness and weight, heavier fixings required

In practice the materials are combined: three-dimensional GFRC units are placed in accent areas, while the main plane is covered with flat cladding. Examples of such solutions can be found in the section on GFRC facade panels.

When comparing options, it is useful to price not only the square metre of cladding but also the subframe: heavy materials require closer bracket spacing and reinforced rails, and the price difference may shift.

A relief facade in use: water, dust and care

Relief collects dust and water more readily than a flat wall, and this has to be taken into account in the geometry of the panel, not only in the maintenance schedule. A well-designed three-dimensional surface is washed by rain and does not streak.

  • Horizontal ledges and pockets where water lingers are eliminated or given an outward fall.
  • A drip is provided on the lower edge of projections so that water falls clear instead of running down the face.
  • The surface is treated with a water repellent; the treatment is renewed periodically as recommended by the manufacturer.
  • In areas accessible to passers-by, thin projecting ribs at head height are avoided.
  • Access is provided for washing: anchor points for cradles or the possibility of working from an aerial platform.

In areas with frequent freeze-thaw cycles it is especially important that water does not stand in the relief: it is freezing in the recesses that damages the edges over time.

The care schedule usually includes an inspection after winter, a check on the condition of the joints and local cleaning of the areas beneath projections that rain does not reach. Washing is carried out with water at moderate pressure, without abrasives or aggressive acid-based products.

What to prepare for pricing a parametric facade

For a preliminary estimate, a three-dimensional model of the surface or elevations showing the panel types are enough. The earlier the manufacturer sees the geometry, the easier it is to optimise the number of moulds without losing the design intent.

  • a 3D model of the facade in an exchange format, or the algorithm from which it was generated;
  • elevations with the dimensions of the areas covered by three-dimensional cladding;
  • the preferred colour and texture, and reference examples;
  • information on the load-bearing walls and the number of storeys;
  • the construction programme and phasing.

On the basis of this data the design office proposes a division into panels, estimates the number of unique types and offers options for simplification. Further development takes place as part of facade design, with the issue of working documentation.

Good results come from the architect and the manufacturer working together on one or two trial pieces: these are used to refine the depth of relief, the edge radii and the position of the joints before the decisions go into series production.

FAQ

What are 3D facade panels?

Cladding elements with volumetric relief — facets, waves, curved surfaces.

What are volumetric facade panels made of?

Mouldable materials — GFRC, fibre-reinforced concrete, composites, metal; GFRC is chosen for freedom of form, low weight and non-combustibility (Russian class NG).

How does a parametric facade differ from a conventional one?

Its geometry is defined by an algorithm with parameters, so panels can change shape smoothly across the whole facade.

Why are 3D panels more expensive than flat ones?

The main costs are master models and moulds; the more unique elements, the higher the price and the longer the lead time.

How are volumetric panels installed?

On a rainscreen facade substructure with three-axis adjustment, strictly following the installation plan with every element marked.

What sizes do GFRC 3D panels come in?

Small frameless modules are usually made up to 1–1.5 m on the longer side, and panels on a steel frame as a rule up to 3–4 m. Exact dimensions are set by calculation, taking account of weight, transport and the joint pattern.

Can three-dimensional panels be used on a high-rise building?

Yes. GFRC is non-combustible and relatively light, so it is suitable for high-rise facades. The fixings and subframe are calculated for wind load, and the relief on the upper storeys is usually made larger so that it reads from the ground.

How should a GFRC relief facade be cared for?

Periodic inspection, washing with water at moderate pressure and renewal of the water-repellent treatment as recommended by the manufacturer are sufficient. If falls and drips are built into the geometry, rain washes away most of the dirt by itself.
