Facades for Developers: How to Reduce the Risk of Delays

For a developer, the facade is not only the building's appearance but also one of the longest and riskiest parts of the schedule: cladding starts after the frame and walls and finishes just before handover, when there is no time buffer left. Any delay in delivery, rework of a detail or shortage of installers pushes back commissioning.

The main criterion when choosing a solution and a contractor is how controllable the 'design → production → delivery → installation' chain is. The fewer hand-overs of responsibility between different companies, the fewer points where a delay cannot be controlled.

This article is for developers, technical clients and general contractors of apartment buildings, business centres and hotels. The general approach to choosing cladding and working with the design is covered in the section Facade Design and Selection.

Facade Risks

Most facade delays have the same causes. It is convenient to break them down by stage:

StageTypical riskConsequence
DesignNo working documentation for the substructure and cladding; details not coordinated with windows and roofRework on site, idle crews
ProductionThe manufacturer has no moulds of its own or no capacity for the volumeDelivery drags on, batches arrive out of sequence
LogisticsNo warehouses in the region, poor packaging, breakage in transitReordering and remanufacturing of elements
InstallationShortage of crews, subcontractors with no experience of the materialPoor quality, repeated installation
ApprovalsSubstitution of 'an equivalent' material without approving the appearanceRepeat approvals, claims

Seasonality is a separate risk. Some works (wet processes, sealing, painting) are limited by temperature, and if the facade falls in winter, the schedule must allow for these limits in advance rather than shifting works as they arise.

Choosing a Contractor

A facade contractor for a developer should cover as many links of the chain as possible with its own resources. What to look at:

  • Design department: who issues the shop drawings and panel layout; are there architects who understand the author's intent.
  • Own production: does the contractor manufacture the cladding itself or resell someone else's; does it make models and moulds for decoration.
  • Capacity: does monthly output match the area of your facade and the schedule.
  • Installation team: in-house crews or one-off subcontractors.
  • Completed projects of comparable scale — with addresses you can visit.

For comparison: according to the company, TECHNOFORMA Group works as a full-cycle contractor — a design department (12 designers, 2 architects), its own mould-making workshops, glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) production with a capacity of over 20,000 m² per month on a site of more than 2 ha, logistics and its own installation team of 100+ specialists who install the substructure, insulation and panels. Contract and Schedule

The contract is the main tool for managing deadlines. What is worth fixing in it:

  1. Stages with separate deadlines: issue and approval of working documentation, manufacture of the first batch, delivery by work zones, installation by facade or section.
  2. Input data and deadlines for the client to provide it: surveys, architectural decisions, work front. Delays on the client's side are a common cause of disputes.
  3. Procedure for approving samples and references of texture and colour before series production.
  4. Readiness of the work front: what counts as a 'facade handed over for installation' — walls, windows, roof junctions, scaffolding or lifts.
  5. Change procedure: how design changes are documented and how they affect the deadline and price.

It is convenient to build the production and installation schedule backwards from the commissioning date. Production of GFRC decoration starts with making models and moulds, so unique elements must be approved earlier than standard ones. The cost of facade works is determined after inspecting the site and studying the design, so a fixed price can only be written into the contract after this stage.

Quality Control

Quality control on a facade has three levels, and the client needs visibility of each:

  • Incoming control at production: raw materials, mix design, compliance of elements with dimensions and the texture reference. At TECHNOFORMA, quality is controlled at every stage of production.
  • Control on delivery: marking of elements according to the layout, completeness, no chips or cracks, batch documents.
  • Operational control of installation: anchoring of brackets, spacing and projection of the substructure, thickness and fixing of insulation, wind barrier, joints and gaps in the cladding.

Most defects of a ventilated facade become concealed within a few days of installation, so certificates of concealed works are signed as the work proceeds, not at the end. It makes sense to involve the client's technical supervision and the architect's design supervision precisely at the substructure stage and on the first cladding zones — then errors are corrected on one section rather than across the whole building.

Acceptance

Acceptance should be staged and based on documentation, not just a visual inspection. The minimum set:

  • certificates of inspection of concealed works for the substructure, insulation and wind barrier;
  • as-built drawings showing the actual position of elements;
  • quality documents for materials and products;
  • geometry checks: flatness, verticality, width and straightness of joints;
  • checks of junctions with windows, parapets and plinth.

Defects are recorded in a list with photos and a deadline for rectification. If acceptance is split by work zones, defects are fixed while the crew is still on site and do not pile up towards the end, when there is no time for corrections.

Final acceptance is best carried out by a commission including the client's technical supervision, the general contractor and a representative of the design author. For it, the contractor hands over a set of as-built documentation that will later be needed in operation and for any warranty claims: without it, it is difficult to prove that a defect was not caused by a breach of installation technology.

How to build a facade works programme back from the completion date?

A facade programme for a developer is built by counting backwards: from the date of the completion certificate to the moment when the architecture must be approved. This order immediately shows which decisions are already late even though installation is still some way off.

Milestone (from end to start)What must be ready by this pointWho is responsible
Completion of the projectFacade accepted, as-built documentation handed overDeveloper, main contractor
Striking of scaffolding and external worksRemarks on cladding and junctions rectifiedFacade contractor
Installation of cladding and decorationSubframe and insulation accepted and signed off, units on site in section orderFacade contractor
Installation of subframe and insulationWalls and windows handed over, survey completed, anchors testedMain contractor, facade contractor
Series production of unitsReference samples approved, moulds made, first batch checkedManufacturer
Making of patterns and mouldsWorking documentation for the units agreedDesigner, manufacturer
Working documentationAppearance, materials and input data approvedDeveloper, architect

The duration of each interval depends on volume and complexity and is determined for the particular project. It is useful to allow a reserve between production and installation: the units need time to gain strength, to be made up into sets and to be transported. Approaches to estimating the duration of the works are described in the article on how to plan facade installation times.

What work front must the developer hand over to the facade contractor?

The work front is considered handed over when the contractor can start installation and not stop because of other trades. In practice a considerable share of delays arises exactly here: the walls are formally ready, but it is impossible to work on them.

  • External walls and slab edges are complete over the full height of the section, and the mortar and concrete have gained sufficient strength for anchoring.
  • Window and balcony units are installed and the perimeter joints are closed; the actual positions of the frames are known.
  • The as-built survey of the facade planes has been carried out and handed over.
  • Roofing work on the parapets has reached a stage that allows the junctions to be made.
  • Service outlets on the facade and fixings for external units, lighting and signage have been located.
  • A hard-surfaced storage area, vehicle access and power connection points have been provided.
  • It has been agreed who erects the scaffolding, hoists or cradles, and when.

The handover of the front is recorded by a certificate for each section. The choice of access equipment affects both time and cost; a comparison of the options is given in the article on scaffolding and cradles.

How to synchronise production and installation on a large volume

Synchronisation rests on three things: a single division of the facade into sections, marking of units according to that division, and a buffer of finished products. Production must run ahead of installation, but not so far that the site turns into a warehouse.

The sections are defined in the design — by block, elevation and tier. The schedule of units is divided in the same way, and the workshop produces not "cornices in general" but a set for a specific area. Each delivery arrives with a packing list of marks; acceptance on site comes down to checking the list and an inspection.

The buffer is needed in case of breakage in transit and defects found at acceptance. For unique units a repeat order takes longer than for standard ones, so it makes sense to manufacture them at the beginning of the series. On multi-block residential complexes repetition helps further: moulds proven on the first block are used unchanged on the following ones. The requirements for such buildings are discussed in the article on facades of multi-storey residential buildings.

Early signs of programme slippage on a facade

A delay is rarely sudden: signs usually appear several weeks beforehand and can be seen in the reports and on site. It is useful for the developer to track them at weekly meetings.

  • Working documentation is issued in parts, and installation has already begun on an incomplete set.
  • Reference samples of texture and colour have not been approved although series production is under way according to the programme.
  • Units on site belong to a section other than the one being installed, and the marks needed are missing.
  • Crew output is consistently below plan and the gap is not narrowing.
  • Certificates for concealed works are signed several sections late.
  • The number of open remarks from the client's supervisor grows faster than it is closed.
  • The contractor changes the make-up of crews or brings in new gangs without training.
  • The work front is handed over piecemeal, and installers move from area to area without finishing any.

Each of these signs has a simple numerical expression: percentage of sheets issued, number of approved samples, square metres per week, number of open remarks. If such indicators are included in the contractor's weekly report, the lag is discussed while it can still be recovered.

A single contractor or several contracts: how to allocate responsibility

The allocation of responsibility determines whom the developer will hold to account for a missed date. The more separate contracts there are, the more interfaces at which each party can point to the other.

ArrangementAdvantagesRisks
One contractor: design, production, installationSingle responsibility for time and result, short route for resolving issuesDependence on one firm; its capacity must be checked thoroughly
Design separately, supply and installation under one contractIndependent designer, competition between contractors at tenderDisputes about buildability and completeness of the documentation
Separate contracts for design, supply and installationOpportunity to choose the best terms for each partCoordination falls on the client; idle time due to uncoordinated deliveries
Supply of units with manufacturer's supervision, installation by the main contractorWork for the main contractor's own crewsQuality depends on the skill of the crews and on discipline in following instructions

Under any arrangement the dates for handing over documentation, deliveries and the work front must match across the contracts. How to formulate the requirements and compare bids is explained in the article on a facade tender and its technical brief.

It is worth defining separately who coordinates the adjacent works on the facade: window installation, roof junctions, routing of services, fitting of lighting. If there is no such person, each contractor carries out its part at a time convenient to itself, and cladding has to be removed to let a late trade through. This function is usually taken on by the main contractor, and the interaction procedure is fixed in a combined programme signed by all participants. The same document should state who reinstates cladding damaged during the work of other organisations.

Facade of a residential complex, a business centre and a hotel: what changes for the developer

The type of project changes the priorities: in one case the main thing is pace, in another the quality of detail at eye level, in a third working without closing a building in use.

  • Residential complex. Large areas and repeating blocks; the key factors are steady deliveries, a programme tied to the dates promised to buyers, and phased completion of blocks.
  • Business centre. Cladding combined with large glazed areas, high demands on geometry and on the details around curtain walling; the facade affects the class of the property and rental rates.
  • Hotel. Particular attention to the entrance and lower floors, often complex decoration; opening is usually tied to the season, and a postponement means lost revenue.
  • Public building. Approvals of the appearance take longer, and acceptance involves several authorities.

For tall and long buildings the solution is usually based on a rainscreen system; the scope and sequence of the works are described on the page on installation of ventilated facades.

The winter period in a facade programme: what to postpone and what to continue?

In winter the "dry" operations continue, and everything involving water, adhesives and sealants is postponed where their use is not permitted at sub-zero temperatures. It is convenient to build this rule into the programme at the planning stage rather than decide in November.

Installation of brackets, rails and insulation on disc anchors, and hanging of mechanically fixed cladding, are as a rule possible in frost, subject to limits on wind, precipitation and icing. Wet processes, joint filling, painting and water-repellent treatment depend on the temperature range stated by the material manufacturer. Production of units in a heated workshop does not depend on the season, so the winter months are suitable for building up a stock of decoration.

The practical conclusion for the developer: if the building envelope is closed in autumn, the subframe and the main cladding can proceed in winter, while the finishing operations are planned for spring as a separate line in the programme. Details are given in the article on facade works in winter conditions.

FAQ

Who is responsible for the facade — the general contractor or the facade contractor?

The general contractor is responsible to the developer if the facade is part of its contract. The facade contractor works under a contract with the general contractor or directly with the client; the split of responsibility is fixed in the contracts.

How can the risk of facade delays be reduced?

Issue working documentation before production starts, approve references in advance, choose a contractor with its own production and installation, and accept work in stages.

When should GFRC facade decoration be ordered?

As soon as the architecture is approved: production of unique elements starts with making models and moulds, and this must be built into the schedule.

Can the facade price be fixed at the start?

Only after studying the design and inspecting the site. At TECHNOFORMA, the cost is determined after the inspection.

What should be checked when accepting a facade from a contractor?

Certificates of concealed works for the substructure and insulation, as-built drawings, material documents, cladding geometry and junctions.

How much float should be allowed in a facade programme?

There is no single norm: the float depends on the share of unique units, the season and the readiness of the work front. In practice it is placed between production and installation and before the scaffolding is struck — where a delay costs most. Specific values are determined when the programme is developed for the project.

What should the facade contractor's weekly report contain?

The percentage of documentation issued, the number of units manufactured and dispatched by section, the area installed during the week and cumulatively, crew numbers, the number of open and closed remarks, and a list of issues requiring a decision by the client.

Can facade installation begin before the windows are fitted?

The subframe can be started on blank areas of wall, but the cladding and the surrounds of openings are installed after the windows: their position determines the dimensions of reveals, sills and decorative elements. Installation without the frames in place leads to units being trimmed on site.
