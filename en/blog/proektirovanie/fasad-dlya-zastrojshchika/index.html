Facades for Developers: How to Reduce the Risk of Delays

Updated: 2026-09-25 · TECHNOFORMA editorial team

Contents

  2. Choosing a Contractor

For a developer, the facade is not only the building's appearance but also one of the longest and riskiest parts of the schedule: cladding starts after the frame and walls and finishes just before handover, when there is no time buffer left. Any delay in delivery, rework of a detail or shortage of installers pushes back commissioning.

The main criterion when choosing a solution and a contractor is how controllable the 'design → production → delivery → installation' chain is. The fewer hand-overs of responsibility between different companies, the fewer points where a delay cannot be controlled.

This article is for developers, technical clients and general contractors of apartment buildings, business centres and hotels. The general approach to choosing cladding and working with the design is covered in the section Facade Design and Selection.

Facade Risks

Most facade delays have the same causes. It is convenient to break them down by stage:

StageTypical riskConsequence
DesignNo working documentation for the substructure and cladding; details not coordinated with windows and roofRework on site, idle crews
ProductionThe manufacturer has no moulds of its own or no capacity for the volumeDelivery drags on, batches arrive out of sequence
LogisticsNo warehouses in the region, poor packaging, breakage in transitReordering and remanufacturing of elements
InstallationShortage of crews, subcontractors with no experience of the materialPoor quality, repeated installation
ApprovalsSubstitution of 'an equivalent' material without approving the appearanceRepeat approvals, claims

Seasonality is a separate risk. Some works (wet processes, sealing, painting) are limited by temperature, and if the facade falls in winter, the schedule must allow for these limits in advance rather than shifting works as they arise.

Choosing a Contractor

A facade contractor for a developer should cover as many links of the chain as possible with its own resources. What to look at:

  • Design department: who issues the shop drawings and panel layout; are there architects who understand the author's intent.
  • Own production: does the contractor manufacture the cladding itself or resell someone else's; does it make models and moulds for decoration.
  • Capacity: does monthly output match the area of your facade and the schedule.
  • Installation team: in-house crews or one-off subcontractors.
  • Completed projects of comparable scale — with addresses you can visit.

Contract and Schedule

The contract is the main tool for managing deadlines. What is worth fixing in it:

  1. Stages with separate deadlines: issue and approval of working documentation, manufacture of the first batch, delivery by work zones, installation by facade or section.
  2. Input data and deadlines for the client to provide it: surveys, architectural decisions, work front. Delays on the client's side are a common cause of disputes.
  3. Procedure for approving samples and references of texture and colour before series production.
  4. Readiness of the work front: what counts as a 'facade handed over for installation' — walls, windows, roof junctions, scaffolding or lifts.
  5. Change procedure: how design changes are documented and how they affect the deadline and price.

It is convenient to build the production and installation schedule backwards from the commissioning date. Production of GFRC decoration starts with making models and moulds, so unique elements must be approved earlier than standard ones. The cost of facade works is determined after inspecting the site and studying the design, so a fixed price can only be written into the contract after this stage.

Quality Control

Quality control on a facade has three levels, and the client needs visibility of each:

  • Incoming control at production: raw materials, mix design, compliance of elements with dimensions and the texture reference. At TECHNOFORMA, quality is controlled at every stage of production.
  • Control on delivery: marking of elements according to the layout, completeness, no chips or cracks, batch documents.
  • Operational control of installation: anchoring of brackets, spacing and projection of the substructure, thickness and fixing of insulation, wind barrier, joints and gaps in the cladding.

Most defects of a ventilated facade become concealed within a few days of installation, so certificates of concealed works are signed as the work proceeds, not at the end. It makes sense to involve the client's technical supervision and the architect's design supervision precisely at the substructure stage and on the first cladding zones — then errors are corrected on one section rather than across the whole building.

Acceptance

Acceptance should be staged and based on documentation, not just a visual inspection. The minimum set:

  • certificates of inspection of concealed works for the substructure, insulation and wind barrier;
  • as-built drawings showing the actual position of elements;
  • quality documents for materials and products;
  • geometry checks: flatness, verticality, width and straightness of joints;
  • checks of junctions with windows, parapets and plinth.

Defects are recorded in a list with photos and a deadline for rectification. If acceptance is split by work zones, defects are fixed while the crew is still on site and do not pile up towards the end, when there is no time for corrections.

Final acceptance is best carried out by a commission including the client's technical supervision, the general contractor and a representative of the design author. For it, the contractor hands over a set of as-built documentation that will later be needed in operation and for any warranty claims: without it, it is difficult to prove that a defect was not caused by a breach of installation technology.

FAQ

Who is responsible for the facade — the general contractor or the facade contractor?

The general contractor is responsible to the developer if the facade is part of its contract. The facade contractor works under a contract with the general contractor or directly with the client; the split of responsibility is fixed in the contracts.

How can the risk of facade delays be reduced?

Issue working documentation before production starts, approve references in advance, choose a contractor with its own production and installation, and accept work in stages.

When should GFRC facade decoration be ordered?

As soon as the architecture is approved: production of unique elements starts with making models and moulds, and this must be built into the schedule.

Can the facade price be fixed at the start?

Only after studying the design and inspecting the site. At TECHNOFORMA, the cost is determined after the inspection.

What should be checked when accepting a facade from a contractor?

Certificates of concealed works for the substructure and insulation, as-built drawings, material documents, cladding geometry and junctions.
