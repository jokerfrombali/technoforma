Choosing Facade Materials for the Regional Climate

A facade in a southern region must withstand strong heat and ultraviolet radiation, large daily temperature swings, downpours and wind, and on the coast salt aerosols as well. In central Russia, frost resistance under frequent freeze-thaw cycles comes first; in the north, prolonged frost, snow loads and thermal protection.

The main selection criterion is not abstract 'strength' but the resistance of the material and details to the specific climatic conditions of the site. Mineral materials — natural stone, glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC), porcelain stoneware — are broadly suitable for all zones, but with different requirements for rock type, mix design, fixing and protection.

This article is for architects and developers designing buildings in different regions. Related topics are in the section Facade Design and Selection.

Climatic Loads

Climatic loads on a facade are best divided into groups. Each requires its own check of the material and structure:

ActionWhat happensWhat to check
Heat and solar radiationThermal movement, pigment fading, ageing of polymersThermal gaps, colour lightfastness, sealant durability
Daily temperature swingsCyclic expansion and contraction, fatigue of fixingsMovable details, spacing of movement joints
Freeze-thawBreakdown of porous materials when saturated with waterFrost resistance grade, water absorption
WindPressure and suction, vibrationSubstructure calculation, element fixings
Driving rainWetting, leaks at junctionsWater absorption, design of sills and cornices
Salts (sea, de-icing chemicals)Efflorescence, corrosion of metalSubstructure material, cladding resistance
Seismic activityInertial loads on the claddingFixings and gaps by calculation

Facades in hot climates are often underestimated: in summer, dark cladding on the south side can heat up far above the air temperature and then cool quickly at night. It is these swings, not the absolute temperature, that load the fixings and joints.

The South and the Caucasus

Southern Russia and the North Caucasus mean hot summers and intense sun; in the foothills and on the coast, strong winds and downpours; and in a number of areas, elevated seismicity. Winters are mild, but freeze-thaw transitions occur, and in mountain areas severe frost is possible.

What to consider when choosing materials:

  • Colour lightfastness: integral pigments in GFRC and the natural colour of stone resist fading better than many painted surfaces; light tones heat up less.
  • Thermal gaps between cladding elements and movable fixings.
  • Salt environment on the Caspian and Black Sea coasts: corrosion-resistant substructure and fixings, stone resistant to efflorescence.
  • Seismicity: the cladding and its fixings are designed for seismic loads; lightweight materials reduce inertial forces.
  • Fire safety: non-combustible cladding on high-rise buildings.

GFRC is in demand in this region: it is lightweight, non-combustible (NG class, according to the manufacturer) and allows decoration in keeping with the local architectural tradition. Most TECHNOFORMA Group projects are located here — in Dagestan, Chechnya, Karachay-Cherkessia and Stavropol Krai.

Central Russia

Central Russia has moderately cold winters with frequent thaws, a large number of freeze-thaw cycles per season, de-icing chemicals near pavements and wet snow. The most vulnerable zone of the facade is the plinth and lower floors, which receive water with snow and de-icing salts.

  • Frost resistance of the cladding is the key property; for stone it depends on the rock type and quarry, for GFRC on the mix design and admixtures.
  • Water absorption: the lower it is, the lower the risk of damage when water freezes in the pores.
  • Plinth: dense stones — granite, basalt, dense limestone — or cladding designed for contact with de-icing chemicals.
  • Water shedding: sills, cornices with drip edges, no horizontal ledges where snow can accumulate.

A ventilated rainscreen facade works well in this climate: the ventilated cavity removes moisture from the insulation, and the cladding protects the wall from direct wetting.

The North

The north and Siberia have long, severe frosts, heavy snow loads, strong winds and a short construction season. Facade requirements here are stricter:

  • High frost resistance of the cladding, confirmed by tests for the specific batch or mix design.
  • Insulation thickness per thermal calculation — usually greater than in central regions, which increases bracket projection.
  • Fixings designed for thermal movement of the substructure with a large winter–summer temperature range.
  • Snow and ice: cornices and projecting decoration are designed so that snow does not accumulate on them and icicles do not form above pavements.
  • Ease of installation in a short season: factory-finished elements, a minimum of wet processes on site.

Work schedules on northern projects should allow for the season: some operations — sealing, painting — are limited by temperature.

How climate affects the facade subframe and fixings

Climate determines not only the choice of cladding but also the material of the load-bearing subframe: the metal of brackets, rails and fixings is the first to react to humidity, salts and temperature swings. Cladding may last for decades, yet the service life of the facade is ultimately limited by the weakest element of the frame.

In a dry continental climate, galvanised steel with a polymer coating or aluminium alloys are usually sufficient. In coastal areas and in cities where de-icing agents are used heavily, corrosion-resistant steel is specified more often, and fixings are made of stainless grades only. Contact pairs are checked separately: aluminium and steel without insulating pads form a galvanic couple in a humid environment and deteriorate faster than the design life.

Thermal movement also depends on the metal: aluminium expands under heat roughly twice as much as steel, so in regions with a large annual temperature range the length of rails and the design of sliding joints are set by calculation. More on frame types can be found in the article on ventilated facade subframes.

What insulation and what cavity are needed in different climate zones?

Insulation thickness and the arrangement of the air cavity are set by a thermal calculation for the specific city rather than copied from another project. The higher the heating degree-days, the higher the required thermal resistance of the wall and the thicker the mineral wool layer.

ZoneInsulationAir cavity and protectionSpecifics
South, coastMinimum calculated thickness; the layer also protects against overheatingThe cavity matters for removing heat and moisture after downpoursProtecting insulation from driving rain during installation
Central RussiaMedium thickness, usually in two layers with staggered jointsA consistent cavity over the full height, vents at the plinth and under the parapetRisk of wetting during thaws
Urals, Siberia, the NorthIncreased thickness, long brackets with thermal breaksProtection against wind washing, wind barrier by calculationThe share of heat loss through brackets grows

As insulation thickness grows, bracket projection increases, and with it the load on anchors and the heat loss through metal. In cold regions it therefore makes sense to calculate the facade as a whole: cladding, frame and facade insulation as a single system, taking point thermal bridges into account.

The Urals, Siberia and the Volga region: what a continental climate changes

A sharply continental climate combines the loads of the south and the north: in summer the cladding heats up in the sun, in winter it cools to minus thirty and below. The annual range of surface temperature can exceed one hundred degrees, and this is the main design parameter for joints and fixings.

  • Movement joints and gaps between panels are sized for the full annual range, not for an average winter.
  • Sealants and gaskets are selected with proven elasticity at low temperatures.
  • Test reports are requested for the cladding: the frost resistance of facade materials must be confirmed for the specific mix or stone type.
  • Snow drifts on cornices, parapets and projecting decor are treated as an additional load on fixings.

Low precipitation does not mean a dry facade: in spring, meltwater from cornices and sills freezes and thaws repeatedly, so drainage and drip edges matter here just as much as in central Russia.

What climate input data the facade designer needs

Choosing facade materials for the regional climate requires a small set of input data, most of which comes from current codes of practice and site survey reports. It is worth collecting them before the concept is developed, so that the solution does not have to change at the working documentation stage.

  1. Climatic parameters of the site under SP 131.13330 Building Climatology: design temperatures, length of the heating season, humidity.
  2. Wind and snow zone under SP 20.13330 Loads and Actions, terrain type, building height.
  3. Site seismicity in points under SP 14.13330 and survey data.
  4. Degree of environmental aggressiveness: proximity of the sea, industrial emissions, roads treated with de-icing agents.
  5. Orientation of the facades and shading by neighbouring buildings.
  6. Required thermal resistance of walls under SP 50.13330 Thermal Performance of Buildings.

Using these data, the designer calculates the subframe, sets the gaps and selects the cladding. For three-dimensional GFRC elements the same data define the shell thickness, the spacing of embedded parts and the fixing layout — this is how GFRC facade panels are designed, for example.

Seasonality of installation and facade care in different climates

Climate also affects the works calendar. Mechanically fixed systems are installed almost all year round, whereas wet processes — adhesives, joint grouting, sealing, water-repellent treatment — are limited by the temperature and moisture of the substrate.

In the south the main difficulty is heat and wind: mortars and sealants lose workability quickly, so work is moved to morning and evening hours. In central Russia and the north the short warm season is reserved for wet operations, while the frame and cladding are installed in winter subject to technological restrictions; see the article on how facade works in winter are carried out.

Maintenance regimes differ too. On the coast, cladding and exposed metal are periodically washed to remove salt deposits; in cities using de-icing agents special attention is paid to the plinth; in the north, cornices, sills and joints are inspected after winter.

A facade by the sea: what to consider in coastal cities

For a coastal city the main actions are salt aerosol, high humidity, strong wind and driving rain. They act simultaneously, so cladding, metal and joints are checked more strictly here than inland at the same temperatures.

Salt settles on the surface and in the pores of the material and crystallises as it dries. In porous stone this eventually leads to efflorescence and flaking of the face layer, so for facades exposed to the sea dense stone or GFRC with low water absorption is usually chosen, and the surface is treated with a water repellent where necessary, with periodic renewal.

  • Wind load is calculated with regard to open terrain and building height; suction is highest at corners and parapets, so fixing spacing is reduced there.
  • Horizontal surfaces of decor — cornices, string courses, sills — are made with a fall and a drip edge so that salty water does not run down the wall.
  • Window abutments and parapet details are sealed with UV-resistant materials.
  • Embedded parts and concealed fixings of three-dimensional elements are specified in stainless steel: their condition is difficult to inspect later.
  • The maintenance schedule includes washing the facade with fresh water, as a rule once every one or two years.

On the first line of development the influence of the sea is at its greatest, but just a few blocks from the shore it is noticeably weaker. The degree of environmental aggressiveness is therefore established for the specific plot rather than for the city as a whole.

Typical mistakes when transferring a facade solution to another region

Problems most often arise when details and materials proven in one region are repeated in another without recalculation. The mistake does not show at once but after several seasons.

  • Porous stone that performed well in the south is used in a zone of frequent thaws without checking the water absorption of the material.
  • Dark cladding is installed on a south elevation with the same gaps as light cladding.
  • A subframe designed for a temperate climate is used by the sea without changing the steel grade and fixings.
  • Insulation thickness is taken by analogy, without a thermal calculation.
  • Seismic action is considered for the building frame but not for the cladding and decor.
  • Cornices and sills are designed without drip edges and falls, counting on a dry climate.

Calculation for the specific site and testing of samples from the actual batch of material help to avoid this.

FAQ

Which facade is best for a southern region?

Mineral cladding with durable colour and well-designed thermal gaps: GFRC, natural stone, porcelain stoneware; on the coast, with a corrosion-resistant substructure.

What matters for a facade in a hot climate?

Resistance to heat and UV, light tones, movable fixings and movement joints designed for daily temperature swings.

How do I check a facade's frost resistance?

By the frost resistance grade of the cladding in the product passport or test report, and by the material's water absorption.

Is GFRC suitable for northern regions?

Yes, if the frost resistance of the mix design is confirmed by tests and the fixings are designed for thermal movement.

Which stone should I choose for a plinth?

Dense frost-resistant stones — granite, basalt, dense limestone; the final choice depends on test results for the specific quarry.

Does the facade subframe need to change for a coastal city?

As a rule, yes. Salt aerosols accelerate corrosion, so near the sea corrosion-resistant steel or aluminium alloys with a protective coating and stainless steel fixings are usually specified, and contact between dissimilar metals is isolated with pads. The exact option is determined by calculation and by the degree of environmental aggressiveness on site.

Does cladding colour affect the durability of the facade?

Indirectly, it does. A dark surface heats up in the sun considerably more than a light one, which increases thermal movement of panels and the load on joints and fixings. For dark facades on the south side, gaps and sliding joints are sized with a margin, and the colour is chosen with stable mineral pigments.

Can one facade solution be used on projects in different regions?

The architectural solution can; the structural one only after recalculation. The appearance, layout and cladding material may be retained, but the subframe, insulation thickness, gaps, anchors and frost resistance requirements are checked again for the climatic and seismic conditions of each site.
