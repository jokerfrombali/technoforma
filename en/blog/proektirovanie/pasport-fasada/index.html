Facade Passport: What It Is and When You Need One

Updated: 2026-09-25 · TECHNOFORMA editorial team

A facade passport is a document that records the architectural appearance of a building: its colour scheme, cladding materials, the layout and design of windows, doors, balconies, signage, external equipment and decorative elements. It is used to approve the building's appearance with the local architecture authority and then to control any subsequent changes.

The key point: a passport is not working documentation for installation but a 'reference image' of the building. It answers the question of how the facade should look, not how to assemble it. That is why it makes sense to develop it alongside the concept, while materials and divisions can still be changed without reworking the shop drawings.

The document is needed by developers, property management companies and building owners in cities where local improvement rules set requirements for external appearance. The general approach to concept work and cladding selection is described in the section Facade Design and Selection.

What a Facade Passport Contains

The contents of a passport are set by local improvement rules and the regulations of the architecture authority, so they differ from city to city. A typical set of sections looks like this:

  • General information: address, building use, year of construction or commissioning, owner, cadastral data.
  • Site plan and photographs of the existing condition (for reconstruction).
  • Elevations or orthogonal drawings of all facades tied to grid lines and levels.
  • Schedule of finishing materials: cladding type by zone (plinth, main wall surface, cornices, opening surrounds), texture, fixing method.
  • Colour scheme with colour codes from a catalogue (RAL, NCS) and samples.
  • Element layout: windows and curtain walls (glazing pattern, frame colour), entrance groups, balconies and loggias, railings, rainwater system.
  • Locations for air conditioners, antennas, cameras, signs, information plates and lighting.
  • Architectural lighting — if provided for in the design.

For facades with glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) or natural stone decoration, it is useful to list each element type as a separate line in the materials schedule — cornice, pilaster, window surround, rustication. This avoids questions during approval about what the projecting details are made of, and during later repairs it is clear which shape and texture must be reproduced.

When a Facade Passport Is Mandatory

Whether a passport is mandatory is determined not directly by federal law but by the improvement rules of the specific municipality and the procedure for approving the architectural and urban-planning appearance (AGO). In practice the document is required in the following situations:

  • new construction in cities where the building's appearance is approved separately — with a certificate or decision approving the AGO;
  • major repair or reconstruction of a facade involving replacement of cladding or changes to colour or divisions;
  • glazing of balconies and loggias, or window replacement with a changed glazing pattern or frame colour on an apartment building;
  • installation of signs, advertising or outdoor air-conditioning units on facades in a historic centre or on main streets;
  • buildings in protection zones of cultural heritage sites — requirements there are usually stricter, and approval goes through the heritage protection authority.

Even if your city's improvement rules do not require a passport, the document is still useful: it fixes the agreed appearance within the developer's team and serves as a reference during acceptance of works and claims against the contractor. Check the exact list of cases and the passport format with the local architecture department before design starts.

Who Prepares the Facade Passport

The passport is prepared by an architect or design organisation commissioned by the building owner. For a new building this is usually the author of the architectural design, who knows the logic of the facade best. For an existing building, the passport can be prepared by any design organisation with experience of working with the architecture authority of the specific city.

The facade contractor plays a supporting but important role. The cladding manufacturer can confirm in advance that the chosen materials, textures and colours are actually produced, that element sizes are feasible to manufacture, and that the fixings allow for the projections and shadows built into the design. If a passport is approved for a material that later proves impossible to make in the required size or colour, any deviation will have to be approved again.

Facade Colour Passport

A facade colour passport is part of the general passport (or a separate document) describing the building's colour scheme. For each facade zone it specifies the colour with a catalogue code, the material and the type of finish. A colour passport ensures that when individual elements are repaired or replaced years later, the facade is repainted in the same colour rather than 'by eye'.

Facade zoneWhat is specifiedNote
Main wall surfaceMaterial, colour, textureFor GFRC, colour is set either by pigment in the mix or by painting — this must be stated explicitly
Decoration: cornices, bands, surroundsColour and finishing methodOften contrasts with the main surface
PlinthMaterial and colourFor natural stone, colour is defined by the rock type and quarry, not by a catalogue code
Windows, curtain walls, doorsFrame colour, glassBoth sides, if they differ
Roof, gutters, railingsMetal colourFrom the same catalogue as the frames

For natural stone, instead of a colour code the passport states the rock type, name and quarry, the surface finish, and includes a reference sample. Batches of natural stone vary in shade, so the reference sample matters more than any number.

Changes to the Facade

The passport fixes the appearance, so any change visible from the street formally requires it to be amended. Typical situations:

  • replacing the cladding material — for example, render with a ventilated rainscreen facade with panels;
  • changing the colour or texture of the cladding, including during partial repair;
  • changing divisions, simplifying or adding decoration;
  • new openings, glazing, entrance groups, extensions;
  • new signs and external equipment outside the approved locations.

The procedure for amendments usually repeats the initial approval: revised sheets are prepared, the architecture authority approves them, and only then is the work carried out. So during construction it is better to prevent deviations than to approve them retrospectively. Any contractor proposal to substitute 'an equivalent' material should be discussed before purchase: a visually similar material may differ in texture, module size and colour so much that the appearance no longer matches the document.

FAQ

What is a facade passport?

A document that records a building's external appearance: materials, colours, windows, balconies, locations for signs and equipment. It is used to approve the appearance and control changes.

How does a facade colour passport differ from a facade passport?

A colour passport describes only the colour scheme, with colour codes by zone. A facade passport is broader: it includes drawings, materials, elements and equipment locations.

Who prepares a facade passport?

An architect or design organisation commissioned by the owner. The cladding manufacturer confirms that the chosen materials and colours can be produced.

When is a facade passport mandatory?

When local improvement rules require it: new construction with appearance approval, reconstruction, cladding replacement, installation of signs and equipment. Check with your city's architecture authority.

Does the passport need to be amended when cladding is replaced?

Yes, if the change is visible from the street: a new material, colour, texture or divisions. Revised sheets are approved before work starts.
