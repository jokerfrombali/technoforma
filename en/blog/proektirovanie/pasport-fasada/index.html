Facade Passport: What It Is and When You Need One

A facade passport is a document used in Russian cities that records the architectural appearance of a building: its colour scheme, cladding materials, the position and design of windows, doors, balconies, signs, external equipment and decorative elements. It is used to approve the building's appearance with the local architecture authority and then to control any changes.

The key point: the passport is not working documentation for installation but a reference for appearance. It answers the question of how the facade should look, not how to build it. That is why it makes sense to develop it in parallel with the concept, while materials and divisions can still be changed without redoing the shop drawings.

The document is needed by developers, property management companies and building owners in cities whose local improvement rules include requirements for external appearance. The general process of working on the concept and choosing cladding is described in Facade design and selection.

What a Facade Passport Contains

The content of the passport is set by local improvement rules and the regulations of the architecture authority, so it varies from city to city. A typical set of sections:

  • General information: address, use of the building, year of construction or commissioning, owner, cadastral data.
  • Site plan and photographs of the existing condition (for reconstruction).
  • Elevations or orthogonal drawings of all facades referenced to grid lines and levels.
  • Schedule of finishing materials: cladding type by zone (plinth, main surface, cornices, opening surrounds), texture, fixing method.
  • Colour scheme with colour codes from a catalogue (RAL, NCS) and samples.
  • Elements layout: windows and curtain walls (glazing pattern, frame colour), entrances, balconies and loggias, railings, rainwater system.
  • Locations for air conditioners, antennas, cameras, signs, information plates and lighting.
  • Architectural lighting, if included in the design.

For facades with decor in glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) or natural stone, it is useful to list each element type (cornice, pilaster, window surround, rustication) as a separate line in the materials schedule. Then there are no questions during approval about what the projecting details are made of, and for later repairs it is clear which shape and texture must be reproduced.

A facade passport, a facade alteration project and an architectural and urban planning solution are different documents, although they are often confused:

DocumentPurposeWhen needed
Facade passport (colour passport)Records the existing or approved appearance: colours, materials, elementsRepair, repainting, installing equipment and signs on an existing building
Facade alteration projectJustifies a change in appearance: new openings, entrances, claddingReconstruction, alterations that change the facade, cladding replacement
AGR (architectural and urban planning solution) / appearance approval decisionApproves the appearance of a new capital construction projectNew construction and reconstruction in cities with such a procedure (in Moscow, for example, approved by the Moscow Committee for Architecture and Urban Planning)

Which document is needed in a particular case is decided by the local architecture authority; it is worth finding out the route before work starts so as not to produce an unnecessary set.

When a Facade Passport Is Mandatory

Whether a passport is mandatory is determined not directly by federal law but by the improvement rules of the particular municipality and the procedure for approving architectural appearance. In practice, the document is required in the following situations:

  • new construction in cities where the building's appearance is approved separately, with a certificate or appearance approval decision issued;
  • major repair or reconstruction of a facade with replacement of cladding or a change of colour or divisions;
  • glazing balconies and loggias, or replacing windows with a change in glazing pattern or frame colour on an apartment building;
  • installing signs, advertising or external air conditioning units on facades in a historic centre or on main streets;
  • buildings in heritage protection zones, where requirements are usually stricter and approval goes through the heritage protection authority.

Even if your city's improvement rules contain no passport requirement, the document is still useful: it fixes the agreed appearance within the developer's team and provides a basis for accepting work and making claims against the contractor. Check the exact list of cases and the passport form with the local architecture department before design begins.

When a facade passport is usually not needed: interior work that does not change the exterior; replacing windows without changing the glazing pattern, colour or frame material (if local rules allow); routine repair restoring cladding in the same colour and material; detached private houses outside areas with special appearance requirements. Even in these cases, the rules differ between cities, so check the wording in the current edition of the improvement rules.

The cost of a mistake when working on a facade without a passport or deviating from it: an order to restore the facade to its approved appearance, administrative liability under local improvement rules, dismantling of unapproved elements at the owner's expense, and delayed commissioning if the appearance does not match the approved decision. Redoing the cladding costs many times more than approving changes before procurement.

Who Prepares the Facade Passport

The passport is prepared by an architect or design firm commissioned by the building's owner. For a new building this is usually the author of the architectural design, who knows the logic of the facade best. For an existing building, any design firm experienced with the architecture authority of the particular city can prepare it.

The facade contractor's role here is supporting but important. The cladding manufacturer can confirm in advance that the chosen materials, textures and colours are actually produced, that element sizes are manufacturable and that the fixings allow the projections and shadows built into the design. If the passport is approved for a material that later turns out impossible to make in the required size or colour, any deviation has to be approved again.

TECHNOFORMA Group has a design department of 12 designers led by 2 architects, plus 3D visualisers. They prepare the concept and visualisations of the appearance, select materials (GFRC, natural stone, porcelain stoneware) and produce working documentation for the substructure and cladding that underpins the approved appearance.

The general procedure for obtaining a passport:

  1. Request the requirements from the architecture authority: passport form, sections, colour catalogue requirements.
  2. Collect input data: title documents, technical plan or floor plans, measurements, photographs.
  3. Develop facade drawings, the materials schedule and colour scheme; if needed, visualisations in context.
  4. Agree with the owners: in an apartment building, a decision of the general meeting of owners if local rules require it.
  5. Submit to the architecture authority, receive comments, make corrections.
  6. Receive the approved passport and pass copies to the facility manager and contractors.

The time for preparation and approval depends on the city, the complexity of the building and the number of comment rounds; it should be built into the programme in advance, especially if the start of facade work depends on approval.

Facade Colour Passport

A facade colour passport is part of the general passport (or a separate document) setting out the building's colour scheme. For each facade zone it gives the colour with a catalogue code, the material and the type of finish. The colour passport ensures that when individual elements are repaired or replaced years later, the facade is repainted in the same colour rather than matched by eye.

Facade zoneWhat is specifiedNote
Main wall surfaceMaterial, colour, textureFor GFRC, colour is set by pigment in the mix or by painting; this must be stated explicitly
Decor: cornices, string courses, surroundsColour and finishing methodOften contrasts with the main surface
PlinthMaterial and colourFor natural stone, colour is determined by the stone and quarry, not a catalogue code
Windows, curtain walls, doorsFrame colour, glassBoth sides, if they differ
Roof, rainwater goods, railingsMetal colourFrom the same catalogue as the frames

For natural stone, instead of a colour code, the stone type, name and quarry and the surface finish are given, and a reference sample is attached. Batches of natural stone vary in shade, so the reference sample matters more than any number.

What to check in the colour passport before handing it to the contractor:

  • every facade zone has a material, colour and texture assigned, with no zones left to be decided on site;
  • colour codes come from one catalogue, without mixing systems;
  • for through-coloured elements, the pigment or a reference sample is given, not just a paint code;
  • for stone, the type, quarry, surface finish and reference sample are given;
  • metal elements are described separately: sills, railings, rainwater goods, window frames;
  • sealant and joint colour is specified if the joints are visible.

Colour on a sample and on a facade is perceived differently: over a large area it looks lighter and more saturated, and lighting and orientation change the shade. So it is better to approve the colour on a test patch or trial element on the building itself, not only from fan decks.

The colour passport is kept by the facility manager together with reference samples of the cladding and decor. When the management company changes, the documents and samples are handed over under a formal record; otherwise, after a few years it will be hard to restore the original appearance, especially on facades of natural stone and through-coloured GFRC.

Changes to the Facade

The passport records appearance, so any change visible from the street formally requires it to be amended. Typical situations:

  • changing the cladding material, for example from render to a ventilated rainscreen facade with slabs;
  • changing the colour or texture of the cladding, including during partial repair;
  • changing divisions, simplifying or adding decor;
  • new openings, glazing, entrances, extensions;
  • new signs and external equipment outside the approved locations.

Making changes usually follows the same procedure as the original approval: amended sheets are prepared, the architecture authority approves them, and only then is the work carried out. So during construction it pays to prevent deviations rather than approve them retrospectively. A contractor's proposal to substitute an 'equivalent' material should be discussed before procurement: a visually similar material may differ in texture, module size and colour enough that the appearance no longer matches the document.

Typical reasons for refusals and comments during approval:

  • an incomplete set of input documents or outdated measurements;
  • discrepancies between the drawings and photographs of the existing condition;
  • a colour scheme that does not meet the requirements for surrounding buildings or the protection zone;
  • air conditioners and signs outside permitted zones;
  • no owners' consent where it is required;
  • materials not permitted for this type of building under fire requirements.

For the facade contractor, the passport is an input document. Before production of elements starts, it is checked that the shape, colour and texture of each element match the approved appearance, and during installation photographs are taken for comparison with the document at acceptance.

The facade passport: what it means for the developer, the managing company and the tenant

Different parties use the same document in different ways: for the developer it is part of the approval of the building's appearance, for the managing company it is the benchmark against which the condition of the building is checked, and for the tenant it sets the rules for placing signage and equipment. Understanding your own role helps to determine in advance which sections of the passport matter to you and which questions to put to its author.

PartyWhy the passport is neededWhat to look out for
Developer, employer's agentTo confirm the approved appearance and pass it to contractors as a baseline requirementConsistency of the passport with the design documentation and the materials actually purchased
Architect, author of the designTo record the design intent and protect it from arbitrary changesCompleteness of the zone descriptions, unambiguous colour codes and material names
Facade contractorTo obtain the list of materials, colours and textures to be reproducedWhether the specified colour and texture are achievable in the chosen material; availability of reference samples
Managing company, ownerTo carry out repairs and maintenance without altering the appearanceWhether the document is up to date; the list of permitted locations for equipment
Tenant, owner of ground-floor premisesTo understand where and what kind of sign, awning or entrance may be installedDimensions and type of structures, method of fixing to the cladding

In practice, most questions arise where roles meet. A tenant changes the entrance glazing, the managing company repaints the plinth in the nearest available shade, a repair contractor replaces several slabs with material from another batch. Each such action looks minor on its own, but taken together they mean that after a few years the facade no longer corresponds to the document. It is therefore useful to hand the passport over not only to the archive but also, in working form, to everyone who takes decisions about the building.

A convenient way to do this is to prepare a short guidance note for owners and tenants on the basis of the passport: one or two pages with a diagram of the facade, the permitted zones for signage and equipment, the list of works that require approval, and the contact details of the responsible person. Such a document is read more often than the full set of drawings, and it answers most of the routine questions.

What input data to gather for preparing the passport

The author of the passport needs the documents for the building and the plot, information on the materials, and up-to-date drawings or measured surveys of the elevations. An incomplete package is the most common cause of delays: the architecture authority returns the documents for revision, and the approval cycle starts again.

In general, it is worth preparing:

  • title documents for the building and the land plot, and details of the rights holders;
  • the technical passport or floor plans, where an existing building is concerned;
  • architectural drawings of the elevations on all sides, including courtyard and end elevations, or measured survey drawings;
  • a photographic record of the existing condition and the surrounding development — to assess how the building fits its context;
  • a location plan referenced to streets and neighbouring properties;
  • information on the status of the building: whether it is a cultural heritage asset or lies within a protection zone;
  • a list of cladding materials with names, colours and samples;
  • data on planned signage, lighting, external air-conditioning units, entrances and ramps.

For new buildings, most of this information is contained in the design documentation, and the passport is compiled on that basis. For existing buildings, drawings are often unavailable, and the work begins with a measured survey: modern methods — laser scanning and photogrammetry — make it possible to obtain accurate elevations even for buildings with complex decoration.

It is worth clarifying separately who acts as the applicant. If the building has several owners, their consent or a resolution of a general meeting is, as a rule, required to submit the documents, and collecting signatures often takes longer than drafting the passport itself. This matter is best settled before a contract is signed with the designer.

The requirements for the form and content of the document should be requested from the local architecture authority before work begins. The scale of the drawings, the colour notation system, the list of mandatory sheets and the submission procedure differ from city to city. The capital has its own rules — they are described in detail in the article on approval of the appearance in Moscow.

How materials are described in the passport: GFRC, stone, porcelain tile

A material must be described in the passport in such a way that, several years later, it can be reproduced unambiguously or a replacement can be selected. For painted surfaces a colour code is sufficient, but for materials with their own texture and natural pattern a code alone is not enough.

MaterialWhat is specifiedHow it is confirmed
GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete)Colour (integral or surface-applied), type of texture, presence of water-repellent treatmentA reference sample approved by the architect, marked and dated
Natural stoneStone type, quarry or trade name, surface finish, permissible range of toneA set of samples showing the limits of natural variation in colour and pattern
Porcelain tileManufacturer, collection, product code, format, surface typeA sample tile and the manufacturer's documents
Render and paintCode in the adopted colour system, type of coating, gloss levelA painted sample on a substrate of the same type
Metal (cassettes, railings, sills)Colour code, type of coating, textureA coated sample

For GFRC products it should be borne in mind that the shade depends on the mix composition, the pigment and the method of surface treatment. Smooth, textured and sandblasted surfaces of the same colour are perceived differently on a facade, which is why the texture is recorded in the passport on an equal footing with the colour. More on this in the article on the colour of GFRC.

With natural stone, variation in tone is unavoidable even within a single block. In the document it is correct to specify not one sample but an agreed range, and to state separately that the slabs are sorted and laid out before installation. The principles of this work are described in the article on stone layout.

It is useful to produce the reference samples in two or three copies: one stays with the architect, the second with the client, the third at the factory. It is against these that batches are checked at incoming inspection.

Finally, it is useful to record in the material description not only the appearance but also the main performance properties on which the durability of the appearance depends: combustibility class, frost resistance, water absorption. When a replacement is being selected years later, it is these parameters that distinguish an equivalent material from one that merely looks similar.

How the passport relates to the working documentation for the facade

The passport answers the question of how the building should look; the working documentation answers how to build it. There must be no discrepancies between them: any difference in material, colour or articulation of the facade is either corrected in the working drawings or entered in the passport through the approval procedure.

The sequence is usually as follows. First the architect develops the concept and the cladding layout; the passport is then prepared on that basis and approval of the appearance is obtained. After that the working documentation is issued: subframe detailing, unit drawings, details. If the order is reversed and the appearance is approved before the technical feasibility of the solution has been checked, a risk arises: the working design may reveal that the specified slab format or cornice projection requires changes, and the approval will have to be repeated.

To avoid this, at the passport preparation stage it is worth obtaining confirmation from the cladding manufacturer and the subframe designer on several points:

  • the chosen panel and slab formats can be manufactured and transported without non-standard solutions;
  • the joint width shown on the elevations matches what is technologically necessary;
  • the colour and texture are achievable in the chosen material and are reproduced consistently from batch to batch;
  • decorative elements — cornices, string courses, surrounds — have real cross-sections rather than notional lines;
  • the fixing points for signage and equipment are provided with cast-in inserts or reinforced areas of the subframe.

For facades with large-format cladding this is particularly important: the articulation of GFRC facade panels is determined not only by composition but also by mould dimensions, the weight of the unit and the capacity of the lifting equipment. When design, production and installation are handled by one organisation, such questions are resolved before the documents are submitted.

Feedback in the other direction is also needed: if a solution had to be changed during working design or installation, the amendment is reflected in the passport before the building is handed over. It is convenient to time this reconciliation to coincide with facade acceptance, when the commission compares the completed work with the design in any case.

Signage, air conditioners, lighting: what to build into the passport in advance

External equipment is the main reason a facade loses its approved appearance over time. If the locations and types of such elements are defined in the passport in advance, owners and tenants receive clear rules, and the managing company has grounds to require compliance with them.

It is advisable for the document to provide for:

  • Signage. Placement zones, maximum dimensions, type (individual letters, light box, projecting sign), permissible fixing methods.
  • Air conditioners. Locations for external units — recesses, decorative baskets, service balconies, roof areas; pipe routes and condensate drainage.
  • Lighting. Position of luminaires, colour temperature, method of cable routing.
  • Entrances. Canopies, awnings, doors, shopfront glazing, ramps and railings.
  • Services elements. Ventilation grilles, rainwater pipes, aerials, CCTV cameras.
  • Address signs and memorial plaques.

For a ventilated facade the question of fixing is fundamental. A sign or an air-conditioner basket must not bear on a cladding slab: the load is transferred to the subframe or to the load-bearing wall through embedded elements provided for in the design. If there are none, every later fixing means drilling the cladding and the risk of compromising its integrity and the weatherproofing of the detail.

The same applies to lighting. Luminaires, cable routes and power supply units are most conveniently housed in decorative elements — behind cornices, in recesses, in string courses — but for this they must be taken into account before the units are manufactured. How this is done is described in the article on architectural lighting of the facade.

Condensate drainage from air conditioners deserves particular attention. Water dripping onto the cladding leaves streaks and efflorescence, which are especially noticeable on light-coloured stone and concrete, so the drainage is led into a common system rather than onto the plane of the facade.

A practical recommendation for commercial ground floors: build into the passport a single signage band with fixing points prepared in advance and a concealed power supply. Tenants change, but the facade remains intact.

How to use the facade passport during operation and repair

Once the building has been handed over, the passport becomes a working tool for the operating organisation: it is used to select materials for repairs, to check whether changes are permissible and to negotiate with tenants. A document that sits in the archive and is not updated does not perform this function.

In day-to-day work it is needed in several situations:

  1. Local repair. When a damaged slab or decorative element is replaced, the material, colour and texture are selected from the passport and the reference samples, not from an adjacent area of the facade, which may have changed tone over time.
  2. Cleaning and protective treatment. The washing method and water-repellent compounds are chosen with regard to the material stated in the document: what suits granite may damage limestone or a painted surface.
  3. Tenant requests. A request for a sign or an awning is checked against the permitted zones and dimensions.
  4. Scheduled inspections. The actual condition is compared with the recorded appearance, and unapproved changes are noted.
  5. Major repair and refurbishment. The passport serves as a source document for the designer.

Together with the passport it is useful to keep information that it does not usually contain: who manufactured the cladding, from which batch and quarry the stone came, which compound was used for protective treatment, and where the reference samples are held. A small stock of slabs and units from the same batch, set aside after installation, simplifies spot repairs for years to come.

If changes are nevertheless approved and carried out during operation, a new edition of the passport is issued with the date and a brief description of what has changed. In this way the document remains reliable both for the next managing organisation and for the designer who comes to the building ten years later.

The frequency of inspections and the scope of maintenance work depend on the type of system and the material. The general procedure is set out in the article on maintenance of a ventilated facade.

What mistakes are made when preparing the passport and how to avoid them

Most problems with a passport are built in not at the approval stage but earlier — when the document is drawn up as a formality, without checking the materials and without involving those who will build and operate the building. The situations listed below are the ones encountered most often.

  • The colour is specified from a screen or a printout. Monitors and printers distort the shade. The colour is approved from a physical sample viewed outdoors in daylight.
  • The texture is not specified. A colour code without a surface type allows too broad an interpretation, and the result may differ from what was expected.
  • Only the main elevations are described. Courtyard and end elevations, the plinth, lightwells and rooftop structures are left without solutions and are completed as an afterthought.
  • Equipment is not taken into account. Air-conditioning units and signs appear haphazardly within the first year.
  • The material is chosen without checking availability. The stone type or tile collection named in the document turns out to be unavailable in the required volume by the time of purchase, and the replacement has to be approved again.
  • The passport is not updated after changes. A material was replaced during construction, but the document remained as it was; a discrepancy arises at the very first inspection.
  • Nobody is made responsible for safekeeping. A few years later the copy cannot be found either with the developer or with the managing company.

A simple procedure helps to avoid this: before the document is submitted, hold a joint review with the architect, the cladding manufacturer and the future operating organisation; approve samples of all materials; and, once the work is complete, check the passport against the as-built condition. The document should be kept in both paper and electronic form, with the date of the latest edition stated.

One further measure is to build a full-size fragment of the facade before main production starts. Such a mock-up shows how the materials, joints and textures combine at true scale, and the solution can be refined before it finds its way into the approved document and the order.

FAQ

What is a facade passport?

A document that records a building's external appearance: materials, colours, windows, balconies and locations for signs and equipment. It is used to approve the appearance and control changes.

How does a facade colour passport differ from a facade passport?

A colour passport describes only the colour scheme, with colour codes by zone. A facade passport is broader: it includes drawings, materials, elements and equipment locations.

Who prepares a facade passport?

An architect or design firm commissioned by the owner. The cladding manufacturer confirms that the chosen materials and colours can be produced.

When is a facade passport mandatory?

When local improvement rules require it: for new construction with appearance approval, reconstruction, cladding replacement, and installing signs and equipment. Check with your city's architecture authority.

Does the passport need changing when cladding is replaced?

Yes, if the change is visible from the street: a new material, colour, texture or divisions. Amended sheets are approved before work starts.

How does a facade passport differ from an AGR?

The passport records the appearance of an existing building: colours, materials, elements and equipment locations. An AGR (or architectural appearance approval decision) approves the appearance of a building in new construction and reconstruction. The local architecture authority decides which document is needed.

Is a facade passport needed for a private house?

For a detached house on its own plot such a document is not, as a rule, required. Exceptions are possible if the house is located in a historic zone, within an area subject to special development requirements, or if the local improvement rules of a particular municipality apply to all buildings facing streets. A definitive answer is given by the local architecture authority.

What should be done if the facade passport for an existing building has been lost?

First, it is worth requesting a copy from the architecture authority where the document was approved and from the previous managing organisation. If no copy has survived, the passport is drawn up afresh: a measured survey and a photographic record are made, the actual materials and colours are described, and approval is obtained under the current procedure.

Can several material options be included in the passport?

Usually one specific solution is approved; however, a permissible range can be described: for example, a stone type with agreed limits of tone, or a colour with a stated permissible deviation. Alternative materials in case the main one is unavailable are sensibly discussed with the architect and the approving authority in advance, so that a substitution does not require a full repeat cycle.
