Facade Shop Drawings (KMD): What They Are and Why They Matter

Facade KMD (Russian abbreviation for detailed metal structure drawings) are the shop drawings from which the metal substructure of a ventilated rainscreen facade is manufactured and assembled. They detail every bracket, rail, clip and anchor, with dimensions, spacing, grade, quantity and installation position.

The main purpose of shop drawings is to turn the architectural solution and calculations into specific components that can be ordered, manufactured and installed without guesswork on the scaffold. Without them it is impossible to calculate the metal and fixings correctly, check that the substructure suits the loads and make sure every cladding panel lands on its support.

Shop drawings are needed by general contractors and installation companies, technical supervisors, procurement and estimators, and by the client as the basis for acceptance. At TECHNOFORMA they are developed by the company's own design department together with cladding production and the installation teams. More on design is in the section Facade design and selection.

What KMD is

Russian construction documentation distinguishes two stages, KM and KMD:

  • KM (metal structures): the design stage: schematic layouts, calculations, member sections, loads, material requirements.
  • KMD (detailed metal structures): the working stage: detailing of every element with dimensions for fabrication and installation diagrams.

For a rainscreen facade, the KMD shop drawings are the main working document for the substructure. They answer these questions:

  • where each bracket sits on the wall and which anchor fixes it;
  • what length the rails are and at what spacing;
  • where the clips or concealed cladding fixings go;
  • how corners, reveals, parapets, plinth and movement joints are resolved;
  • how projecting elements (cornices, pilasters, canopies) are fixed;
  • how many of each element are needed on each facade.

The working facade drawings used for installation are the shop drawings plus the cladding layout and details.

Who prepares them

Shop drawings are developed by a designer who has the substructure calculation and understands its manufacture and installation. In practice this is one of three parties:

WhoAdvantagesRisks
The building's lead designerCoordination with the structure and other sectionsDoes not always know the specifics of a particular cladding and substructure
The substructure supplierKnows its system and its catalogue of technical solutionsMay not account for the specifics of another manufacturer's cladding
A full-cycle facade companyShop drawings, cladding, production and installation in one pair of handsExperience on projects of a similar scale needs to be checked

For cladding with complex geometry (GFRC cornices, columns, window surrounds, large panels) it is more convenient for shop drawings to be developed by those who design and manufacture the products themselves: the cast-in fixings in the product and the brackets on the wall are designed together. At TECHNOFORMA the design department of 12 designers led by two architects works with the company's own GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) production and installation teams.

Contents

A typical facade shop drawing package includes:

  1. General data: drawing register, fabrication and installation instructions, material grades, corrosion protection.
  2. Bracket layout plans for each facade, referenced to axes and levels.
  3. Rail layouts: vertical and horizontal rails, their lengths and joints.
  4. Cladding layout referenced to the rails: the position of every panel and its fixings.
  5. Details: corners, window reveals and sills, parapets, plinth, junctions, movement joints, fire barriers.
  6. Detailing of non-standard elements: brackets for cornices, canopies, cantilevered parts.
  7. Specifications: per facade and summary: brackets, rails, fixings, anchors, insulation.

Detailing at the level of junctions is the most valuable part: this is where leaks, thermal bridges and installation problems arise.

Link to production

Shop drawings link the design to production in two directions.

Substructure. Brackets, rails and fixings are ordered or fabricated from the specifications. The accuracy of the specification determines whether there is enough metal for the project without top-up orders and without surplus.

Cladding. Panel sizes, the position of clip kerfs, cast-in fixings in GFRC products and holes in stone are set so as to match the substructure. If the shop drawings and product drawings are made by different teams, there is a risk that a cast-in fixing in a panel ends up where there is no rail on the wall.

In TECHNOFORMA's full cycle the path is: design department → the company's own mould shops, where models and moulds are made → production with quality control at every stage → logistics → installation of the substructure, insulation and panels by the company's own installation teams. The shop drawings are the common language for all these links.

Another link is with the schedule. Based on the shop drawings, installation is divided into sections, and production plans the output of products and substructure in the order they will be fitted. When drawings are issued in phases (first for the initial facades, then for the rest), manufacturing and installation start before the documentation for the whole building is complete. But this is only possible with a layout and standard details approved in advance; otherwise changes in later phases will affect elements already manufactured.

Mistakes

Mistakes in shop drawings show up during installation and are costly:

  • No on-site anchor pull-out tests. Bracket spacing is calculated from the rated capacity, but the wall actually holds less.
  • Shop drawings based on design rather than actual dimensions. Walls that are out of plumb make it impossible to set the substructure at the required offset.
  • Details not worked out. Reveals, sills and parapets are resolved on site, causing leaks and thermal bridges.
  • Cladding layout not coordinated with the rails. A panel joint misses its support.
  • No thermal expansion joints. Long continuous rails deform when heated.
  • Fire requirements ignored: fire barriers above openings, requirements for substructure materials for the given building height.
  • Specifications by eye. A shortage or surplus of fixings stops installation.

What input data are needed to prepare facade shop drawings

Architectural drawings alone are not enough to detail a subframe: the actual dimensions of the building and a confirmed load-bearing capacity of the substrate are required. The more complete the package at the outset, the fewer sheets have to be reissued once the installers arrive on site.

The designer normally requests:

  • the architectural section with elevations, sections and the approved cladding layout;
  • structural drawings of the building — wall material and thickness, position of floor slabs, columns and movement joints;
  • an as-built geodetic survey of the elevations showing the actual deviations of the walls from plumb and plane;
  • reports of on-site anchor pull-out tests for each type of substrate;
  • window data: position in the opening, frame dimensions, projection of the sills;
  • system information: technical certificate, album of technical solutions, range of profiles;
  • the construction region, building height and terrain type — for determining wind loads.

The weight and format of the cladding are recorded separately. A porcelain tile, a GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) panel and a stone slab 30–40 mm thick impose different loads on a bracket, so the calculation and fixing spacing differ for each.

How shop drawings are developed: the sequence of work

A set of shop drawings is issued in sequence: first the calculation and the principal solutions, then the layout drawings, and only after that the detailing. Attempting to draw details straight away without an approved layout almost always ends in rework.

  1. Review of input data. The architectural drawings are checked against the survey, and areas where the actual wall departs from the design plane are identified.
  2. Structural calculation. Loads on brackets and anchors in the field and corner zones are determined, and rail sections are selected. A separate article explains how wind loads are calculated.
  3. Choice of subframe type. Vertical, horizontal or cross-rail arrangement, profile material, bracket projection to suit the insulation thickness.
  4. Layout drawings. Brackets and rails are referenced to the building grid and the floor slab levels.
  5. Details. Corners, reveals, parapet, plinth, and junctions with glazing and the roof are worked out.
  6. Schedules. Profiles, fixings, insulation and membrane are quantified and broken down by work section.
  7. Checking and issue. The set undergoes an internal check, is agreed with the client and is marked as released for construction.

On projects with several blocks, the documentation is usually issued in stages — by block or by elevation — so that installation can begin before the whole set is complete.

How do shop drawings for GFRC and stone differ from those for flat cladding?

For flat cladding, detailing comes down to a repeating grid of rails and a handful of standard details. For three-dimensional decorative elements and heavy stone, every element receives its own fixing arrangement, and the volume of drawings increases several times over.

ParameterFlat cladding (porcelain tile, cassettes)GFRC and natural stone
Load on the subframeUniform, predictableConcentrated, with eccentricity on projecting elements
FixingClips, rivets, standard carriersCast-in inserts in the unit, concealed anchors, bespoke brackets
Number of detail typesUsually up to a few dozenDepends on the number of unit types; may run into hundreds
Link to productionThrough the cutting scheduleThrough unit drawings showing the position of inserts and lifting loops
Installation tolerancesTaken up by bracket adjustmentRequire adjustment in three directions and allowance in the joints

For GFRC cornices with a large projection, the designer separately checks the overturning moment and specifies the installation sequence: which element is set first and where temporary supports are needed. These instructions also form part of the set.

Three-dimensional elements are more conveniently detailed in a 3D model: clashes between the frame and the inserts are visible before the drawings are issued. The benefits of 3D facade modelling are covered separately.

How to check shop drawings before releasing them to production

The set is best checked before the metal is ordered: correcting a sheet is cheaper than replacing a batch of brackets with the wrong projection. The client and the technical supervisor need only work through a short list.

  • The loads in the calculation correspond to the actual weight of the cladding and the wind region.
  • The bracket spacing in corner zones is smaller than in field zones — a direct consequence of the calculation.
  • The design load on an anchor does not exceed the value in the test report, allowing for the safety factor.
  • The bracket projection allows for the insulation thickness, the air gap and the actual deviations of the wall.
  • Fire barriers and the solutions around openings are shown.
  • Allowance is made for thermal movement of the rails.
  • The materials of the components comply with the technical certificate for the system: an aluminium, galvanised or stainless steel subframe each has its own field of application.
  • The schedule matches the drawings in quantities and grades.

If even one item raises a question, it is resolved in writing — through a list of comments and the designer's response.

How facade shop drawings affect the estimate and procurement

An accurate estimate for the subframe appears only after detailing: before that, the consumption of metal and fixings is calculated from aggregated rates per square metre. The difference between the preliminary and the final figure can be noticeable, especially on facades with a large number of openings, corners and projecting elements.

The budget is most affected by several decisions taken precisely at the working drawing stage:

  • the spacing of brackets and rails — directly determines the number of anchors and the linear metres of profile;
  • the bracket projection — on an uneven wall one size is replaced by two or three, so as not to overpay for extended components across the whole area;
  • the cutting of rails — matching the stock length to the storey height reduces waste;
  • standardisation of details — the fewer bespoke parts, the simpler the fabrication and supply;
  • the subframe material — the choice between aluminium, galvanised and stainless steel depends on the cladding weight, the service environment and fire safety requirements.

Schedules broken down by work section make it possible to buy materials in batches to suit the installation programme, rather than tie up funds in the full volume of metal in storage. Procurement uses the same schedules to monitor stock and reorder items in good time.

What the installation team receives together with the working drawings

Installers do not need the whole set but a working extract for a specific work section: the bracket layout, the rail layout, the details and the schedule of components. Sheets on site should be readable without the designer's explanations — with references to grid lines and levels that can be set out on the building.

Besides the drawings, the package for construction usually includes:

  • instructions on the installation sequence and permissible deviations;
  • requirements for drilling and setting anchors for each type of substrate, including minimum edge distances;
  • tightening torques for threaded connections, where specified by the system manufacturer;
  • a marking scheme for the cladding units that matches the marking on the packaging;
  • a list of concealed works to be presented for inspection before being covered by the cladding.

Before work begins, it is useful to hold a kick-off meeting between the designer, the foreman and the technical supervisor: to go through non-standard details, the procedure when a drawing differs from the actual condition, and the channel through which site queries reach the author of the design. This takes an hour or two, and the number of installation stoppages in the first weeks falls noticeably.

How changes are made to shop drawings during installation

Changes to working drawings are made only by the author of the design — with a new revision of the sheet and an entry in the register of changes. Handwritten amendments on site without the designer's signature have no legal standing and are treated at handover as a departure from the design.

The reasons for amendment are typical: the wall turns out to be of a different material, the floor slab is offset from the design, the client has changed the slab format. In each case the designer assesses whether the design model changes. If it does, the loads are recalculated and new sheets are issued; if not, a technical solution is prepared for the specific detail.

Only the current revision should be on site. Superseded sheets are withdrawn, and all changes are eventually transferred to the as-built documentation. When the author of the shop drawings and the installation contractor work within one organisation, as in the TECHNOFORMA design department, the approval cycle is usually shorter.

FAQ

How do working facade drawings differ from KMD?

KMD are the part of the working drawings covering the metal substructure. The facade working documentation also includes the cladding layout, details, product drawings and specifications.

What is facade detailing?

It is working out every element and junction to a level sufficient for manufacture and installation: dimensions, fixings, references, assembly sequence.

Does a private house need shop drawings?

For a rainscreen facade, yes, although the documentation is smaller. Without them it is hard to confirm that heavy cladding is securely fixed and to order the substructure correctly.

Can shop drawings be made before the cladding is chosen?

No. Rail spacing, fixings and loads depend on the material, format and weight of the panels, so the cladding is chosen before the shop drawings are developed.

How many sheets are there in a set of facade shop drawings?

There is no single standard: the volume depends on the area, the number of detail types and the kind of cladding. For a building with flat cladding the set usually runs to a few dozen sheets; for a facade with extensive GFRC or stone decoration it is considerably larger, since each type of unit receives its own fixing arrangement.

Can the system manufacturer's album of technical solutions be used instead of shop drawings?

No. The album shows standard details and the permitted fields of application of the system, but does not relate them to a specific building. Shop drawings are developed on the basis of the album: with a calculation for the actual loads, references to the grid and a schedule for the project.

Who is responsible for errors in the shop drawings?

Responsibility lies with the organisation that prepared and signed the documentation, within the limits set by the contract. That is why it matters that changes are made by the author of the design rather than the installation team: otherwise the boundary of responsibility is difficult to establish.
