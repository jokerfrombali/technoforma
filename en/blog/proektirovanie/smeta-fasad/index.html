Facade Works Estimate: Structure and Review

A facade works estimate is a document that breaks the cost of a facade down into materials, labour, plant, overheads and the contractor's profit. It should be checked in two directions: completeness (are all items in the design included) and justification (do quantities match the design and rates match the market).

The main mistake when checking is looking at the total or the price per square metre. Two estimates with the same total may describe different facades: one includes substructure, insulation and junctions, the other only cladding.

This article is for developers, quantity surveyors, technical supervision and private clients who have received an estimate from a contractor. Other design-stage questions are covered in Facade design and selection.

Sections of the Estimate

The structure of an estimate for a ventilated rainscreen facade usually follows the installation sequence. Typical sections:

SectionWhat it includesWhat to look at
Design workSubstructure calculations, shop drawings, cladding layout, detailsIs it there at all; who issues it
Preliminary workScaffolding, lifts, hoarding, anchor testsWho provides the plant — client or contractor
SubstructureBrackets, rails, anchors, thermal break padsMaterial (aluminium, galvanised or stainless steel), spacing
InsulationInsulation, dowels, wind barrier membraneThickness and density per the thermal calculation
CladdingPanels, ornament, fixings, clips, cast-in platesMatches the element schedule
Junctions and trimsReveals, sills, parapets, corners, fire breaksNothing omitted
LogisticsDelivery, unloading, storageIs distance accounted for

If a section is missing, it does not mean the work is free: it will have to be paid for separately, possibly to another contractor at a higher price.

Estimates are prepared in two ways. Resource-index or base-index methods under Russian state estimating standards (GESN, FER and their regional equivalents) are mandatory for publicly funded projects and are often required by banks and large developers. A contractor's commercial estimate is based on its own unit rates and supplier price lists. Comparing totals of the two formats directly is incorrect: in a standards-based estimate indices, mark-ups and limited costs are shown separately, whereas in a commercial one they are usually built into the rates.

The basis of any check is a bill of quantities prepared from the design. If contractors price the same bill, their estimates can be compared line by line; if each measured quantities themselves, quantities are reconciled first and only then prices.

Materials

In the materials section three things are checked: description, quantity and price.

Description. An item "facade cladding" without material, thickness, texture and manufacturer cannot be checked. For glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC), state the element type and dimensions; for natural stone, the stone, thickness and finish; for porcelain stoneware, the format, thickness and fixing method. The substructure must be named with its brand and material: aluminium and steel systems differ in both price and load capacity.

Quantity. Cladding area is measured from facade elevations less openings but including reveals. A cutting allowance is added to slab materials; its size depends on format and layout complexity. For GFRC ornament the quantity is pieces and linear metres from the element schedule; the allowance here is minimal because elements are made to size.

Price. Compare with current price lists or quotations from several suppliers. For bespoke products — GFRC ornament, stone with non-standard working — there is no market price, so such items are checked by requesting an alternative quote from another manufacturer against the same schedule.

An estimate for a ventilated facade differs from one for a rendered facade in its materials structure. In a rainscreen facade the main share is cladding and substructure, and the number of brackets and anchors depends on the wind and cladding weight calculation: spacing is closer at corners and on upper floors. In a wet (render) facade the main items are insulation, adhesive and reinforcing compounds, mesh, primers and decorative render, and their consumption is calculated per square metre from the system manufacturer's method statements. Comparing per-square-metre prices of a rainscreen and a rendered facade without allowing for these differences is meaningless.

Labour

The labour section describes work content: substructure installation, insulation, cladding installation, junctions. What to check:

  • Units of measurement match the type of work: substructure and cladding in m², cornices and mouldings in linear metres, columns and complex ornament in pieces.
  • Quantities match the materials section: if materials show 8,000 m² of cladding and labour 7,000 m², one figure is wrong.
  • Separate rates for difficult areas: installing large ornament, working from a truck-mounted platform, curved surfaces usually cost more than a standard plane.
  • No duplicates: the same work must not be counted twice — for example, fixing insulation in both the insulation and substructure sections.

If one company handles both manufacture and installation, the cost of work is often priced as a package. In that case ask for a breakdown so it is clear how the price is recalculated if quantities change.

Overheads

Overheads and estimated profit are the contractor's costs of organising the work and its income. In estimates under state methods they are applied at standard percentages of the labour cost; in commercial quotations they are often included in unit rates.

Besides overheads proper, estimate "add-ons" include:

  • temporary buildings and facilities;
  • winter working surcharge if the programme falls in the cold season;
  • travel and accommodation for crews working in another region;
  • a contingency for unforeseen work;
  • taxes.

Check the base each percentage is applied to. A typical error is applying overheads to the cost of materials when the method uses labour as the base, or charging the winter surcharge twice. The contingency is worth discussing separately: it is often better for the client to pay for additional work as it arises than to fix a percentage in advance.

Typical ways of inflating cost found during checks:

TechniqueHow it looks in the estimateHow to check
Inflated quantitiescladding area larger than facade area on the elevationsremeasure from drawings, deduct openings

The opposite problem is an underpriced estimate that omits junctions, fire breaks, trims or logistics. It looks better at tender stage, but the difference comes back through variations.

How to Check an Estimate

A checking procedure that suits both technical supervision and a client without estimating training:

  1. Compare the list of sections with the design: are all facade elements included.
  2. Remeasure the main quantities from the drawings — cladding, substructure and insulation areas, cornice lengths.
  3. Reconcile material and labour quantities with each other.
  4. Check material descriptions and brands against the design.
  5. Compare key rates with alternative offers.
  6. Check mark-ups: base and percentage.
  7. List everything missing from the estimate and ask the contractor for a written answer.

Separately, find out what happens if actual quantities differ: a fixed-price estimate and one based on measured quantities require different control. A contractor who prices after inspecting the site and from working drawings gives a more accurate figure than one who estimates by facade area at a glance.

A short checklist of input data to give contractors to get comparable estimates:

  • facade working drawings, or at least elevations with materials and levels;
  • a bill of quantities broken down by cladding type;
  • an ornament schedule with dimensions and quantities;
  • substructure and insulation requirements — type, thickness, fire classification;
  • site conditions: access, storage areas, availability of scaffolding or lifts;
  • programme — the season affects surcharges;
  • who supplies materials — contractor or client.

Limits of any check: without working drawings an estimate remains indicative and comparing contractors approximate. Even a perfectly checked estimate does not replace control of actual quantities during the work.

At what project stage does a facade works estimate become accurate?

The accuracy of an estimate is determined not by the estimator's diligence but by the completeness of the input documents. A sketch gives only a rough order of cost, the design documentation gives a budget with a tolerance, and working drawings with shop drawings give an estimate suitable for a fixed-price contract.

StageWhat is availableWhat can be pricedWhat is still missing
Sketch, conceptVisualisations, elevations without details, facade areasRough cost by cladding type, comparison of material optionsSubframe spacing, quantity of decor, junctions
Design documentationElevations with materials, typical details, thermal calculationBudget by section with a reserve for refinementExact setting-out, element schedule, anchor test results
Working and shop drawingsCladding layout, subframe specifications, product schedule, detailsItemised estimate with quantities down to the piece and linear metreOnly the actual wall deviations revealed by a geodetic survey

At an early stage it is sensible to ask the contractor not for a single figure but for a range with a list of assumptions: which subframe is assumed, what the bracket spacing is, whether decor is included. Once the facade shop drawings appear, assumptions are replaced by quantities and the estimate is recalculated. A difference between the first assessment and the final estimate is normal as long as it is clear which refinements produced it.

The geodetic survey of the walls deserves separate mention. Deviations of the substrate from the vertical affect bracket length and the number of extensions, and on buildings under refurbishment they can change the subframe section noticeably. On existing buildings the survey and inspection are therefore carried out before the final calculation.

How quantities are measured: units and common discrepancies

Most disputes over an estimate arise not from prices but from the parties measuring quantities differently. To avoid this, the measurement rules are fixed in the bill of quantities or in an appendix to the contract.

ItemUnitHow it is usually measuredWhere mistakes occur
Cladding of the wall planem²From elevations less openings, reveals as a separate lineReveals are included in the plane and counted again
Subframem²By the area of the clad surface, corner and edge zones with closer spacingA single rate that ignores reinforced zones
Insulationm² or m³By wall area with thickness and number of layers statedA second layer or a different thickness at the plinth is omitted
Reveals, sills, parapet cappingslin. mBy the perimeter of openings and the parapet lengthItems are omitted altogether
Cornices, string courses, mouldingslin. mBy centre-line length including corners and breaksCorner pieces are not itemised although they cost more than standard ones
Columns, capitals, bracketspcsBy the element scheduleSizes are not separated
Fire barrierslin. mBy the perimeter of openings and floor by floor, as per the designNot included in the estimate

A useful check is to compile control ratios: cladding area per square metre of gross floor area, number of brackets per square metre of facade, length of reveals per window. Sharp differences from comparable projects do not necessarily mean an error, but they do call for an explanation.

It is also agreed separately how small openings and niches, areas behind downpipes, and the surrounds of ventilation grilles and air-conditioner baskets are measured. Each of these details is minor, but on a multi-storey building they are repeated hundreds of times and together add up to a noticeable volume of make-up pieces and labour. If the bill contains no such items, it is worth asking the contractor whether they are included in the rate for the main wall plane or will be charged separately.

How does an estimate for GFRC decor and natural stone differ from one for panel materials?

For bespoke products the estimate contains one-off costs that standard panels do not have: models, moulds, selection and cutting. These lines cannot be assessed 'per square metre' — they are checked against the element schedule.

  • Tooling for GFRC. The cost of models and moulds depends on the number of unique sizes, not on the area; it is shown as a separate line or spread over the production run — in either case the approach must be stated.
  • Cast-in parts and frames. Metal in the products and the matching brackets sometimes appear both in the product price and in the subframe section; the scope of supply should be clarified.
  • Samples and reference pieces. Making control samples of texture and colour is a small but separate item.
  • Stone cutting. Slabs are cut from larger slabs or blocks, and the yield depends on the format, the pattern and the tone-matching requirements; waste is higher with a complex layout.
  • Edge and face finishing. Chamfers, drip grooves, flaming or polishing are priced per linear and square metre on top of the slab price.
  • Imported supply. For stone from abroad the price includes freight, customs clearance and the exchange rate; the estimate states the date and rate used for the calculation.

The make-up of stone prices is examined in detail in the article on what the cost of stone depends on. The same rule applies to GFRC facade panels as to decor: pricing is based on the design and the schedule, not on a price list.

Which logistics and site organisation costs are often forgotten?

Delivery, unloading and storage rarely account for a large share of the estimate, yet they are what most often surfaces as additional work. They should be discussed before the contract is signed, and who pays for what should be recorded.

  • packaging: pallets, A-frames, crating for large decor, and whether it is returnable;
  • transport: number of trips, oversized loads, delivery to the region, restrictions on city access and unloading times;
  • unloading and lifting to the floor or work zone: crane, lorry loader, forklift, riggers;
  • on-site storage: shelter, level base, security, re-handling of material when the work front moves;
  • access equipment: hire of scaffolding, cradles or mast climbers for the whole period, erection and dismantling, relocations;
  • electricity, water, welfare facilities, removal of waste and packaging;
  • protection of installed cladding until handover and final washing of the facade.

On remote sites, accommodation and travel for installers are added. TECHNOFORMA Group has warehouses from Novorossiysk and Makhachkala to Yekaterinburg and Novosibirsk, which shortens the delivery leg, but logistics are still calculated for the specific address. How the choice of access equipment affects site organisation is described in the article on scaffolding and cradles.

In the estimate it is convenient to gather these costs in a separate section, 'Site organisation and logistics', rather than dissolving them in the installation rates. Then, if the programme changes or the storage area is moved, it is clear exactly which line changes, and the cost per square metre of cladding does not have to be revised. If some resources are provided by the client or the main contractor — a crane, scaffolding, electricity — this is recorded in a responsibility matrix stating the dates and the persons responsible.

Fixed price or payment by actual quantities: what to set out in the contract

The estimate is an appendix to the contract, and how it works depends on the price terms. With a fixed price the contractor bears the risk of a quantity error; with payment by actual quantities the client does; a mixed scheme with fixed unit rates and adjustable quantities is the most common.

  • Unit rates are fixed for the whole contract term, or the conditions for revising them are described — for example, changes in metal prices or in the exchange rate for imported materials.
  • The procedure for changing quantities: who initiates it, which document records it and within what period it is agreed.
  • Additional work is paid for only after its estimate has been agreed in writing; rates are taken from the main estimate, and for new items from an agreed calculation.
  • The payment schedule is tied to stages: an advance for materials and tooling, payment for supply, payment for installation by work zone.
  • Completion is confirmed by certificates showing quantities for the same items and units as in the estimate; in Russian practice these are usually forms KS-2 and KS-3.
  • Retention and the conditions for its release are set out separately.

When the structure of the certificates mirrors that of the estimate, the client can see at any time how much has been done and how much remains under each item. If the estimate is itemised but certificates are issued as a lump sum per stage, control over quantities is lost.

For bespoke products the contract separately describes what happens to the tooling: whether it stays with the manufacturer, how long it is stored and on what terms additional elements can be ordered from it. The procedure for approving reference samples should also be recorded: the approval date usually serves as the starting point for the manufacturing period, and changes to texture or colour after it are priced as additional work. Such terms do not directly change the estimate total, but they determine whether it will remain the same by the end of construction.

How to compare estimates from several contractors

What should be compared is not the totals but like-for-like items brought to a common structure. To do this, the bidders' estimates are transferred to a single table in which the rows are the sections and items of the bill of quantities and the columns are the bids.

  1. Bring all bids to the same scope of work: add missing items marked 'not included' and request a price for them.
  2. Check the quantities in each row against the bill; eliminate discrepancies before comparing prices.
  3. Check that materials are equivalent: subframe brand, insulation density and thickness, cladding thickness and texture.
  4. Highlight the rows where prices differ most and ask for explanations — the cause is usually a different understanding of the scope.
  5. Compare the terms separately: programme, advance payment, warranty, who provides scaffolding and plant.
  6. Assess what remains outside each bid and add those sums to the total.

After such levelling, the lowest bid often ceases to be the lowest. The preparation of input data and the bidder evaluation criteria are described in the article on the tender for facade works, and guidance on cost structure is given in the article on the cost of a ventilated facade.

The estimate and cost of ownership: what to consider beyond construction

The estimate reflects the cost of creating the facade, but the decision on material is best taken with operating costs in mind. A solution that is cheaper at the outset may require frequent renewal of the coating or repair of fixings.

  • frequency of facade inspections and washing;
  • service life of the subframe and fixings compared with that of the cladding;
  • the possibility of replacing a single element without dismantling a whole area;
  • availability of spare products and slabs from the same batch;
  • warranty obligations of the manufacturer and the installer.

A small stock of slabs and decorative elements included in the estimate from the start costs less than making a few pieces years later: for stone, a block of the same tone may not be found, and for GFRC the mould will have to be put back into use. It is convenient to show these items as a separate line in the estimate so that they are not lost when the budget is optimised.

When comparing cladding options it is useful to bring the costs to a single time horizon — for example, the design service life of the building up to the first major facade overhaul. Such a calculation includes the initial estimate, scheduled inspections and washing, renewal of protective coatings, replacement of sealants and gaskets, and the likely repair of individual areas. Non-combustible mineral materials — natural stone, porcelain stoneware, GFRC — usually come out ahead thanks to infrequent maintenance, although their initial cost is higher than that of render systems. Exact figures depend on the climate, the building height and the quality of installation, so the comparison is made for the specific project.

FAQ

What should a facade works estimate include?

Design, preliminary work, substructure, insulation and wind barrier, cladding, junctions and trims, logistics, overheads and profit.

How do I check a facade estimate?

Compare sections with the design, remeasure quantities, reconcile materials and labour, check rates and the mark-up base, and list missing items.

Why do rainscreen facade estimates vary so much between contractors?

Usually because of different scopes of work and materials: substructure, insulation thickness, junctions, plant and delivery are treated differently.

How can I check the price of bespoke GFRC ornament?

Request an alternative quote from another manufacturer against the same element schedule.

When can a contractor give an accurate estimate?

After studying the design and inspecting the site; at TECHNOFORMA the cost of construction and installation work is determined after inspection.

How does an estimate under Russian state standards (FER) differ from a contractor's commercial quotation?

A standards-based estimate uses base rates with indices and separately shown mark-ups for overheads, profit and limited costs. In a commercial quotation the contractor uses its own rates, which usually already include these costs. Totals cannot be compared directly — first reconcile quantities against one bill.

Should design work be included in the estimate if a design already exists?

If only the design documentation exists, working drawings and shop drawings for the subframe and cladding still have to be produced, and this work should be a separate line in the estimate. If the working documentation is ready, the contractor usually allows for checking it and adapting it to its own subframe and products.

How is material allowance accounted for in the estimate?

For panel materials the allowance for cutting and breakage is set from the layout and depends on the format and the number of make-up pieces. For GFRC products, which are made to size, the allowance is minimal; it is sensible to include a few spare elements of each high-volume size in case of damage during installation and for future repairs.

Can the estimate be changed after the contract is signed?

Yes, by a supplementary agreement. The grounds are a design change, the discovery of hidden defects in the substrate, or a change of material at the client's decision. To keep changes from becoming a matter of dispute, the contract describes in advance the approval procedure and the rates at which new quantities are priced.
