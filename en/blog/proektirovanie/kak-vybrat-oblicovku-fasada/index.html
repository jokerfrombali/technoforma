How to Choose Facade Cladding: Criteria and Comparison

How to choose facade cladding: start not with the material but with the conditions: building type and height, load-bearing capacity of the walls, climate, fire safety requirements, local planning regulations, budget and service life. Only then compare materials: natural stone, glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC), porcelain stoneware, fibre cement and composite panels, clinker, render and metal cassettes.

For multi-storey buildings the main criterion is the system as a whole: cladding, substructure, insulation and fixings must be compatible and permitted for a building of that height and fire hazard class. An attractive slab on an unsuitable substructure is a risk, not a saving.

This article is for architects, developers, general contractors and private homeowners. TECHNOFORMA designs, manufactures and installs facades in GFRC, natural stone and porcelain stoneware, so the comparison below is a practitioner's view rather than that of a single-material seller. More on choosing a facade is in Facade design and selection.

Selection criteria

Cladding selection criteria can be divided into mandatory (without them the solution is not permissible) and desirable (they affect quality and cost).

Mandatory:

  • Fire safety. For multi-storey and public buildings, requirements for the combustibility of the cladding and the system as a whole are set by the applicable fire safety codes. Non-combustible materials (natural stone, porcelain stoneware, GFRC, which at TECHNOFORMA has Russian combustibility class NG according to the company) give more freedom.
  • Loads. The weight of the cladding and the wind load must be carried by the wall and substructure; for lightweight concrete and hollow brick this is often the limiting factor.
  • Climate. Frost resistance, water absorption, resistance to UV and temperature swings.
  • Regulations. In some cities the facade appearance must be approved, and materials and colours are fixed in a facade passport or an architectural design approval.

Desirable:

  • service life and ease of repair;
  • freedom of form: whether three-dimensional decor or curved elements are needed;
  • installation speed and the possibility of winter work;
  • maintenance in service: washing, impregnation, element replacement;
  • cost of a finished square metre including substructure and installation.

Choosing by climate is a separate filter. Guidelines for the main zones of Russia:

A step-by-step selection procedure: determine the building type, height and fire safety requirements; check the load-bearing capacity of the walls and the substrate for anchors; find out the local regulations on appearance; draw up a shortlist of two or three materials that pass the first three points; compare them by cost of a finished metre and maintenance; approve samples and visualisation; commission the substructure and layout design.

Materials

The main facade cladding materials and what they offer:

  • Natural stone: granite, marble, limestone, travertine, sandstone, dolomite, shell limestone (coquina). Unique texture and durability of dense stones; heavy, needs a strong substructure; cost varies greatly by stone.
  • GFRC: thin-walled elements with a cement matrix and glass fibre; at TECHNOFORMA it has 4 components: glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement and water. It allows cornices, columns, window surrounds, relief and large panels of any shape; lighter than stone; imitates stone and concrete.
  • Porcelain stoneware: uniform, stable, light; the main material of mass-market rainscreen facades. Limited to flat shapes.
  • Fibre cement panels: light, coloured or textured; installed on a substructure.
  • Composite (aluminium) panels: light, bent shapes, contemporary look; the fire performance of the specific product needs special attention.
  • Clinker tiles and brick: traditional look, low water absorption.
  • Render systems (ETICS): economical for low- and mid-rise housing; require careful workmanship and maintenance.
  • Metal cassettes and profiled metal: for industrial and retail buildings.

Rainscreen facades clad with composite panels, porcelain stoneware, stone or GFRC are built in the same way: brackets on the wall, insulation, a wind barrier where needed, rails and cladding with an air cavity. Loads, element spacing and fixings differ.

Ventilated or 'wet' facade is the first fork that determines the range of materials.

For a private house the choice is wider: in addition to the materials listed, there is facing brick with an air gap, facade thermal panels and timber cladding. For multi-storey housing and public buildings, it is in most cases a rainscreen system with non-combustible cladding.

What to check when accepting material on site: marking and batch number, match with the approved sample in daylight, no chips or cracks, slab geometry, data sheets and test reports on frost resistance and water absorption.

Budget

A facade budget is made up of material, substructure, insulation, installation, scaffolding or cradles and design. So materials should be compared by the cost of a finished square metre on a specific building, taking maintenance into account.

  • A cheap material on an expensive substructure can cost more than it seems. Conversely, light cladding saves on brackets and anchors.
  • Complex decor such as cornices, pilasters and window surrounds is expensive in natural stone; in GFRC it is cast in moulds, and the unit cost falls for repeated elements.
  • Combining materials is a common way to manage the budget: stone or GFRC on the plinth, entrances and decor, porcelain stoneware on plain surfaces.
  • Maintenance: render is periodically repaired and repainted, porous stone is impregnated, while porcelain stoneware and GFRC mainly need washing.

TECHNOFORMA determines the cost of work after inspecting the site; the design department can price several cladding options for one building.

Where not to economise: substructure and layout design, anchor testing, insulation and fixings, and junction details at windows and parapets. Mistakes here can only be corrected by dismantling the facade. It is reasonable to economise on the complexity of cladding on plain surfaces, the number of decor sizes and the standardisation of elements.

When expensive does not mean better: polished granite on a shaded facade in urban smog quickly loses its look; complex large-format slabs on a low building cannot be seen from the street; expensive decor without well-designed drainage darkens with streaks within a couple of seasons.

Mistakes

Typical mistakes when choosing facade cladding:

  1. Choosing from a picture. The material is approved from a catalogue photo without samples in daylight or a visualisation on the building.
  2. Separately from the substructure. The cladding is chosen without checking that a permitted substructure exists for the building's height and type.
  3. Ignoring the substrate. Heavy cladding is put on a wall that cannot hold anchors with the required load.
  4. Combustible materials at height. Panels without verified fire performance on multi-storey buildings.
  5. Saving on details. Reveals, sills, parapets and junctions are improvised on site, leading to leaks and efflorescence.
  6. Different batches. Cladding is bought in parts, and the shade differs between neighbouring work zones.
  7. No shop drawings. Installation without working drawings of the substructure and cladding layout.

Questions to ask the contractor before ordering:

  1. Which substructure is proposed, and is there a document confirming it is suitable for a building of this height?
  2. Is the fire hazard class of the assembled system with the chosen cladding confirmed?
  3. Who does the substructure calculation and anchor testing?
  4. Who produces the shop drawings and cladding layout?
  5. Is installation done in-house or subcontracted?
  6. Which cladding samples will be approved, and how is batch consistency controlled?
  7. How are details resolved: reveals, sills, parapets, plinth, movement joints?
  8. What is included in the estimate and what is paid separately (scaffolding, cradles, design, testing)?
  9. What as-built documentation is handed over on completion?
  10. How are maintenance and replacement of damaged elements organised?

When accepting materials, check: marking and batch match the order, data sheets and test reports, geometry and absence of chips, shade match with the approved sample, and completeness of fixings. For GFRC and stone elements, check the marking against the layout drawing so that each element goes in its place.

Another mistake is not accounting for lead times of made-to-order elements and the seasonality of installation when planning the programme.

How to choose facade cladding: a step-by-step procedure

It is more convenient to approach the choice of cladding not from the material but from the constraints: first establish what is permissible on the given building, and only then compare the remaining options by appearance and price. This order avoids the situation where a preferred material has to be replaced after the concept has already been agreed.

  1. Record the input data. Height and function of the building, material of walls and floor slabs, climatic and seismic zone, requirements of planning regulations.
  2. Rule out what is not permitted. Fire requirements, the load-bearing capacity of the substrate and the climate exclude some materials and systems straight away.
  3. Define the image. The architectural concept sets the modelling, colour and the share of decor and flat surface — this determines whether three-dimensional elements are needed or a flat slab is enough.
  4. Divide the facade into zones. The plinth, entrance groups, the main plane, the top of the building and the decor are usually resolved in different materials.
  5. Compare options by full cost. The calculation includes cladding, subframe, insulation, installation, logistics and future maintenance costs.
  6. Check on samples. Colour, texture and the junctions between materials are assessed on full-scale samples and a facade mock-up.
  7. Fix the solution in the documentation. The layout, details and specifications are recorded before materials are ordered.

In practice steps three to five are repeated several times: the first calculation shows where the budget is exceeded, and the concept is adjusted — decor on the upper floors is simplified, the slab format is changed, materials are redistributed between zones. This is a normal working process, and time for it should be allowed in advance.

It is useful to keep a table of options from the very beginning: facade zones in the rows, two or three combinations of materials in the columns, with areas, weight per square metre, indicative cost and delivery time. In this form the solution is easier to discuss with the client, the architect and the structural engineer, and when the budget changes it is clear which zone can be simplified without losing the image. The table is updated after each refinement of the calculation, and by the time the documentation is issued it becomes the basis of the specification.

Which cladding suits different building types?

The building type sets the priorities: for a residential complex repeatability and pace of installation matter more, for a hotel a recognisable image, for a public building durability and prestige. The same material may be justified on one project and excessive on another.

Building typePrioritiesTypical combination of materials
Multi-storey residential complexFire safety, pace of installation, budget per square metrePorcelain stoneware or fibre cement on the main plane, GFRC decor and panels in accent zones, stone at the plinth
Business centreContemporary image, junction with glazing, geometric accuracyLarge-format GFRC panels, porcelain stoneware, composite, accents of natural stone
HotelIndividual appearance, robust entrance areas, ease of maintenanceNatural stone and GFRC on the main elevations, simpler cladding on courtyard elevations
Public or educational buildingDurability, repairability, resistance to vandalism at low levelNon-combustible cladding on a subframe, stone plinth, portals of GFRC or stone
Private houseQuality of detail at close range, individuality, ease of careStone, clinker, render with profiled GFRC decor

For multi-storey buildings, zoning by height is economically justified: expensive materials and complex decor are concentrated on the first two or three floors and at the top, where they are noticed, while flat cladding is used on typical floors. Most often this is porcelain stoneware cladding combined with serial elements of GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete).

In refurbishment the logic is different: the choice is limited by the condition of the existing walls and by requirements to preserve the appearance. For buildings with historic decor, lost cornices, window surrounds and moulded details are often recreated in GFRC from measured surveys, since it is lighter than the original materials and does not overload old masonry. On projects in established urban settings, the confined site and work without interrupting the use of the building are also taken into account, which affects the format of elements and the installation method.

How the walls and frame of the building limit the choice of cladding

The material of the external walls determines what load can be transferred to the substrate and with which fixings. In-situ reinforced concrete and solid brick hold anchors reliably, whereas aerated concrete, hollow brick and ceramic blocks have a considerably lower pull-out capacity, and heavy cladding is hung on them with restrictions.

  • In-situ frame with lightweight block infill. The main brackets are usually fixed to the edges of the floor slabs, and the infill takes wind load only. The slab spacing sets the length of the vertical rails.
  • Brick walls of old buildings. The strength of the masonry is uneven, so a survey and anchor tests in several zones are carried out before design.
  • Aerated concrete and hollow blocks. Special anchors are used — chemical ones or those with an extended expansion zone — and the number of fixing points is increased.
  • Precast panels. The condition of the joints and the permissible load on the outer layer are checked.

The load-bearing capacity of the fixing is confirmed by tests on site: anchors are pulled with a proof load, and their type and spacing are set according to the results. How this is done and which values are considered sufficient is described in the article on anchor pull-out tests.

The other side of the question is the weight of the cladding itself. Natural stone 30 mm thick weighs about 80 kg per square metre, porcelain stoneware noticeably less; thin-walled GFRC panels fall in between and depend strongly on shape. The heavier the cladding and the greater its stand-off from the wall, the more substantial the subframe; the calculation is carried out when designing the ventilated facade.

Deviations of the walls from vertical and from plane are assessed separately. On in-situ concrete and brick buildings they often reach several centimetres, and the subframe must have a sufficient adjustment range to bring the cladding into plane. A geodetic survey of the elevations is carried out before brackets are ordered: its results are used to refine their sizes and projection and, for three-dimensional decor, the position of embedded parts and supporting elements.

Fire requirements: what to check before choosing a material

For most multi-storey and public buildings, fire requirements are the first filter in choosing facade cladding. It is not an individual material that is assessed but the assembled system: cladding, subframe, insulation, membrane and the details at openings.

The basic requirements are set by Federal Law No. 123-FZ, the Technical Regulation on Fire Safety Requirements, and the fire hazard class of a facade system is confirmed by fire tests under GOST 31251. In practice the following is checked before a choice is made:

  • the combustibility group of the cladding — GFRC, natural stone and porcelain stoneware are non-combustible materials;
  • whether the system has a confirmed fire hazard class with this particular cladding and this particular insulation, not with similar ones;
  • the combustibility of the wind barrier membrane, if one is provided;
  • the design of window opening surrounds and the presence of fire barriers in the air cavity;
  • height limits of application stated in the system documents.

Replacing a single component — for example, insulation or membrane with cheaper ones — formally makes the system a different one, and confirmation has to be obtained again. The purpose and arrangement of horizontal barriers are explained in the article on fire barriers in the cavity.

The system documents are requested from the supplier before the contract is signed and checked against the design: the names of the cladding, insulation and subframe in the report must match those that will actually be installed. During construction the same conformity is verified at incoming inspection of materials, so the replacement of any component is agreed with the designer in advance rather than upon delivery.

Cost of ownership: what a facade costs over its service life

The installation price is only part of the expenditure on a facade. Over decades of operation it is joined by washing, renewal of protective coatings, replacement of damaged elements and repair of joints, and in these items materials differ more than in initial cost.

Natural stoneWashing; for porous types, periodic water-repellent treatmentSurface of porous stone, jointsReplacement of an individual slab; matching the shade years later is difficult

Fibre cementWashing, repainting as required by conditionPaint coating, edgesSlab replacement, touching up
Render systemWashing, repainting at intervals depending on the compositionFinish coat, areas near the plinth and sillsLocal repair is visible; the whole plane is usually repainted

When comparing options, it is useful to ask the contractor to estimate not only installation but also the expected scope of work over the first ten to fifteen years. It is also worth clarifying how access to the facade is arranged and what reserve of material should be left on site. The inspection schedule and typical operations are described in the article on ventilated facade maintenance.

The cost of ownership is also affected by design decisions unrelated to the material. Drip edges on cornices and sills reduce staining of walls by run-off, removable cladding elements simplify access to the subframe, and anchorage points provided for cradles or fall-arrest systems make every wash and inspection cheaper. These details cost almost nothing at design stage but noticeably reduce expenditure in operation.

Samples, mock-up and control area: how to check the choice before ordering

A solution should be checked in three steps — sample, mock-up, control area. Each answers its own question, and skipping any of them shifts the risk to a stage where corrections cost more.

Material samples. Colour and texture are approved on samples. Their size should allow assessment from a distance of several metres, and they should be viewed outdoors, in different lighting, dry and wet. For natural stone several slabs from the batch are requested in order to see the acceptable variation in shade and pattern.

Facade mock-up. A section assembled on the subframe with a window, a corner and characteristic decor shows what cannot be seen on a sample: the width and pattern of joints, the junction of different materials, the behaviour of shadow on relief. On large projects the mock-up is agreed by the client, the architect and the contractor, after which it serves as the quality benchmark.

Control area on the building. The first work section is installed and accepted separately. Geometry, flatness, uniformity of joints and the practicality of installation details are checked on it, and where necessary the method is adjusted before moving on to the main volume.

The cost of these stages is small relative to the cost of the facade and, as a rule, is recovered simply because disputed questions are settled before the whole batch is purchased.

Approved samples are signed and kept until the end of the works: one set with the client, the other with the contractor. When batches and completed work sections are accepted, colour and texture are compared with these samples rather than with a catalogue or an image on a screen. For natural stone the acceptable limits of shade are additionally recorded — the lightest and the darkest sample.

What to prepare for a facade cladding estimate

For an initial estimate the contractor needs drawings of the building and an understanding of the expected result. A full set of working documentation is not required at this stage, but the more accurate the input data, the fewer assumptions there are in the estimate.

  • Architectural drawings: elevations, plans, sections, in an editable format where possible.
  • Information on the structure: wall material, spacing and thickness of floor slabs, condition of the substrate for buildings under refurbishment.
  • Construction region and building height.
  • Concept or references: preferred materials by zone, character of decor, colour scheme.
  • Insulation requirements, if the thermal calculation has already been carried out.
  • Desired programme and sequence of work by block and elevation.
  • A budget guide, if there is one: this makes it possible to propose a realistic combination of materials straight away.

From these data a preliminary layout is prepared, areas and linear metres of decor are measured, a subframe is selected and an estimate broken down by item is produced. Which items it consists of and how to compare offers from different contractors is discussed in the article on how a facade cost estimate is structured.

If some of the information is not yet available, the estimate is prepared with stated assumptions and refined as data arrive. It is important that the assumptions are listed explicitly in the offer: type of anchors, insulation thickness, adopted slab format, number of moulds for decor. Offers from different contractors can then be compared on the same basis, and differences in price will be explained by the scope of work and the quality of materials rather than by different starting assumptions.

FAQ

Which facade is best for a multi-storey building?

A ventilated rainscreen facade with non-combustible cladding (porcelain stoneware, GFRC or natural stone) on a substructure permitted for the building's height. The choice between them depends on appearance, weight and budget.

Which cladding materials suit a private house?

Natural and cast stone, clinker, render, fibre cement, porcelain stoneware and GFRC all suit a private house. Consider the walls, climate, desired appearance and maintenance budget.

Which is cheaper, porcelain stoneware or GFRC?

On flat surfaces, usually porcelain stoneware. For complex decor such as cornices, columns and window surrounds, GFRC is often more cost-effective than the alternatives, especially natural stone.

Can several materials be combined on one facade?

Yes, this is a common solution. For example, on Dynasty and RUBLEVO BUSINESS PARK, TECHNOFORMA combined GFRC with porcelain stoneware, and on Dynasty also with granite.

Where should cladding selection start?

With the input data: building height and type, wall material, climate, regulatory requirements and budget. Then the concept, visualisation of options and design.

What should you ask a contractor before ordering facade cladding?

Which substructure is proposed and whether it has a suitability document, whether the fire hazard class of the assembled system is confirmed, who does the calculations, shop drawings and anchor testing, whether the contractor installs in-house, what the estimate includes and what as-built documentation is handed over.

Can cladding be chosen after the building has already been built?

It can, but the choice will be narrower. By that point the wall material, slab levels, window positions and permissible loads are fixed, so heavy materials and deep decor projections may require strengthening or prove impossible. Ideally the cladding type is determined at design stage, when embedded parts and loads can be allowed for in the structure.

Is a mock-up needed for a small project?

A full-size mock-up is justified primarily on large projects. For a small building, large material samples and a control area on the facade itself, accepted before work continues, are usually enough. The main thing is to approve colour, texture and joints before the whole batch is purchased.

How should maintenance costs be taken into account when choosing cladding?

It is worth asking the contractor for a list of scheduled works for the first ten to fifteen years: the frequency of washing, renewal of protective compounds and sealant, and the procedure for replacing damaged elements. Comparing materials by the sum of installation and these costs gives a more accurate picture than comparing the initial price alone.
