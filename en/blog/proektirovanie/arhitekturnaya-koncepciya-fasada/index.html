Facade Architectural Concept: Where a Project Begins

A facade architectural concept is the first stage of a project, at which the image of the building is defined: composition, divisions, proportions, materials, colour and key details. The concept is presented as sketches, elevations and 3D visualisations; the client makes a decision on it, and designers then develop the working drawings.

The main test of a good concept is that it can be built. An attractive image that cannot be manufactured in the required quantities, installed on the given wall or approved in the given city will have to be reworked, and the client loses time. So the concept should take materials, production technology and budget into account from the start.

A concept is needed by developers of residential complexes and business centres, building owners refurbishing a facade, and architects who need the cladding worked out. At TECHNOFORMA concepts are prepared by the design department: 12 designers led by two architects, plus 3D visualisers. More on choosing and designing facades is in Facade design and selection.

What it includes

The content of a concept depends on the building, but it usually includes:

  • Analysis of input data — site, surroundings, planning constraints, intended use, client wishes.
  • Composition — base, body and top of the building, vertical and horizontal divisions, rhythm of openings, accents at entrances and corners.
  • Materials and colour scheme — main and secondary cladding materials, colours, textures; a finishes schedule at concept level.
  • Architectural details — cornices, pilasters, window surrounds, portals, reliefs, their scale and profile.
  • Visualisations — views from key points, daytime and evening light, and if needed a photomontage in context.
  • Facade elevations — with the main divisions and materials.
  • Preliminary assessment — buildability of the solutions, indicative material quantities.

A concept is not a working design: it has no substructure shop drawings, details or specifications. But it sets the framework within which the working design will be developed.

For multi-building residential complexes the concept adds principles of variation: how buildings differ from one another within a shared language — colour, accents, entrance ornament — and which elements remain standard. This directly affects production: the more repeated elements, the fewer unique moulds for GFRC elements and the lower the unit cost. A good concept finds the balance between variety and repetition before the working design.

When a concept is essential: new residential complexes and public buildings whose appearance must be approved by the city; facade refurbishment with new cladding; buildings in historic areas and conservation zones; multi-building complexes where a common language must be set in advance; projects with three-dimensional ornament that will be made to order. For a small private house the concept is often combined with the sketch design, but even there a visualisation before ordering materials saves money.

Context analysis

A facade is perceived not on its own but in its surroundings. So the concept starts with an analysis of the place:

  • Surrounding buildings — height, materials and colours of neighbours, historical context. In a city centre the facade must fit in; in a new area it can set the tone.
  • Viewpoints — where the building is seen most: from the main street, the embankment, the courtyard. These elevations get more attention and budget.
  • Orientation — light and shadow affect how relief reads; on north elevations dark materials look gloomier.
  • Climate — precipitation, wind, frost and sun determine acceptable materials and details. Horizontal projections must be protected from standing water.
  • Regulations — land use and development rules, appearance requirements, conservation zones.
  • Use — housing, offices, hotel, educational building — determines the character of the facade, the layout of entrances and ground floors.

The result of the analysis is diagrams, photographic records and conclusions that define constraints and opportunities for the concept.

Input data worth collecting before work on the concept starts:

  • the site's town planning plan and an extract from the land use and development rules;
  • architectural layouts or the building design if one exists — floor plans, sections, levels;
  • for refurbishment — measured drawings and photographs of the existing facades, wall survey data;
  • the client's brief: building class, target audience, style preferences, reference examples;
  • a budget guide for the facade — without it a concept easily drifts into unbuildable solutions;
  • operational requirements: maintenance access, positions for outdoor air-conditioning units, signage, lighting.

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) is particularly important for richly ornamented styles: cornices, columns and ornament of any complexity can be made from models and moulds and repeated in the required numbers. At TECHNOFORMA models and moulds are made in its own mould shops, so a concept with three-dimensional ornament can be checked for production feasibility straight away.

When choosing materials at concept stage it helps to assess them against several criteria at once so the image does not drift from reality:

Visualisations

Visualisations are the main tool of a concept. Through them the client sees the future building and chooses an option. What matters:

  • Several options — comparing materials, colours and degree of ornament on one model.
  • Real materials — textures of specific stones, porcelain ranges and GFRC finishes, not generic library ones.
  • Correct scale — storey heights, opening sizes and cornice depths match the design.
  • Human viewpoints — from pavement level, not only a bird's-eye view.
  • Context — photomontage into the surroundings for approvals and assessment.
  • Evening light — if architectural lighting is planned.

TECHNOFORMA has 3D visualisers on staff, so models are built on the same data that will later go into the working design.

How to commission a concept and what you get: the client hands over input data and a brief; the designer prepares 2–3 image options; for the chosen option, elevations of all facades, visualisations from key viewpoints, a schedule of materials and colours, and preliminary quantities of cladding and ornament. For city approval the package is prepared to local requirements. Time and cost depend on facade area, number of buildings and complexity of ornament and are determined after studying the input data.

Approval

Approval of a concept goes through several rounds:

  1. Within the client's organisation — investor, sales team and facilities management assess image, budget and maintenance.
  2. With the lead designer — the facade concept is coordinated with layouts, structure and windows.
  3. With the city — for buildings whose appearance requires approval (in Moscow this is the architectural and urban appearance approval, AGO), the concept becomes the basis of the document package.
  4. Sign-off — approved visualisations, elevations, materials schedule and samples become the benchmark for the working design and acceptance.

Changing the concept after city approval often requires going through the procedure again, so the choice of materials and colours should be closed at this stage and backed with physical samples.

The regulatory framework for a concept is the site's town planning plan, land use and development rules, appearance requirements where they apply, and restrictions in protection zones of cultural heritage sites. Fire requirements and load codes are not calculated in detail at concept stage but are taken into account as constraints: which materials are permitted for the given height and building class.

Risks and weaknesses of a concept to be aware of in advance:

  • Image without technology. A visualisation with materials not available in the required format, or ornament without fixing details — at working design stage solutions get simplified and the building loses its intended look.
  • Colour mismatch. On screen and in print, shades differ from real materials; without physical samples the final facade may not match the approved image.
  • Budget "later". If cost was not assessed at concept stage, it is often cut during working design at the expense of ornament and cladding quality.
  • Excessive uniqueness. Dozens of non-repeating elements increase the number of moulds, lead times and manufacturing cost.

These risks are reduced when the concept is developed with the cladding manufacturer: feasibility, quantities and repetition of elements are checked straight away rather than after approval.

Stages in developing a facade architectural concept: from brief to concept book

Work on a concept moves from the general to the particular: first the task and constraints are fixed, then the image is explored in several options, and only after a choice is made is one of them detailed. This order avoids spending time on working up solutions that will be rejected.

StageContentOutcome
BriefProject goals, building class, target audience, budget limits, stylistic preferencesAn agreed design brief for the concept
Pre-design analysisStudy of the site, constraints, precedents, floor plans and the structural scheme of the buildingA list of constraints and opportunities, a set of references
Sketch explorationOutline composition, options for articulation, proportions and materials on a simplified model2–3 fundamentally different directions
Choice of directionComparison of options by image, cost and buildabilityAn approved direction with comments
DetailingWorking up elevations, fragments, entrance areas, colour and material solutionsElevations, fragments, visualisations from the main viewpoints
PresentationCompiling the concept book, explanatory note, schedule of materialsA concept book ready for approval

At each stage the client takes a decision, and the earlier it is taken, the cheaper changes are. Returning to sketch exploration after detailing means that a significant part of the work has to be done again.

The duration of the stage depends on the scale of the project and the number of approval rounds. In practice most time goes not on drawing but on waiting for decisions: if the client has no single responsible person, comments from different departments arrive separately and contradict one another. It is therefore useful to name in the brief who approves each stage and within what period consolidated comments are given. It is also recorded separately how many rounds of revisions are included in the work and what counts as a new option rather than a refinement.

How to articulate a facade vertically: base, body and crown

Most successful facades are built on a three-part scheme: base, main plane and crown. It works in both classical and contemporary architecture because it corresponds to how a building is perceived from different distances.

ZoneHow it is perceivedTasksAppropriate solutions
Base (plinth and lower floors)Close up, at eye and hand levelTactility, resistance to damage and soiling, expressive entrancesNatural stone, dense textured panels, portals, bold rustication
Main plane (typical floors)From a middle distance, as a wholeRhythm of windows and piers, repetition of elements, economyGFRC panels, porcelain stoneware, fibre cement boards, a limited set of decorative elements
Crown (upper floors, parapet, cornice)From afar, as a silhouetteA legible termination, concealment of rooftop plantCornices, attics, a change of spacing or relief, accent colour

It makes sense to spend more at the base: here the material is examined at close range and wears more heavily. On typical floors rhythm matters more than richness of finish. How axes, proportions and the scale of details work is examined in more detail in the article on facade composition.

The boundaries of the zones need not coincide with floor levels. In a tall building the base may occupy two or three floors and the crown several upper ones; in a low-rise house the base comes down to the plinth and the entrance. What matters is that the proportions of the parts read from the main viewpoints — from the street, from the courtyard, from distant approaches. Transitions between zones are formed with string courses or a change of texture or cladding format: an abrupt change of material without a horizontal division usually looks accidental.

How can the cost of a facade be estimated at concept stage?

There can be no precise estimate at this stage, but an order-of-cost estimate is essential: without it, choosing an option becomes choosing a picture. For each direction the areas by cladding type and the quantity of decorative elements are measured, and benchmark rates for materials and installation are then applied.

The budget is most affected by a few parameters that can be controlled directly in the concept:

  • The ratio of expensive to basic materials. Stone and complex decoration are concentrated in zones where they are noticed, and the remaining area is covered with more economical cladding.
  • Repetition of elements. The fewer panel and profile sizes, the fewer moulds and tooling and the faster the production.
  • Depth of relief. Large cornice projections and three-dimensional elements require reinforced fixings and affect the subframe.
  • Cladding format. Large slabs are striking but produce more waste and are harder to install at height.
  • Share of glazing and non-standard openings. Every atypical window brings atypical reveals and surrounds with it.

It is convenient to compare options in a table of "image — cost estimate — production time" and take the decision on all three columns at once. What makes up the final calculation at later stages is described in the article on the facade cost estimate.

It makes sense to supplement the order-of-cost estimate with a calculation of running costs. A material that is cheaper at the outset but requires regular repainting or frequent washing may prove more expensive over the life of the building. For comparison it is enough to assess three things: the expected service life of the cladding, the maintenance interval and the possibility of replacing a single element without dismantling its neighbours.

How does a concept differ for a residential development, a business centre and a hotel?

The set of sections is the same, but the criteria for assessing the image differ between building types. This is worth recording in the brief so that options are compared on the parameters that matter for the particular project.

  • Residential developments. The facade works for sales and for the identity of the neighbourhood. What matters is that blocks are distinguishable within a common style, well-considered positions for air-conditioning units, the quality of the lower floors and courtyard elevations, and buildability: large areas require repeatable solutions.
  • Business centres. The priority is prestige, the main entrance, wayfinding and the placement of tenant signage. The facade must allow different letting scenarios for the lower floors without loss of integrity.
  • Hotels. The image of the building is part of the product. The night-time appearance, the arrival zone, terraces and balconies, and the durability of finishes in a resort climate are significant.
  • Public and educational buildings. Durability, vandal-resistant solutions in the lower zone, ease of maintenance and consistency with the status of the institution are important.
  • Private houses and low-rise club houses. More bespoke details and fewer constraints on repetition; the facade is viewed close up, so the requirements for surface and joint quality rise.

The choice of materials also depends on the building type: comparison criteria are given in the article on how to choose facade cladding.

In mixed-use developments these requirements are combined: the residential part, offices and retail premises receive their own accents, while unity is provided by a common palette, a module of articulation and recurring details. For projects in southern and mountain regions, climatic and seismic conditions are added to the brief, since they affect the permissible weight and fixing method of the cladding.

What to check in a concept from the standpoint of production and installation

A concept is buildable if each of its solutions can be manufactured, delivered and fixed. The check is made before an option is approved, while changes amount to editing the model rather than reworking drawings.

  1. Format and weight of elements. Panel dimensions are related to production capabilities, transport dimensions and the lifting capacity of equipment on site.
  2. Joint layout. Joints between panels and slabs should be part of the facade pattern: they are coordinated with windows, string courses and the corners of the building.
  3. Relation to the structural frame. Large and heavy elements are placed where there are floor slabs, columns or walls to fix to.
  4. Corners, reveals, junctions. It is here that a concept most often diverges from reality; outline details are prepared for the key junctions.
  5. Thickness of the facade build-up. Insulation, cavity, subframe and cladding alter the outline of the building and the depth of window reveals.
  6. Access for installation and maintenance. How the facade is reached from scaffolding or lifts, how the cladding is washed and repaired.
  7. Fire requirements. The admissibility of materials for the height and use of the building is checked before the choice, not after.

For facades with complex relief, GFRC panels are convenient: relief, rustication and decoration are cast together with the plane, which reduces the number of separate parts and joints.

A good way of checking is a trial mock-up. For demanding projects a full-size sample of a panel or junction is made: on it the texture, colour, joint and meeting of materials are assessed in real lighting. A mock-up is cheaper than correcting a mistake over hundreds of square metres, and at the same time serves as the reference for accepting production items.

Lighting, equipment and signage: what to build into the concept in advance

The image of a facade is spoiled not by materials but by what appears on it after handover: air-conditioning units, grilles, cables, signs of assorted sizes. If positions for them are defined in the concept, they become part of the architecture.

  • Architectural lighting. It is decided which elements are highlighted in the evening, and recesses and embedded fixings are reserved for luminaires and cable routes. Techniques and luminaire types are examined in the article on facade lighting.
  • Air conditioning and ventilation. Baskets, screens or recesses of a uniform design; ventilation grilles in the colour of the cladding and within the overall joint grid.
  • Signage and wayfinding. Placement zones, maximum dimensions, a fixing method that does not damage the cladding.
  • Rainwater drainage. Concealed or exposed, with downpipes tied to the articulation of the facade.
  • Lower floors. Shopfronts, entrances to commercial premises, canopies, ramps.

These solutions are set out on a separate sheet of the concept book. Later it forms the basis of the facade design rules for tenants and owners.

The same principle applies to balcony balustrades, loggia glazing and solar shading. If the concept does not offer a single solution, residents and tenants will find their own, and within a few years the facade will lose its coherence. It is simpler to provide a standard element in advance and include it in the design.

Checklist before approving the concept and handing it over to detailed design

Before signing off the concept, it is useful to go through a short checklist. It shows whether the concept book is ready to serve as the basis for the next stage.

  • All elevations, including courtyard and end elevations, have been worked up, not just the main one.
  • For each material the type, texture, colour and approximate format are stated.
  • There is a schedule of areas by cladding type and a list of decorative elements.
  • An order-of-cost estimate has been obtained and it fits within the budget.
  • The key junctions — corner, reveal, plinth, parapet, cornice — are shown at least schematically.
  • Samples of materials and textures have been agreed physically, not just on screen.
  • Positions for lighting, equipment and signage have been defined.
  • The comments of the lead designer and the facilities management team have been taken into account.

If the answer to even a third of the items is negative, the concept should be developed further: at the detailed stage these gaps will turn into design changes. Further development — working documentation, fabrication drawings and production support — is carried out by the facade design department.

A list of matters left for the detailed stage is attached to the concept book: what requires calculation, which materials await confirmation by testing, which junctions need to be clarified after the measured survey. Such a list makes the transition between stages transparent: the designer of the working documentation sees where decisions are final and where justified departures are possible. Changes that arise later are agreed with the author of the concept so that individual amendments do not undermine the overall idea.

FAQ

What does a facade concept for a residential complex include?

Context analysis, composition, materials and colour scheme, architectural details, elevations and visualisations from key viewpoints. For multi-building complexes, also principles of facade variation between buildings.

How does a concept differ from a working design?

The concept defines the image and materials; the working design defines how everything will be made and installed: substructure shop drawings, details, layout, specifications.

Is a concept needed for facade refurbishment?

Yes, especially if the cladding or appearance changes. It starts with a measured survey of the existing building and context analysis.

Can a concept be changed after approval?

It can, but changes to an appearance approved by city authorities usually require re-approval. So materials are best approved on samples in advance.

What input data are needed to develop a facade concept?

The town planning plan and development rules, layouts or the building design (for refurbishment, measured drawings and facade photos), the client's brief with style preferences and references, a budget guide and operational requirements — positions for air conditioners, lighting and maintenance access.

How many facade concept options is it reasonable to consider?

Two or three fundamentally different directions at the sketch exploration stage are usually sufficient. A larger number of options rarely improves the result and prolongs the choice. After a direction is approved, one option is detailed, and alternatives are worked up only for individual decisions — colour, plinth material, the design of the entrance area.

Who on the client's side should take part in choosing the concept option?

In addition to the project manager, it is useful to involve the sales or marketing department, the client's technical representative and the facilities management team. The first assess how the image works for the product, the second cost and buildability, the third upkeep and ease of repair. A decision taken without one of these parties is often revisited later.

Can a concept be developed for a building already under construction?

Yes, but freedom of design will be limited by the finished frame, openings and levels. In this case the concept begins with a measured survey and an analysis of the structure, and the main attention is given to cladding, decoration, colour and details that can be implemented without altering load-bearing elements.
