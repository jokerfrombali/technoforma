How to Cut the Facade Budget Without Compromising the Design

Saving on a facade without compromising its appearance is achieved not by substituting a cheaper material but by working on the design: standardising elements, reusing moulds, sensibly combining materials by zone and planning the phasing. These techniques reduce production and installation costs while the building still looks as the architect intended.

The main principle is to save where the viewer will not see the difference, and not to save where safety and durability depend on the decision: substructure, fixings, insulation, fire protection.

This article is for developers, architects and private clients who need to fit a decorated facade into a budget. Related topics are in the section Facade Design and Selection.

Standardising Elements

Standardisation means reducing the number of different element types and sizes while keeping the overall facade pattern. Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) and natural stone decoration are made to size, and each unique element means a separate model, mould or cutting pattern. The fewer there are, the cheaper the facade.

Where there is usually room to save:

  • Window surrounds: if openings differ in width by a few centimetres, they can often be reduced to two or three types without changing the appearance.
  • Cornices and bands: one cross-section profile for the whole building instead of several similar ones; a 20–30 mm difference in projection cannot be read from the street.
  • Corner and end elements: a standard corner instead of different solutions for each corner.
  • Division module: equal lengths for cornice and rustication elements, with make-up pieces only in pre-selected places.
  • Balconies and railings: identical balusters and handrails for all balconies.

Standardisation is best done at the concept stage together with the manufacturer, who can see immediately which elements can be merged without harming the architecture. The design department of TECHNOFORMA Group (12 designers, 2 architects) does this work when developing the concept and layout.

Reusing Moulds

Mould reuse means using one mould for as many products as possible. It follows directly from standardisation: when there are few types and sizes, each mould works on a long run and its cost is spread across many elements.

SituationWhat happens to the price
Many unique elements, 1–5 pieces eachThe cost of models and moulds is the main part of the product price
Several types, dozens of pieces eachTooling costs per element fall noticeably
Repeating sections and buildingsTooling barely affects the price; the main costs are material and casting

For multi-section residential complexes, it pays to design identical sections with a single set of decoration and differentiate them by colour or combination of elements rather than by shape. The facade stays varied while the number of moulds stays minimal. For a private house the same principle works at the level of repeating windows and identical elevations.

Combining Materials

Combining means using different materials depending on the role of each facade zone. Not all areas are equally important to the appearance or equally exposed:

  • Plinth and lower floors — the zone of contact with people, moisture and de-icing chemicals; a durable material is justified here: granite, basalt, dense limestone.
  • Main facade and entrance — the zone people look at; GFRC decoration and natural stone with an expressive texture are appropriate here.
  • Main surface of upper floors — simpler cladding can be used: porcelain stoneware, fibre cement or composite panels, flat GFRC panels.
  • Courtyard and blank facades — a simplified solution in the same colour range.

GFRC is convenient for combined solutions: it can be made with a stone-look texture and colour-matched to natural stone or porcelain stoneware. The key condition is a single colour concept and well-designed junctions between materials; otherwise the savings will show.

Another reserve is logistics and storage. Natural stone from a stock range is usually more affordable than a rare stone ordered from abroad for a specific project, and products made closer to the site or shipped from a regional warehouse cost less to deliver. TECHNOFORMA Group keeps a stock of stone from India, Turkey, China and Iran and has warehouses from Novorossiysk and Makhachkala to Novosibirsk and Yekaterinburg, so the choice of stone and delivery route can also be discussed as part of budget optimisation.

Phasing

Phasing makes it possible to spread costs over time and reduce the risk of rework:

  1. Concept and visualisation: the appearance is fixed, materials are chosen, standardisation is carried out.
  2. Working documentation and trial elements: texture and colour references are approved, manufacturability is checked.
  3. Production by work zones: the first batches for the start of installation, the next ones as it progresses.
  4. Installation by facade or section with staged acceptance.

For multi-building projects, phasing allows buildings to be commissioned in stages; for a private house, the substructure and main cladding can be done first and the decoration installed later, provided the fixing details are built in advance. Small architectural forms, railings and courtyard facade decoration, for example, can be deferred. Anything that affects weather-tightness and insulation cannot.

Where Not to Economise

There are items where saving costs more than it saves:

  • Substructure and anchors: load-bearing capacity is confirmed by calculation and anchor tests; switching to a thinner profile or cheaper fixings is a direct risk of cladding collapse.
  • Insulation and wind barrier: non-combustible insulation of the required thickness and density; cutting corners leads to heat loss and breaches of fire requirements.
  • Fire protection measures: fire barriers, non-combustible materials around openings.
  • Product thickness and reinforcement: set by calculation; reducing them leads to cracks.
  • Design: a lack of working documentation results in rework on site.
  • Junctions and sealing: leaks at windows and parapets damage both cladding and insulation.

If a contractor's offer is cheaper because of these items, it is not a saving but a transfer of costs to future repairs.

What makes up a facade budget, and where are the reserves in it?

A facade budget consists of several items, and the reserve in each of them is different. Before cutting costs it is useful to understand which item weighs most in the particular project: on a building with rich decoration it is tooling and units; on a high-rise block with flat cladding it is the subframe, installation and access equipment.

Cost itemWhat it depends onWhere a reserve usually exists
DesignComplexity of the appearance, number of details, completeness of input dataAlmost none; a good design saves on the other items
Patterns and mouldsNumber of unique sizes, complexity of the reliefStandardisation, repetition of elements, simplification of details hidden from the viewer
Units and claddingArea, thickness, texture, colouring method, type of stoneChoosing texture and colour from proven solutions, optimal dimensions
Subframe and insulationBuilding height, cladding weight, projection, calculation requirementsOnly through calculated optimisation of spacing and sections, not through the safety margin
LogisticsDistance, dimensions of units, packagingSizes to suit standard transport, dispatch in sets by section
InstallationNumber of units and fixings, height, mechanisationLarger units, convenient details, steady deliveries

How these items appear in a contractor's documents is explained in more detail in the article on the structure of a facade works estimate.

How to optimise a facade budget: the sequence of steps

Optimisation produces a result when it is carried out before the working documentation is issued and together with the author of the appearance. Once moulds have gone into production the options narrow sharply: only what has not yet been made can be changed.

  1. Compile a schedule of elements from the concept: types, quantities, dimensions, material.
  2. Sort it by contribution to cost and single out the items with small runs and complex shapes.
  3. Divide the facade into zones by visibility: entrance and lower floors, main elevation, upper floors, courtyard sides.
  4. For each expensive item, propose an option: merge it with a similar one, simplify the profile, change the material in a less visible zone.
  5. Show the options to the architect in visualisations from the points from which people actually see the building.
  6. Record the decisions taken in the documentation and recalculate the estimate.

Steps four and five must not change places with the financial decisions. If a sum is set first and the appearance is then "fitted" into it, what gets cut is whatever is easiest to remove rather than whatever affects perception least.

Texture, colour and unit dimensions: less obvious ways to reduce costs

Part of the saving is hidden in the parameters of the units themselves, which the viewer does not perceive as a separate decision. They are worth discussing with the manufacturer at the sample stage.

  • How the colour is obtained. Integral colouring and surface painting differ in cost, durability and repair options; the choice depends on the shade and the uniformity required. The differences are covered in the article on the colour of GFRC.
  • Number of colours and textures. Each additional shade means separate mixes, reference samples and control of colour variation. Two or three colours on a building usually provide enough variety.
  • Dimensions to suit transport and casting tables. A unit that fits a standard lorry body and needs no non-standard tooling is cheaper to make and to transport.
  • Length of linear units. Longer sections of cornice or string course reduce the number of joints and fixings but require mechanised lifting; the optimum is sought for the particular project.
  • Depth of relief. Projection and undercuts complicate the mould and demoulding. On upper floors fine detailing cannot be read, and the profile can be made more restrained.

The same approach applies to flat cladding: a layout of GFRC facade panels with a minimum number of formats and make-up pieces reduces both the cost of the units and the labour of installation.

Savings on installation: larger units and organisation of the works

Installation becomes cheaper when the facade involves fewer separate operations and less idle time. The material stays the same — what changes is the way it reaches the wall.

The first reserve is larger units. A window surround made of three or four large pieces is installed faster than one made of a dozen small ones and has fewer joints that need sealing. The limits are the weight of the unit and the capacity of the lifting equipment on site. Examples of how such units are divided are given on the page about architraves and window surrounds.

The second reserve is the delivery sequence. When units arrive in sets by section and in the order of installation, the crew does not waste time looking for the right mark and restacking pallets. The third is combining the works: subframe, insulation and cladding carried out by one contractor from one scaffold avoid repeated hire and repositioning of access equipment.

The fourth reserve relates to the season. Temperature-sensitive work is best planned for the warm period, so as not to pay for enclosures and heating.

False economy: which substitutions make construction cheaper and operation dearer?

An economy is false when costs are shifted from the construction stage to the years of operation. The section on the subframe and fixings above concerns safety; here we consider decisions that affect the appearance and the owner's expenses.

Porcelain stoneware, fibre cement or flat panels: what to use for the main plane

For the main plane a material is chosen that gives a calm background for the decoration and requires no bespoke tooling. The choice is determined not only by the price per square metre but also by how the material meets the decorative elements.

Porcelain stoneware offers stable colour and ready-made formats; in design it is important to coordinate the tile module with the window spacing in order to avoid narrow cuts. The conditions of use are described on the page on porcelain stoneware facade cladding. Fibre cement boards are easily cut to size and suit complex layouts. Flat GFRC panels are chosen when the same texture and shade as the decoration are needed, or a large format with a minimum of joints.

In all cases the junction of two materials should be resolved through a moulding, a string course or a recessed joint. Butted in one plane, the difference in texture reads as unfinished work.

What to prepare so that the manufacturer can propose optimisation options

A manufacturer can propose options only on the basis of specific input data. The fuller the set, the more accurate the comparison and the fewer assumptions in the estimate.

  • Elevations and sections with dimensions and, if possible, a three-dimensional model or visualisation.
  • A list of decorative elements with profiles, or at least sketches of the characteristic details.
  • Information on the wall and the frame: substrate material, building height, insulation thickness.
  • The client's priorities: what must not change in the appearance and what is open to discussion.
  • The desired dates and the order in which the blocks are to be completed.
  • The region of construction, on which logistics and climatic requirements depend.

A convenient form of reply is two or three specification options stating exactly what has been changed and how it affects the appearance. Prices are not published on the company's website: the estimate is prepared from the design. The factors on which the result depends are listed in the article on the cost of GFRC decoration.

FAQ

How can I make a facade cheaper without changing its appearance?

Standardise decorative elements, increase the run from each mould, combine materials by zone and split the work into phases.

What should never be skimped on in a facade?

The substructure and anchors, insulation and wind barrier, fire protection, product thickness, design and junctions.

How much can be saved by standardising decoration?

It depends on the project and the original number of types and sizes; the manufacturer assesses the effect from the element schedule.

Can GFRC be combined with porcelain stoneware?

Yes, it is a common solution: GFRC for decoration and accents, porcelain stoneware for large surfaces, within a single colour concept.

Can decoration be installed after the main cladding?

Yes, if the fixing details and embedded parts are provided for in the design in advance.

At which design stage does optimising the facade budget have the greatest effect?

At concept stage and before the working documentation is issued. Until the patterns and moulds are made, the sizes, profiles and distribution of materials by zone can still be changed. Once production has started, only what has not yet been cast can be altered.

Does the number of colours affect the cost of a facade?

Yes. Each shade requires separate mixes or batches of paint, its own reference sample and control of colour variation, and separate storage on site. Two or three colours are usually enough for the facade not to look monotonous.

Which is more economical — large decorative units or small ones?

Large units are, as a rule, more economical to install: fewer fixings and joints, faster placing. The limits are the weight of the unit, transport dimensions and the lifting equipment on site, so the optimal size is selected for the particular project.
