BIM and 3D Modelling of Facades

A facade BIM model is a three-dimensional information model in which every cladding, substructure and decorative element exists as an object with dimensions, material, fixing and a product mark. Drawings, specifications and element schedules are generated from it, and clashes with other parts of the design are checked in it.

The main benefit for a facade is not a nice picture but accuracy: decorative elements, cornices and window surrounds are coordinated with the architecture, structure and windows before production, and the product schedule is generated from the model automatically. For facades with complex glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) and stone decoration this is especially important: each product is made to size.

This article is for architects, BIM managers and developers. Other design questions are covered in the section Facade Design and Selection.

Why BIM

For a facade, BIM solves several practical tasks that take longer and produce more errors with flat drawings:

TaskIn 2D drawingsIn an information model
Element scheduleCounted manually from elevationsGenerated from the model by product mark
Coordination with windows and structureCross-checking sheets from different disciplinesClash checking in a shared model
ChangesAll sheets corrected manuallyChanging the model updates drawings and schedules
Visualising the appearanceSeparate work by a visualiserViews from the same model
Cladding layoutSeparate diagrams for each elevationLayout on a 3D surface, including corners and curves

For the client, this means less rework on site and a more accurate estimate: quantities come from the model rather than a manual count. For technical supervision, it means a clear scheme against which installation is checked.

Modelling the Decoration

A 3D model of a decorated facade is built at several levels of detail:

  • Concept: volumes, divisions, main decorative elements as simplified shapes — for approving the appearance and visualisation.
  • Design: actual profiles of cornices and string courses, window surrounds, columns with capitals, cladding layout with joints.
  • Working: each decorative element as a separate product with a mark, dimensions, positions of embedded parts and fixings, tied to the substructure.

For GFRC decoration, the model is especially useful at corners, on curved sections and where profiles intersect — where it is hard to understand the real shape of a junction from a flat drawing. The model shows how a cornice wraps around a corner, how a pilaster meets a band, and where a make-up piece is needed.

The design department of TECHNOFORMA Group includes designers and 3D visualisers: they prepare visualisations of the facade appearance and working documentation for the products and substructure. Data exchange formats with the lead designer (IFC, DWG, RVT) should be agreed at the start of the project.

Link to Production

The link between the model and production is what really distinguishes digital facade design from a simple 3D picture. The data path looks like this:

  1. A product schedule with marks, dimensions and quantities is generated from the model.
  2. A product drawing is issued for each mark: geometry, thickness, embedded parts, texture.
  3. Master models and moulds are made from the drawings; for complex geometry, 3D data can be used to mill the models.
  4. Products are marked in accordance with the model and layout.
  5. On site, installers place elements according to the marking and an installation diagram taken from the same model.

When the design department and mould-making workshop are in the same company, this chain is shorter: questions of mould manufacturability are resolved in the model before the tooling is made. At TECHNOFORMA, models and moulds are made in the company's own mould-making workshops.

Clashes

Clashes are intersections between elements of different design disciplines that are not visible on flat drawings. Typical facade clashes include:

  • a substructure bracket lands on the edge of a floor slab, in a masonry joint or where services pass through;
  • a GFRC window surround overlaps the window frame or prevents a casement from opening;
  • a cornice intersects the rainwater system or roofing elements;
  • the decoration projection and insulation thickness do not fit between the wall and the sill;
  • decoration obstructs the fixing of outdoor air-conditioning units, cameras or lighting;
  • cladding fixings fall into a building movement joint.

Clash detection in the shared model is carried out before working documentation is issued. Every clash found at this stage is a detail not reworked on site and a product not thrown away.

Savings

Savings from digital facade design come from several sources:

  • Less rework: clashes are resolved in the model, not on the scaffolding.
  • Accurate schedule: exactly as many products are ordered as are needed, without large 'just in case' allowances.
  • Standardisation: near-identical elements are easy to spot in the model and can be reduced to one type and size, cutting the number of moulds.
  • Fast changes: adjusting the appearance updates drawings and schedules.
  • Transparent estimate: quantities come from the model and are easy to check.

The cost of modelling pays back faster the more complex the facade and the more custom elements it has. For a simple panel facade, a 2D layout is enough; for a facade with elaborate decoration, curves and several materials, a 3D model is practically essential.

There are indirect savings too. Visualisations from the model help approve the appearance with the client and the architecture authority faster: instead of several rounds of sketch revisions, a single three-dimensional image is discussed. The model stays with the client after construction — it makes ordering a replacement for a damaged element easier, because the product's mark, dimensions and fixing are known.

It is important to keep the model up to date until construction is complete: changes made on site must be reflected in it; otherwise, by handover it no longer matches the built facade and loses its value for operation.

What input data is needed for a BIM model of a facade?

An information model of a facade is only as accurate as the geometry it relies on. If the model is built on the design axes while the building has been erected with deviations, the schedule of units will be correct on paper and wrong on the scaffolding. The work therefore begins with collecting the input data.

  • Architectural and structural models of the building, or the drawings of these disciplines in their current revision.
  • An as-built survey of the erected walls and floor slabs; for refurbishment and complex geometry, laser scanning with a point cloud.
  • Data on windows and glazed screens: frame dimensions, position in the opening, opening pattern.
  • The thermal calculation with the insulation thickness, which determines the projection of the subframe.
  • The selected fixing system and its range of components: profiles, brackets, anchors.
  • The approved appearance concept: materials, textures, decorative profiles, colour scheme.

An incomplete set does not stop the modelling, but every assumption should be recorded: discrepancies later appear in exactly these places. The data required for an estimate and for the documentation is described in more detail on the facade design page.

Laser scanning and as-built survey: why the model needs the actual geometry

The actual geometry is needed because GFRC and stone units are made to size and are difficult to adjust on site. A cast in-situ frame and infill masonry always deviate from the design; within tolerances this is not a defect, but for cladding with open joints even a small difference becomes visible.

The point cloud obtained by the scanner is overlaid on the design model. The designer sees where a wall leans outwards, where a floor slab projects, where an opening is offset, and takes a decision before production: change the bracket projection, introduce a make-up unit, adjust the size of a surround. For small buildings of simple shape a total station survey on a grid of points may be sufficient.

Such a survey is particularly useful for facades with curved surfaces and three-dimensional relief. The specifics of these solutions are covered in the article on parametric facades and 3D panels.

What to write into the client's requirements for a 3D facade model

The requirements for the model are fixed before work starts, in the brief or in a separate information modelling document. Without them each participant maintains the model in its own way and the data cannot be combined.

Section of the requirementsWhat is definedWhy the facade needs it
Formats and versionsWorking format, exchange format, software versionThe model opens at the lead designer and the contractor without losses
Coordinates and axesCommon base point, levels, grid of axesThe facade aligns with the structure and the windows without offsets
Level of development by stageWhich elements are modelled, and in what detail, at concept, design and working documentation stagesNo excess detail early on and no gaps later
Element attributesMark, material, texture, colour, weight, area, fixing typeSchedules and specifications are compiled without manual input
Naming rulesStructure of unit marks, names of files and viewsThe mark in the model, on the drawing and on the unit is the same
Exchange procedureFrequency of exports, responsible persons, method of clash checkingChanges in adjacent disciplines are not lost

Of the attributes, the weight of a unit is especially important for a facade: it is needed both for selecting fixings and for planning lifting equipment. How the load from the cladding is taken into account in the subframe is explained in the article on ventilated facade calculation.

An element library: how to model repetitive decoration

Repetitive decoration is modelled not piece by piece but through a library of parametric elements. A cornice, a string course, a window surround or a rusticated panel is created once as an object with variable dimensions and is then placed on the facade as many times as required.

This approach gives three practical results. A change to the profile in the library element updates all of its instances on the building. The number of sizes is visible at once, and the designer notices where two almost identical elements can be reduced to one. Finally, the unit mark is generated from the parameters automatically, which reduces the risk of confusion in marking.

The detail of library objects should be reasonable. The fine relief of an ornament is not needed in the overall building model: it makes the file heavier and affects neither the schedule nor clash checking. The exact surface is modelled separately, in the file from which the master pattern is made. For flat cladding the library is simpler: standard GFRC panels differ in dimensions, the position of cast-in fixings and the texture of the face.

Typical mistakes in 3D modelling of facades

Most mistakes are related not to the software but to the organisation of the work. The following occur more often than others.

  • A model without joints. The cladding is shown as a continuous surface; gaps between units are not included, and a dimensional error accumulates along the length of the facade.
  • No subframe. Only the face surface is modelled, so clashes between brackets, structures and services cannot be detected.
  • An outdated background. The facade is tied to an architectural model that has since changed: the windows have moved, while the surrounds remain in their former positions.
  • Tolerances ignored. Units are modelled tight against the wall and the window, with no allowance for substrate deviations and adjustment.
  • Excessive detail. The file becomes so heavy that the adjacent disciplines stop working with it.
  • A gap between model and drawings. Corrections are made directly on the sheets, bypassing the model, and the specifications diverge from the graphics.
  • Nobody responsible. No one answers for keeping the facade part up to date, and after the first changes on site the model is no longer updated.

A simple measure against most of these mistakes is a regular check of the model against a short list before each issue of documentation.

How is the facade model used during installation and after handover?

During installation the model serves as the source of installation layouts and as a tracking tool: it shows which units have been manufactured, dispatched and installed. Status is usually tracked by mark, and a delay on a particular section becomes visible before the crew runs out of units.

The practical benefit on site consists of several capabilities:

  • the installation layout of any area, with marks and levels, is displayed on a tablet or printed;
  • deliveries are made up by section in the order of installation;
  • if a unit is damaged, its mark, dimensions and fixing are immediately known for reordering;
  • as-built documentation is prepared from the same model with the actual changes incorporated.

After handover the model data passes into the operating documents. Information on materials, colours and fixing details goes into the facade passport and simplifies routine inspections and repairs. For natural stone cladding it is useful to include the agreed slab layout in the model; how it is prepared before the material is purchased is described in the article on 3D visualisation of a stone facade.

Step-by-step procedure for joint work on a BIM model of a facade

Joint work rests on a simple rule: each discipline has its own file and its own responsible person, and the federated model is assembled from them on a schedule. The facade designer does not edit the architectural model, nor the architect the facade one; inconsistencies are raised as remarks and resolved by the author of the discipline.

  1. The lead designer hands over the current architectural and structural models and gives notice of planned changes.
  2. The facade designer places the subframe, the cladding and the decoration in a separate file tied to the common coordinates.
  3. The federated model is checked for clashes; the result is issued as a report with remark numbers and responsible persons.
  4. The authors of the disciplines resolve the remarks by the agreed date, after which the check is repeated.
  5. Once the remarks are cleared, the drawings, schedules and specifications for the next stage are issued from the model.
  6. Changes arising on site are entered into the model by the same route — through a remark and a new export.

The frequency of the cycle depends on the pace of design; at the active stage the federated model is usually assembled once every one to two weeks. It is important that the export dates are known to all participants in advance rather than set when one of the disciplines happens to be ready.

FAQ

What does BIM offer for facade design?

An accurate element schedule, clash checking with other disciplines, fast changes and visualisation from a single model.

Why is a 3D model of a decorated facade needed?

To see the real shape of junctions at corners and curves, coordinate decoration with windows and the substructure, and pass accurate data to production.

Which clashes are most often found on facades?

Brackets on the edge of a floor slab, decoration on a window frame, a cornice intersecting a downpipe, not enough space between insulation and sill.

Is BIM needed for a simple facade?

For a facade of uniform panels, a 2D layout is often enough; for complex decoration and several materials, a 3D model is justified.

In what format should the facade model be handed to the contractor?

The format is agreed before work starts. Usually the native format of the software used for the project is combined with the open IFC exchange format for clash checking; unit drawings are additionally issued in DWG and PDF. It is important to fix the software version as well.

Is laser scanning needed for a new building?

Not always. For a building of simple shape an as-built survey of walls and openings is sufficient. Scanning is justified for curved surfaces, refurbishment and facades with a large number of bespoke units, where a dimensional error means remanufacture.

Who should maintain the facade model during construction?

Whoever issues the working documentation for the subframe and the units — as a rule, the design department of the facade contractor. The responsible person is appointed in writing, and the procedure for incorporating changes from site is described in the model requirements.
