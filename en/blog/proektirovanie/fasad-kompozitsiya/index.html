Facade Composition: Rhythm, Proportion and Division

Updated: 2026-09-25 · TECHNOFORMA editorial team

Facade composition is the organisation of all its elements into a coherent whole: vertical and horizontal division, the relative size of parts, the alternation of windows and piers, the distribution of decoration. A good composition reads immediately from a distance; a poor one falls apart into a set of random details.

The three main tools of composition are division, proportion and rhythm. They are set at the architectural design stage, and the cladding and decoration either emphasise this intent or blur it. That is why the cladding layout and the division of decoration into products should follow the composition, not the most convenient panel size.

This article is for architects, designers and clients discussing a building's appearance with the designer. Other design-stage questions are covered in the section Facade Design and Selection.

Principles of Composition

Facade composition rests on several basic principles common to classical and contemporary architecture:

  • Tripartite vertical division: base (plinth, lower floors), main body and crowning (cornice, attic, top floor). Each part gets its own character of finish.
  • Symmetry or balanced asymmetry: a symmetrical facade reads as calm and formal; an asymmetrical one needs balanced masses, otherwise the building seems to be 'falling'.
  • Hierarchy: the main entrance, a central projection or a corner tower is emphasised by size, decoration or material. If everything is emphasised, nothing is.
  • Scale: the relationship of details to the human figure and to the building as a whole. Small decoration is lost at height; large decoration at the entrance feels oppressive.
  • Unity and contrast: a shared language of forms and materials plus contrasting accents at key points.

These principles apply equally to a multi-storey apartment building, a business centre and a private mansion. The difference lies in the scale of elements and the number of levels of division.

Composition is checked from real viewpoints: from across the street, from the approach to the building, from the entrance. A tall building on a narrow street is seen at a steep angle, and the lower cornices hide the upper ones; a wide facade on a square is read in full. So projections and decoration sizes are chosen with regard to where the building will be viewed from.

Divisions

Divisions are lines that split the facade surface into parts. They are made with cornices, bands, pilasters, rustication, changes of material or colour, and cladding joints.

VerticalPilasters, lesenes, bay windows, vertical rows of windowsThe building looks taller and slimmer

For cladding, it is important that panel joints and junctions between decorative elements coincide with the compositional lines or are subordinate to them. A joint running through the middle of a pilaster or randomly cutting a cornice destroys the design. So the cladding layout is set out from the facade's axes and divisions, and make-up pieces are placed in pre-selected, inconspicuous locations.

Proportions

Facade proportions are the ratios of the parts to each other and to the whole: plinth height to building height, pier width to window width, cornice projection to storey height. Good proportions make a facade harmonious even without decoration; poor ones cannot be rescued by any amount of ornament.

Architects use several approaches:

  • Modular system: all dimensions are multiples of a base module, such as the column spacing or window width.
  • Classical ratios: the golden section, simple whole-number ratios (1:2, 2:3, 3:5), the proportions of the classical orders, in which the sizes of details are derived from the column diameter.
  • Regulating lines: diagonals connecting key points of the facade.

For a decoration manufacturer, proportions have a practical consequence: the cornice profile and the sizes of window surrounds and capitals must be calculated from the actual dimensions of the building. A cornice taken from a catalogue without adjusting it to the facade height almost always looks either too small or too heavy. That is why custom profiles are usually developed for each project.

Window Rhythm

Facade rhythm is the regular repetition of elements: windows, piers, balconies, pilasters. A metric rhythm is an equal spacing of identical elements — calm and regular. A rhythmic sequence with variation — growth or alternation (for example, wide window, narrow window) — gives the facade movement.

Window rhythm is the basis of the whole facade's rhythm. Ways of working with it:

  • aligning windows on vertical axes on all floors;
  • grouping windows in pairs or threes with wider piers between groups;
  • highlighting the central or corner axis with a different window type or surround;
  • varying window height by floor — taller on lower floors, shorter on upper ones;
  • uniting windows on several floors with a common surround.

Breaks in rhythm — windows of different widths with no system, misaligned axes, balconies in random places — are immediately read as a mistake. If the floor plan does not allow a consistent rhythm, it can be restored with decoration: blind windows, niches, pilasters.

Decoration as a Design Tool

Decoration is not an ornament added to a finished facade but a tool that reveals its composition. Each element has a specific role:

  • the cornice crowns the volume and sheds water from the wall;
  • bands and string courses set horizontal divisions and the scale of storeys;
  • pilasters and columns provide vertical rhythm and the order system;
  • window surrounds, hoods and keystones emphasise the window rhythm;
  • rustication gives weight to the base and marks the corners;
  • balustrades complete terraces, balconies and parapets.

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) allows decoration of almost any shape with low weight and thickness, and its colour and texture are matched to the main cladding. FAQ

What is facade composition?

The organisation of facade elements into a coherent whole through division, proportion and the rhythm of windows and decoration.

Which facade proportions are considered harmonious?

There is no universal formula; architects use modular systems, classical ratios and the proportions of the orders, adjusting them to the real scale of the building.

What is facade rhythm?

The regular repetition of windows, piers, balconies and decoration — either uniform or with alternation and growth.

How does decoration affect facade composition?

Cornices and bands set horizontals, pilasters verticals, window surrounds the window rhythm, and rustication the base and corners.

Can I use a cornice from a catalogue?

You can, but its profile should be recalculated for the height and scale of the building; otherwise it will look too small or too heavy.
