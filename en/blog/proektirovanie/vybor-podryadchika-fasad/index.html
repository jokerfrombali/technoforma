How to Choose a Facade Contractor: A Checklist

How to choose a facade contractor: check that it designs, manufactures the cladding and installs it with its own crews, that it has completed projects of comparable scale with addresses, and that the contract fixes stages, deadlines and the acceptance procedure. The price in the commercial offer is the last criterion, not the first: a low rate often means that part of the work has been left out.

Below is a checklist for developers, general contractors and private clients choosing a contractor for a ventilated rainscreen facade or cladding in glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC), natural stone or porcelain stoneware. Other decisions at the design stage are covered in the section Facade Design and Selection.

Verifying Experience

A contractor's experience is verified not by the text on its website but by projects you can see. What to request and how to check:

  • A list of projects with address, number of storeys, cladding area and dates of works. A 1,000 m² project and a 20,000 m² project are different tasks in terms of logistics and installation management.
  • What exactly the contractor did: design, production, installation or only installation supervision. The phrase 'took part in the project' may mean delivering a single batch.
  • Experience with your material: installing porcelain stoneware panels and installing large-format GFRC or stone decoration require different skills.
  • Experience in your region: climate, seismicity, logistics.
  • Site visit: if possible, see the facade in person — joints, geometry, junctions.

Several large projects running at the same time is not a minus if the contractor has its own installation team and production capacity, but it is worth asking about directly.

Own Production or Resale

The key question in the checklist: does the contractor manufacture the cladding itself or buy it from a third party? The difference shows in deadlines, quality and responsibility.

CriterionOwn productionResale
Design changesResolved within one companyVia an order to the supplier, on its timescales
Non-standard elementsModels and moulds made for the projectLimited to the supplier's catalogue
Reorder after breakage or errorFrom the same mix and mouldsRisk of colour mismatch and a new queue
Responsibility for defectsOne partyDispute between installer and factory

Production can be verified by visiting the site: is there a mould-making workshop, a finished goods warehouse, a technical control department. TECHNOFORMA Group has its own mould-making workshops (it makes models and moulds itself), a production site of more than 2 ha and, according to the company, GFRC capacity of over 20,000 m² per month. The company imports natural stone from India, Turkey, China and Iran and processes it on its own equipment.

Documents

Documents to request from a contractor before signing the contract:

  • incorporation documents, an extract from the Russian Unified State Register of Legal Entities (EGRUL), and details of membership in a self-regulatory organisation (SRO) if required by the type and scope of work;
  • quality documents for the cladding and substructure: product passports, test reports, documents on the fire safety of the system;
  • a technical certificate or other document confirming the suitability of the facade system, if a ventilated substructure is used;
  • an example of working documentation — shop drawings, panel layout, junction details;
  • samples of as-built documentation from previous projects: certificates of concealed works, as-built drawings.

Separately, check the combustibility class of the cladding. GFRC is a non-combustible material (NG class), but fire safety requirements apply to the system as a whole, including insulation and wind barrier. If the contractor cannot show documents for the system, that is a warning sign.

The Contract

The facade works contract is the main document that protects both parties. Templates are publicly available, but a downloaded template should be adapted to the project. Sections that must be included:

  1. Subject and scope: which works and materials are included, with reference to the design and schedule of quantities.
  2. Price and how it can change: fixed or based on actual quantities; what happens if the design changes.
  3. Deadlines by stage: documentation, production, delivery, installation, linked to handover of the work front.
  4. Client obligations: input data, access, electricity, scaffolding or lifts, storage.
  5. Quality and acceptance: references, procedure for interim and final acceptance, as-built documentation.
  6. Warranty obligations and the procedure for remedying defects.
  7. Liability of the parties and dispute resolution.

If the price in the contract is not tied to the design and the schedule of quantities, any clarification of quantities turns into a supplementary agreement with a new price.

Red Flags

Signs that you should stop and re-check a contractor:

  • a price noticeably below the market with no explanation of how it is achieved;
  • the estimate omits the substructure, insulation, fixings, delivery or lifting equipment;
  • no working documentation, with installation proposed 'on site';
  • no projects with addresses, or they cannot be visited;
  • the contractor agrees to substitute 'an equivalent' material without the architect's approval;
  • a demand for full prepayment before production starts;
  • no documents for the facade system and materials;
  • installation is carried out by changing subcontractors rather than in-house crews.

One red flag does not mean the contractor is bad, but each should be discussed and answered in writing.

The opposite sign of reliability is a contractor who asks many questions itself: requests the architectural design, surveys, data on the load-bearing walls, wind region and seismicity, and offers to inspect the site before pricing. This means the price will be calculated from real conditions rather than facade area multiplied by an average rate. At TECHNOFORMA, for example, the cost of construction and installation works is determined after inspecting the site, and measurements are taken by the company's own surveyors.

How to choose a facade contractor for the type of building

The requirements for the contractor depend on what is being built: a high-rise residential complex, a hotel and a private house carry different risks, and experience with one building type does not transfer automatically to another. Before sending out enquiries, it is useful to decide which competences are critical for this particular project.

Building typeWhat matters most to check with the contractor
Multi-storey residential complexProduction capacity and number of teams, working by sections, experience of installing from cradles and hoists
Business centreAccuracy of geometry, the interface between cladding and glazing, the quality of joints and corners
Hotel, resort propertyWork with ornament and complex relief, logistics to a remote region, seasonal constraints
Public buildingCompliance with the design and fire safety requirements, completeness of as-built documentation
Private houseWillingness to take on a small volume, availability of standard moulds, neat abutments

On high-rise projects the subframe calculations and the organisation of material hoisting carry particular weight. The requirements for such buildings are discussed in the article on the facade of a multi-storey residential building. If the site is in a seismic area, the contractor is asked separately how this is taken into account in the design of the fixings.

For projects with natural stone there is one further requirement: experience in selecting and laying out the slabs. Stone from different blocks varies in tone, and without sorting the facade turns out patchy. For GFRC cladding, the ability to design the division into units and to make moulds for the specific ornament matters more.

The contractor's design office: what to check before the contract

A facade with ornament or large-format panels cannot be installed from architectural drawings alone: working drawings of the units and details are needed. The contractor should therefore have its own design office or a permanent design partner, and this deserves the same scrutiny as production.

  • Who develops the cladding layout, the fixing details and the unit drawings, and how many people are in the office.
  • Whether the subframe is calculated for wind load and the self-weight of the cladding, and who signs the calculations.
  • Which software is used, and whether there is a three-dimensional model of the facade for checking junctions and clashes.
  • How changes are handled: who approves a revised detail and how it reaches the factory and the site.
  • Whether the designers support installation and attend site when issues are disputed.

Ask to see the documentation set for a completed project: it shows how thoroughly the corners, reveals, parapets and abutments have been detailed. At TECHNOFORMA, facade design is handled by 12 designers and 2 architects, which allows the documentation to progress in parallel with production.

How to assess installation resources and site organisation?

Installation resources are assessed on three points: how many people the contractor can actually bring to site, who manages them, and how the hoisting and storage of materials are organised. The headcount on paper matters less than the presence of site engineers and a clear programme by work section.

Questions worth asking:

  • Whether the teams are on the payroll or engaged under contract for a specific project.
  • Who will be the site manager and the works supervisor, and which projects they have worked on.
  • What access equipment is planned: scaffolding, mast climbers, cradles or aerial platforms. The choice affects both speed and cost; a comparison is given in the article on scaffolding and cradles.
  • Whether there is a method statement, and how storage and protection of the units from damage are dealt with.
  • How the contractor works in the cold season and which operations are postponed.
  • Who is responsible for health and safety and for permits to work at height.

If the contractor offers installation supervision instead of its own installation, clarify how many specialists it will send to site, for how long, and what their duties include. The full scope of the construction, installation and facade works is conveniently fixed in an appendix to the contract.

It is useful to ask for a labour histogram: how many installers will be on site in each month. It shows whether a realistic rate of progress has been allowed for. If the programme has the whole facade installed by a single team in a short period, the promised dates are unlikely to be met.

Quality control: which procedures the contractor should have

A reliable contractor is distinguished not by a promise of quality but by a documented control procedure: what is checked, at which stage, and who signs off the result. Ask for this to be explained using the most recent project as an example.

StageWhat is checkedDocument
Release of unitsGeometry, colour and texture against the reference sample, cast-in fixingsBatch certificate, quality control stamp
Acceptance of the substrateWall deviations, condition of concrete or masonryAs-built survey, certificate
Installation of brackets and anchorsSpacing, embedment depth, tightening force, anchor testsConcealed works certificate, test report
Insulation and wind barrierTight fit of the slabs, number of fixings, absence of gapsConcealed works certificate
Hanging the claddingPlane, joint width, alignment of ornamental linesSite diary, staged acceptance

It is important for the client to take part in the interim inspections: once the panels are hung, the subframe and insulation are covered, and correcting a defect will require dismantling. What to look at during the final stage is described in the article on the handover of facade works.

Which questions should be put to the contractor at the first meeting?

The first meeting is not for discussing price but for understanding how the company thinks and how deeply it engages with the project. Ten questions whose answers say more than a presentation:

  1. What input data is needed for an accurate calculation, and what is missing at present?
  2. Which parts of the works are carried out in-house and which are passed to subcontractors?
  3. How many projects are currently in hand and how are they spread over time?
  4. What, in the contractor's view, is the most difficult thing about this facade?
  5. Which details of the design would it propose to change, and why?
  6. What does the programme look like from the issue of documentation to handover of the last section?
  7. What is required from the client and the main contractor for the programme to be met?
  8. How are additional works documented and valued?
  9. What is the warranty period and how are defects remedied after handover?
  10. Which building can be visited and which clients can be spoken to?

Evasive answers to the questions about subcontracting and workload are a reason to return to the checks on experience and production.

Note also who attends the meeting. The presence of an engineer or the head of the design office alongside the sales manager means that the technical side will be discussed in substance rather than deferred until the contract is signed.

How to compare contractors: a scoring table

When there are several candidates, impressions from meetings are conveniently converted into scores. The client sets the criteria and their weighting in advance and then assesses each bidder on the same scale. The weightings below are given as an example: for an urgent project the weight of the programme is higher, for complex architecture that of design competence.

CriterionIndicative weightingWhat the score is based on
Experience on comparable projects20–25%Addresses, area, scope of work, client references
In-house production and capacity15–20%Factory visit, current workload
Design office10–15%Examples of working documentation, calculations
Installation resources15–20%Staff, works managers, equipment
Completeness and cost of the bid20–25%Compliance with the schedule, absence of omissions
Contract terms and warrantiesabout 10%Stages, liability, warranty period

The scoring is best done independently by two or three members of staff, with the results then compared. If the formal selection takes the form of a competition, the procedure described in the article on the facade tender will be useful.

What the client should prepare before approaching contractors

The accuracy of the contractor's response depends on the input data. The fuller the package, the fewer the assumptions in the calculation and the easier it is to compare companies with one another.

  • architectural drawings: elevations, plans, sections and details in an editable format;
  • information on the load-bearing and enclosing walls: material, thickness and, for refurbishment, the year of construction;
  • the address of the building, the number of storeys and the top level;
  • the preferred cladding materials, samples or photographs of comparable buildings;
  • the expected start and completion dates and the sequence of the blocks;
  • site conditions: access, storage area, and whether the main contractor has scaffolding or hoists.

If there is no design yet, a sketch and a measured survey are enough: a contractor with a design office will give a budget estimate and suggest which solutions should be developed first.

FAQ

How do I choose a contractor for facade works?

Check projects with addresses, own production and installation crews, documents for the system and materials, then compare contracts and the completeness of estimates.

Where can I get a template contract for facade works?

Standard templates are publicly available, but they must be adapted: tie the price to the design and schedule of quantities, set out stages, client obligations and the acceptance procedure.

Which is better: a contractor with its own production or an installation company?

A contractor with its own production resolves changes and reorders faster and bears single responsibility for the product and its installation.

What documents should I request from a facade contractor?

An EGRUL extract, SRO membership details if needed, quality documents for materials, fire safety documents for the system, examples of shop drawings and as-built documentation.

Why do contractors' facade prices differ so much?

Estimates often cover different scopes: without the substructure, insulation, delivery or equipment. Compare offers against a single schedule of quantities.

What is installation supervision and when is it appropriate?

Installation supervision is technical direction of the installation by the manufacturer: its specialists train the team and check the details and acceptance, while the works themselves are carried out by another organisation. The format is appropriate when the main contractor has its own installers but no experience with the particular cladding.

Can different contractors be hired for the subframe and for the cladding?

Yes, but the client then takes on the coordination. The subframe is calculated for the weight and fixing arrangement of specific panels, so the contracts must state who performs the calculation, who accepts the subframe for hanging and who is responsible for deviations.

How many contractors should be compared?

Three to five are usually enough to see the spread of prices and approaches. With more candidates the quality of each check falls, and with one or two it is hard to judge whether the bid is in line with the market.
