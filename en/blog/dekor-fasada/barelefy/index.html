Bas-Reliefs and Decorative Panels on the Facade

A bas-relief on a facade is a relief image that projects from the wall plane by less than half its volume: an ornament, a coat of arms, a narrative composition, a floral or geometric motif. A decorative panel is a broader term: a large decorative composition that may be in relief, mosaic or flat. On modern buildings such elements are usually made of glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) or natural stone and installed with mechanical fixings.

The main selection criterion is scale and viewpoint: the relief must read from the distance it will be seen from and withstand the weather without maintenance. Bas-reliefs are used on entrance areas of residential complexes, public and administrative buildings, hotels, and private houses with a distinct style. Other element types are covered in the section Architectural facade decor.

Types of relief

Architecture distinguishes several types of relief by depth and the way the image is formed:

TypeEssenceWhere it fits
Bas-reliefLow relief, the image projects less than half its volumeFriezes, panels over openings, inserts between windows, entrance areas
High reliefFigures project more than half their volume, partly detached from the backgroundAccent compositions at eye level, pediments, main facades
Sunken (incised) reliefThe image is cut into the planeLettering, coats of arms, geometric motifs, minimalist facades
Ornamental reliefA repeating pattern without a narrativeBands, friezes, railings, screens, cladding panels

For a facade panel, relief depth is chosen according to installation height and lighting. In raking sunlight even a shallow bas-relief casts expressive shadows; on a north-facing facade the pattern needs to be deeper and more contrasting.

Motifs and styles

The motif and style of a relief are defined by the building's architecture. In classical and neoclassical projects these are floral motifs (acanthus, laurel branches, garlands), cartouches, coats of arms, allegorical figures, meander and other antique ornaments. Art Deco features geometric sunbursts, fan motifs, stylised figures and stepped compositions. Oriental architecture uses geometric and floral ornaments without human figures, calligraphy and intricate star patterns.

On contemporary buildings a decorative bas-relief often becomes abstract sculpting: a wave, a parametric pattern, a relief texture on large panels. Such solutions rely not on history but on the play of light and shadow.

A few rules for choosing a motif:

  • the motif and scale should support the facade composition, not compete with it;
  • on a multi-storey building fine details on upper floors will not be visible — enlarge the pattern;
  • images related to a brand, the history of the place or the building's purpose are best agreed at sketch stage, before the mould is made;
  • in a historic setting the relief style may be restricted by urban planning requirements.

Production

Making a GFRC relief involves several stages:

  1. Sketch and model. An architect or sculptor prepares a sketch, from which a 3D model is made and the panel is divided into products, allowing for joints and weight.
  2. Master model. A full-size model is carved or sculpted; the relief is worked out on it and then approved.
  3. Mould. A mould is taken from the master model. If the product repeats, the mould is reused many times.
  4. Casting. A mix of glass fibre, sand, cement and water is sprayed or poured into the mould; stiffening ribs are formed on the back and cast-in fixings installed.
  5. Demoulding and inspection. Once it has gained strength, the product is removed and its geometry and face are checked.

TECHNOFORMA makes models and moulds in its own mould shops, with quality control at every production stage. The company's production site covers more than 2 ha. In natural stone, a relief is cut on CNC machines and finished by hand; this takes longer and the product is noticeably heavier.

Fixing

A relief heavier than a few kilograms must not be set on adhesive alone. GFRC or stone panels are fixed mechanically: via cast-in fixings on the back of the product to brackets, anchors or the substructure of a ventilated rainscreen facade. The scheme is determined by calculation that takes into account self-weight, wind load, thermal movement and the type of substrate.

Practical requirements:

  • anchoring into the load-bearing structure (concrete, solid brick); in hollow materials special anchors are used, verified by pull-out tests;
  • movement joints are left between panels and then sealed or left open as designed;
  • all steel fixing parts are corrosion-protected or made of stainless steel;
  • the top edge of the relief must have a flashing or slope so that water does not run behind the product.

When installed in a ventilated rainscreen system, the panel becomes part of the cladding: its fixings are coordinated with the rail spacing at the design stage.

Applications

Bas-reliefs and panels appear on facades of many building types. Typical applications:

  • Entrance areas of residential complexes — a relief frieze over the portal, ornamental pilasters, cartouches with the complex's name.
  • Public buildings — universities, theatres, administrative buildings, where the relief reflects the purpose or symbols of the institution.
  • Hotels and resorts — themed panels related to the region, ornamental bands.
  • Private houses — small inserts over windows, coats of arms, panels on fences and gates.
  • Modern office buildings — abstract relief sculpting on large-format panels as an alternative to flat cladding.

In every case a relief benefits from well-planned lighting: architectural lighting at a low angle emphasises shadows and makes the pattern readable in the evening.

Another condition for a good result is consistency with the rest of the decor. If the facade already has cornices, window surrounds and bands, the panel should continue their proportions and profile rather than look like a random insert. That is why a relief is best designed together with the other cladding elements, not added after the facade is finished.

How to choose relief depth for the installation height?

Relief depth is set by the viewing distance: the higher and further away the panel is, the larger the pattern and the deeper the difference between planes must be. Fine detailing that looks good on a sample in the workshop merges into a flat grey patch from twenty metres.

The figures below are working guidelines rather than regulatory values; they are refined on the visualisation and on a full-size trial fragment.

Facade zoneViewing distanceRelief depth, guidelineCharacter of the pattern
Entrance, eye level1–5 m10–30 mmFine detailing, small ornament, lettering
Ground to second floor5–15 m30–60 mmMedium module, crisp outlines without small details
Middle floors, piers15–30 m50–100 mmLarge forms, enlarged ornament
Attic, pediment, crowning friezeover 30 mfrom 80–100 mmSilhouette relief, minimal detail, deep shadows

Besides depth, the slope of the side faces matters. Vertical and undercut faces give a sharp shadow but make demoulding harder and collect dust; gentle slopes are easier to produce, yet the pattern looks softer. Exterior relief is normally designed with a draft angle and without horizontal ledges where water and snow can settle.

A separate case is relief texture on large surfaces, where the pattern repeats across dozens of panels. The rules described in the article on 3D panels and parametric facades apply here: the pattern module is coordinated with the panel layout and the spacing of the subframe.

What to make a facade bas-relief from: comparison of materials

In practice three groups of materials are used for exterior relief: GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete), natural stone and polymer compositions. The choice is determined by the weight of the unit, the production run, the required level of detail and the fire safety requirements for the building.

MaterialWeight, guidelineDetailRepetitionLimitations
GFRCTypically 25–50 kg/m² with a 10–20 mm shellHigh, reproduces the master modelOne mould per seriesA model and a mould are required, so a one-off unit costs more than a serial one
Natural stone (limestone, sandstone, marble)From 130 kg/m² at a slab thickness of 50 mm and moreHigh, depends on the stone and the equipmentEach unit is cut anewHeavy weight, reinforced fixing, long lead time
Architectural concrete (solid)About 120 kg/m² per 50 mm of thicknessMediumMould per seriesWeight, limits on size and thin details
Polyurethane, coated expanded polystyreneA few kilograms per m²MediumSerial mouldsCombustibility, vulnerability to impact, usually low-rise buildings only

GFRC is a non-combustible material, so it is used on buildings of any height, including cladding within a ventilated facade system. Stone is chosen where the natural texture itself matters and the relief is viewed at close range, for example a carved limestone insert by the entrance. A detailed comparison of the two materials is given in the article GFRC or natural stone.

In practice the materials are often combined: the background of the composition is assembled from GFRC facade panels, while the plinth and the portal surround beneath it are made of granite or another dense stone.

Dividing a panel composition into units: sizes, joints and weight

A composition larger than a single panel is divided so that the joints follow the natural boundaries of the design: the outline of a figure, the line of an ornament, the frame. A joint that cuts through a face or the central motif is visible from any distance and cannot be corrected during installation.

The size of one unit is limited by three factors: the dimensions of the mould and the transport, the lifting capacity of the equipment on site, and the spacing of the load-bearing members to which the panel is fixed. For GFRC, units of up to several square metres are usual; larger ones are possible but require a steel stiffening frame on the back and a separate calculation.

  • the joint width between units is typically 8–15 mm and is kept constant across the whole composition;
  • joints are aligned with rustication, string courses and the layout of the adjacent cladding;
  • each unit receives a mark, and an assembly diagram is supplied with the composition — without it the parts are easily mixed up;
  • installation proceeds from the bottom row and from the central axis so that accumulated tolerance moves to the edges;
  • before dispatch it is useful to dry-assemble the composition at the production site and check that the pattern matches at the joints.

Packaging and transport are considered separately. Relief units are carried on edge or in individual crates, with padding at projecting details; stacking them flat is not suitable. On site the elements are stored under cover and lifted by the designated embedded parts, not by the projecting parts of the design.

The division, the embedded parts and the assembly diagram are recorded in the shop drawings of the units. The same drawings are used to calculate the weight of each element and to select the fixings.

Colour, texture and lighting: how to make the relief legible

The legibility of a relief depends on the tone of the surface no less than on its depth. On a light matt surface the shadows are soft and distinct; on a dark or glossy one the pattern is lost and reflections distort the form.

GFRC is coloured through the mass or on the surface, and the texture is set by the mould: smooth, stone-like, or slightly rough after treatment. For figurative compositions a single calm tone is more common and details are not picked out in colour — the modelling has to work on its own. The background and the projecting parts are sometimes separated by texture: a smooth relief on a rough field.

In the evening the relief is revealed by grazing light. Luminaires are placed close to the wall plane, from below or from the side, so that the beam falls at an acute angle; frontal floodlighting, on the contrary, wipes out the shadows. Cable routes and luminaire positions are planned before the units are manufactured — more on this in the article on architectural facade lighting.

If the composition stands next to cladding in other materials, the tone is chosen from samples on site rather than from a catalogue: the same colour looks different beside warm stone and cold metal.

What to prepare when ordering a relief from a sketch?

For a quotation and a start of work, the elevation with the installation location, a sketch of the composition and information about the wall are sufficient. The more precise the input data, the fewer iterations at the model stage.

  1. A drawing or photographs of the facade with the dimensions of the area for the composition and the installation height.
  2. A sketch, references or a finished 3D model; for a coat of arms or a logo — a vector file.
  3. The wall material and construction, the presence of insulation and the type of facade system.
  4. The desired colour and texture, samples of the adjacent cladding.
  5. The number of repeats: a single composition or a series of identical inserts.
  6. Delivery dates and access conditions for installation — scaffolding, lift, crane.

Next comes approval of the 3D model, then of the master model or a trial fragment, and only after that is the mould made. Changes at the model stage are inexpensive; after the mould has been taken they mean remaking it. How work on bespoke units is organised is described in the article on bespoke decor to an architect's sketch; the drawings and fixing details are developed by the design department.

Which mistakes shorten the service life of a relief panel?

Most problems with relief on a facade are related not to the material but to water and joints. If the top edge of the composition is not protected, moisture gets behind the unit, freezes and eventually shows as streaks and efflorescence on the face.

  • relief with horizontal ledges without a fall — dirt and ice remain on them;
  • a composition in the run-off zone of a cornice or sill with no flashing above it;
  • rigid filling of joints with mortar instead of an elastic sealant;
  • a pattern that is too fine for a great height;
  • installation without checking the load-bearing capacity of the anchors in the substrate.

Maintaining a finished relief is simple: periodic inspection of joints and fixings, washing with water at moderate pressure and, where necessary, renewal of the water repellent. Abrasive cleaning and aggressive acid-based products are not used: they damage the surface layer and change the tone.

When the installed composition is accepted, the following are checked: pattern match at the joints, uniform joint width, absence of chips on projecting details and a consistent surface tone in daylight. Colour remarks are more reliably assessed on a dry facade: wet concrete darkens temporarily, and the difference between elements appears greater than it really is. Fixings and embedded parts are inspected before the joints are closed with sealant.

FAQ

What is a facade panel?

A large decorative composition on a wall: in relief, mosaic or flat. On facades it is most often a GFRC or stone relief assembled from several products.

What is the difference between bas-relief and high relief?

A bas-relief projects from the plane by less than half its volume, a high relief by more than half, with figures partly detached from the background.

What is an exterior decorative bas-relief made of?

GFRC, natural stone, less often polymer materials. Plaster is not suitable for exterior use.

Can a bas-relief be made to my own sketch?

Yes, a master model and mould are made from the sketch, then a sample for approval. The cost depends on size, complexity and the number of repeats.

How is a heavy panel fixed to a facade?

Mechanically: via the product's cast-in fixings to anchors, brackets or the rainscreen substructure according to a calculated scheme.

How much does a GFRC bas-relief weigh?

The weight depends on the shell thickness and the stiffening ribs; typically it is 25–50 kg per square metre, several times less than carved stone of the same area. The exact weight of each element is stated in the shop drawings and is used to calculate the fixing.

Can a relief panel be installed on an insulated facade?

Yes. In a ventilated facade the composition is fixed to the subframe; in a rendered system it is mounted on brackets that pass through the insulation to the load-bearing wall. A heavy relief must not be bonded to the insulation.

How many units can be taken from one mould?

It depends on the mould material and the complexity of the relief. Flexible and glass-fibre moulds usually withstand dozens of casts, so a series of identical inserts costs noticeably less per unit than a one-off composition.
