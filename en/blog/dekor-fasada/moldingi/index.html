Facade Mouldings: Profiles and Applications

Facade mouldings are linear profiled elements applied to the wall surface: string courses, bands, panel and opening surrounds, frames. Unlike a cornice, a moulding barely projects and does not shed water — its task is graphic: to divide the facade into fields, emphasise horizontals and tie the decorative elements into one system.

The main selection criterion is a profile and scale coordinated with the rest of the decor and the viewing distance. The second criterion is a material and fixing method compatible with the facade system: rendered, ventilated rainscreen or stone cladding.

This article is for architects, contractors and private clients. A general overview of elements is in the section Architectural Facade Details.

Moulding vs Cornice

Both mouldings and cornices are linear profiled elements, so they are often confused. The difference lies in projection and function.

FeatureMoulding (band, string course)Cornice
Projection from wallsmallsignificant, cantilevered
Functiondividing the surface, decorationcrowning the wall, protecting the facade from water
Locationstring courses, frames, surroundscrowning (below the roof), above the plinth, above storeys
Fixinganchors and adhesive, light fixingsbrackets, designed for weight and snow load
Drainageusually not required; top with a slight fallessential: fall, drip, flashing

The boundary between them is conventional: a string course with a projection and a drip already acts as a small cornice. So when ordering a facade moulding, describe it not by name but with a dimensioned profile drawing — then the manufacturer and installer have the same understanding of whether the element needs drainage and brackets.

Profiles

A facade moulding profile is built up from simple classical mouldings, each giving its own play of light and shadow:

  • fillet — a flat narrow band separating other mouldings;
  • torus — a convex semicircular profile giving a soft shadow;
  • cavetto — a concave quarter circle, a deep shadow;
  • ovolo — a convex quarter circle;
  • cyma recta and cyma reversa — S-shaped profiles (straight and reverse), the basis of most cornices and string courses;
  • scotia — a deep concave profile typical of bases.

Combining these mouldings produces profiles of any complexity: from a simple rectangular band to a multi-stepped moulding. Typical solutions by purpose:

  • String course — a horizontal band at floor-slab level separating the tiers of the facade.
  • Sill course — a continuous band along the bottom of the windows, uniting them into a strip.
  • Panel surrounds — frames on the piers.
  • Plinth profile — capping the plinth and making the transition to the wall.

The higher a moulding is placed, the bolder and more contrasting its profile must be: at height, small mouldings merge into a single line.

For multi-storey buildings, especially with rainscreen facades, non-combustible materials are chosen — GFRC or stone. GFRC mouldings are cast as linear elements whose length is set by the mould and ease of installation; joints are placed where they are least noticeable. Product properties are given in the data sheet.

The choice of material is also linked to the facade colour. GFRC mouldings can be produced through-coloured — the colour is then uniform throughout the face layer and minor chips are less noticeable. The second option is painting after installation together with the wall, when an exact match with the render colour is needed. Stone mouldings are usually not painted: their value lies in natural texture, so the stone shade is chosen from samples before ordering a batch.

Fixing

Being lightweight does not mean mouldings can simply be glued on.

  1. Rendered facade. GFRC units are bedded in adhesive and additionally anchored to the load-bearing wall through embedded fixings. Where there is insulation, the anchors pass through it into the wall.
  2. Ventilated rainscreen facade. The moulding is fixed to the substructure or with separate brackets to the wall. Horizontal bands must not block the ventilation cavity: an air passage is left behind them or breaks are made.
  3. Stone. Linear elements are fixed on stainless steel anchors, like cladding.

Joints between linear elements are made with a gap for sealant or repair compound: a continuous rigid fill cracks under thermal movement. Corners and ends are made as separate corner elements or mitred at 45°. The top surface of horizontal bands is given a fall so that water does not linger.

Mistakes

Typical mistakes when installing mouldings:

  • A profile that is too small on a tall facade — it is not legible from the ground and looks like a joint.
  • Different profiles on the same tier — string course, frames and pediments must be built from one system of mouldings.
  • A horizontal top ledge without a fall — water, dust and snow accumulate and streaks appear.
  • A blocked ventilation cavity on a rainscreen facade — disrupts the system's performance.
  • Adhesive-only fixing at height — risk of the element falling as the adhesive ages.
  • Rigid joint filling — cracks along joints after the first winter.
  • Combustible decor on multi-storey buildings without regard to fire safety requirements.

Most mistakes are eliminated at the design stage: moulding layout on the facade elevation, fixing and junction details, and material selection by building height. How do you size a facade moulding for the height of the building?

The height and projection of the profile are set by the distance from which the element will be viewed: the further away the observer, the larger the section and the fewer small members it should contain. The figures below are working ranges for preliminary drawing; final dimensions are checked on the facade elevation and, where possible, on a full-size mock-up.

Facade zoneViewing distanceTypical profile heightCharacter of the section
Plinth and ground floor3–10 m80–150 mmsmall fillets and beads are acceptable
Floors two to five15–30 m150–250 mmtwo or three large members
Floors six to twelve30–60 m250–400 mmone dominant member and a fillet
Above the twelfth floorfrom 60 mfrom 400 mma simple step with a deep shadow

The projection of a moulding is usually between a third and a half of its height. A smaller projection gives a weak shadow and the band reads as a painted stripe; a larger one brings the element close to a cornice and calls for brackets and water shedding. Shadows are sharper on south-facing elevations, so the profile can be more restrained there and deeper on north-facing ones.

The width of frames around panels and openings is tied to the width of the pier: a frame taking up more than a fifth of the pier begins to compete with the windows. Horizontal divisions are covered in more detail in the article on string courses between storeys.

Setting out mouldings on the elevation: joints, corners and returns

The setting-out drawing breaks every run into individual pieces with marks and lengths. It is produced before the moulds are made, because it determines how many standard sizes are needed and where the joints will fall on the facade.

  • Joints are hidden on pilaster centre lines, behind downpipes and on pier centre lines; a joint is never placed in the middle of a window opening.
  • A standard piece is usually 1.5–3 m long: two fixers can lift such units without a crane, and the number of joints stays moderate.
  • Make-up pieces are no shorter than 300–400 mm, otherwise they are difficult to secure with two anchors.
  • External and internal corners, returns around pilasters and end caps are cast as separate parts: cutting on site gives an uneven joint and exposes the hollow of the unit.
  • Movement joints in the building are repeated in the decoration: the run is broken above such a joint and closed with a cover strip or an elastic filler.

The outcome is a schedule of marks and an installation layout, both of which form part of the facade shop drawings. The same layout is used to count the moulds: the more repeating lengths there are, the less tooling is needed and the shorter the production cycle.

What to prepare when ordering GFRC mouldings

A facade elevation and a profile section are enough for a preliminary estimate; a fuller set is needed to start production. The more accurate the input data, the fewer corrections at the mould stage.

  • the profile section with dimensions at a scale of 1:1 or 1:2;
  • facade elevations with the runs referenced to levels and grid lines;
  • the type of facade system and the wall build-up: substrate material, insulation thickness, finishing layer;
  • colour and surface texture, by sample or catalogue;
  • information on facade access: scaffolding, cradles, hoists;
  • a programme in which the decoration is coordinated with windows, insulation and finishes.

If only a sketch or a photograph of a reference exists, the manufacturer's designer draws the profile and agrees it with the architect. Standard and bespoke GFRC mouldings are made by the same process; the difference lies in whether a new mould is required. Texture and shade are best approved on a sample viewed in daylight next to the wall material.

In what order is linear decoration installed?

Mouldings are fixed once the substrate is ready and the levels have been set out, but before the wall receives its final coat of paint. Work proceeds in sections from the top down so as not to soil areas already completed.

  1. Survey setting-out: the level of the underside of the run is transferred around the whole perimeter rather than measured from the windows, since openings almost always deviate.
  2. Checking the substrate and marking the fixing points from the installation layout.
  3. Installing the corner pieces, then the standard ones from the corners towards the middle of the section; the make-up piece ends up in the least visible place.
  4. Fixing with anchors to the cast-in inserts, checking plane and line with a string or a laser level.
  5. Filling the joints, making good the fixing holes and, where necessary, fitting a flashing on the top face.
  6. Final treatment: water repellent or painting together with the wall.

On long elevations the run is sighted along its full length before the joints are filled: a deviation of a few millimetres on one piece becomes a visible wave over the length of the block.

How mouldings work with rustication, pilasters and windows

A run rarely exists on its own: it meets other elements, and it is at these junctions that the quality of the design shows. The general rule is that a horizontal element either runs continuously or stops against a vertical one, but never ends on the plane of the wall.

At pilasters the band is usually returned: it follows the projection with the same profile. On a rusticated wall the run is placed at the boundary between the rusticated and the plain tiers, and the spacing of the rustication on the facade is chosen so that a full course finishes beneath the profile. A sill band is coordinated with the window flashing: the flashing rests on the top face of the run or passes beneath it according to the design detail.

The top of the wall is given a larger profile. GFRC cornices are appropriate here, while the moulding below repeats one or two of their members at a reduced size, so that the facade reads as a single system.

Which mouldings suit which building: residential complex, business centre, private house

The range and complexity of the runs depend on the type of building: on a multi-storey block repetition and a large scale matter, while on a villa it is the close-up detailing that counts. Typical solutions are given below; they are refined in the design.

Type of buildingRuns usedPoints to watch
Multi-storey residential complexbands at tier boundaries, frames around groups of windows, plinth profiletwo or three profiles for the whole building, long series of identical pieces, non-combustible material
Business centrerestrained bands and portal surrounds, runs at podium levelaccurate geometry of long lines, junctions with glazing and ventilated cladding
Hotel, public buildingdeveloped bands, entrance surrounds, panelsexpressiveness of the lower floors, resistance to soiling at the entrance
Private housesill and storey bands, frames, plinth bandsmall scale, proportion to the windows, option of standard profiles

In residential complexes the economics of decoration are governed by repetition: a single profile used on every block pays for its mould many times over, whereas a dozen different sections increase both the preparation time and the number of make-up pieces. At concept stage the whole graphic scheme of the facade is therefore usually reduced to a limited set of runs, and it is their position rather than their section that is varied.

On a private house the task is the opposite: the volume is small, and making bespoke moulds is not always justified. It is sensible to start with standard profiles and add bespoke elements only where they really define the appearance, at the entrance and on the main elevation. On public buildings and hotels the focus is on the lower floors: these are what the visitor sees from a few metres away, so the quality of edges, joints and surface is judged more strictly here than on the upper tiers.

In service: inspection, cleaning and repair of runs

Mouldings made of mineral materials need no regular maintenance, but they do need periodic inspection. This is normally combined with the seasonal facade inspection in spring, after the freeze-thaw cycles.

  • joints: sealant coming away, cracks, dark streaks beneath the joint;
  • top face: standing water, moss, dirt streaks;
  • chips on edges and corners at ground-floor level;
  • traces of rust near the fixing points.

Dirt is washed off with water at moderate pressure and neutral detergents; abrasives and acid-based products damage the face layer. Chips are made good with a colour-matched cement-based repair compound, and the sealant in the joints is renewed over time. More detail is given in the article on caring for GFRC.

FAQ

How does a moulding differ from a cornice?

A moulding barely projects and serves to divide the wall; a cornice has a large projection and protects the facade from water.

Can mouldings be installed on an insulated facade?

Yes: on adhesive with anchors through the insulation into the load-bearing wall, or on the rainscreen substructure.

Which material should I choose for a facade moulding?

For multi-storey buildings, a non-combustible one: GFRC or stone. Polymer decor is restricted by fire safety requirements.

What is a facade moulding profile?

The cross-section of a linear element built up from classical mouldings — fillets, tori, cavettos and cymas.

How should moulding joints be filled?

With a gap for elastic sealant or repair compound, to accommodate thermal movement.

How long are GFRC mouldings?

Standard pieces are usually 1.5–3 m long, which makes them convenient to transport and to fix by hand. Exact lengths are determined by the setting-out on the facade elevation, which takes account of grid lines, corners and joint positions.

Does the top of a moulding need a metal flashing?

With a small projection and a sloping top face, a water repellent is generally sufficient. A flashing is fitted on wide bands where water and snow linger longer; the decision is taken on the basis of the design detail.

Can mouldings be added to an existing building?

Yes, during refurbishment the decoration is installed on the existing facade. The substrate is surveyed first and the load-bearing capacity of the anchors is tested, after which fixing details are developed for the actual wall construction.
