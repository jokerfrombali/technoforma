Facade Columns: Orders, Proportions and Materials

Facade columns are vertical elements that set the rhythm and scale of a facade: they frame an entrance, carry a portico or balcony and divide the wall plane into vertical bays. On modern buildings a column is usually decorative: the load is taken by a reinforced concrete or steel post, while the visible part is cladding or a hollow unit.

The main selection criterion is not the material but the proportions. A column whose diameter-to-height ratio and capital and base sizes do not match the order and the number of storeys looks out of place, however expensive its execution. The material is chosen second, based on weight, installation method, climate and budget.

This article is written for architects, developers and private home owners deciding how to treat an entrance, a gallery or a main facade. General principles for working with decorative elements are collected in the section Architectural Facade Details.

The Classical Orders

An order is a system of proportions between a column and the entablature it carries (architrave, frieze, cornice). Classical tradition recognises five orders: Tuscan, Doric, Ionic, Corinthian and Composite. They are distinguished mainly by the capital and base, but behind the visual pattern lie different proportions.

  • Tuscan — the simplest and most massive: a smooth shaft without fluting, a capital of abacus and echinus, a plain base.
  • Doric — austere, with or without fluting (vertical grooves), a frieze with triglyphs and metopes.
  • Ionic — recognisable by a capital with paired volutes (scrolls); a slimmer shaft and a profiled base.
  • Corinthian — a capital with two or three rows of acanthus leaves and small volutes; the most ornate and slender.
  • Composite — combines Ionic volutes and Corinthian acanthus.

For a facade, choosing an order means choosing the character of the building. Tuscan and Doric suit plinths, galleries, massive private houses and neoclassical buildings with restrained modelling. Ionic and Corinthian belong on grand entrances, upper tiers and buildings with abundant, finely detailed ornament. The classical rule of superposition — heavier order below, lighter order above — also works on multi-storey facades.

Column Proportions

Column proportions are traditionally expressed in modules — the lower diameter of the shaft (sometimes the radius). Different treatises give different heights for a column including capital and base, so the values below are approximate and are used as a starting point in design.

OrderColumn height, in lower diametersCharacter
Tuscanabout 7massive, squat
Doricabout 8austere
Ionicabout 9slender
Corinthian, Compositeabout 10the lightest

Two more parameters without which columns on a house facade look wrong:

  • Entasis — a gentle tapering of the shaft towards the top (the upper diameter is usually 1/6–1/5 smaller than the lower one). A strictly cylindrical column appears concave.
  • Intercolumniation — the spacing between columns. Columns set too close turn an entrance into a fence; set too far apart, they weaken the entablature. The spacing is coordinated with the diameter and with the width of the openings behind the colonnade.

In practice, the architect starts from the given height (one storey, two storeys, portico height), derives the diameter and then checks whether the facade columns fit the porch and do not block windows. If the calculated diameter is too large, a more slender order is chosen or the columns are set on pedestals.

Load-Bearing vs Decorative Columns

Two fundamentally different types of column are found on facades, and they must not be confused at the design stage.

  • A load-bearing column carries loads from a floor slab, balcony, canopy or portico. It is designed by a structural engineer and built in cast-in-place reinforced concrete, steel or reinforced brickwork.
  • A decorative column carries no load. It is either cladding over a load-bearing support or a free-standing hollow element fixed to the wall or base.

The most common solution in new construction is a hybrid: inside stands a reinforced concrete or steel post of calculated section, and outside it is enclosed by two half-shells, a capital and a base made of GFRC or stone. This separates the tasks: strength comes from the frame, while form, texture and colour come from the cladding. The shell must not take load from the slab — a gap or movement joint is left between its top and the structure, otherwise the cladding will crack under shrinkage and deflection.

Fully decorative half-columns and columns set off from the wall are fixed to the load-bearing wall with anchors and embedded plates. If such a column stands alone, its base and wind stability are also checked by calculation.

Materials

The choice of material determines weight, installation method and how accurately the profile of the order can be reproduced.

MaterialStrengthsLimitations
Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC)thin-walled hollow units, complex modelling of capitals, non-combustible materialrequires a layout and fixing design, production from moulds
Natural stonegenuine texture, durabilityheavy weight, costly carving of capitals, demands on the substrate
Cast-in-place concretecombines load-bearing and decorative functionshard to achieve a clean surface and capital detail
Polyurethane, coated expanded polystyrenelow weight, low pricecombustibility, vulnerability to impact and UV, restrictions on facade use

According to the manufacturer, GFRC consists of four components — glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement and water; it is rated non-combustible (NG class under Russian standards) and has high frost resistance, which is further improved with admixtures. For columns, what matters most is that the material makes it possible to cast a hollow shaft with entasis and fluting, as well as a capital with three-dimensional ornament, while the unit remains noticeably lighter than stone. Exact properties depend on the mix design and are stated in the product data sheet.

Stone is justified where the column is seen close up and natural texture matters: entrance areas, plinths, interiors. Lightweight polymer columns are appropriate indoors or in low-rise private construction, subject to fire safety requirements.

Installation

Installation of facade columns starts not on site but in the design: each column needs a breakdown into elements, fixing details and an embedded-plate drawing.

  1. Preparing the substrate. The geometry of the load-bearing post or wall is checked, and axes and levels are set out. Deviations of the support must fit within the gap between it and the cladding.
  2. Setting the base. The base is installed first on a levelled substrate — on mortar or on bearing elements, depending on the design.
  3. Installing the shaft. Hollow shafts are assembled from half-shells or drums, each fixed to the load-bearing post or wall with metal brackets and anchors of stainless or galvanised steel. The inner cavity is sometimes filled with grout — only if the design provides for it.
  4. Capital and entablature. The capital is fixed to the structure independently rather than resting on the thin-walled shaft.
  5. Joints and finishing. Vertical and horizontal joints are filled with repair compound or sealant, followed by final painting or water-repellent treatment.

On TECHNOFORMA Group projects, fixing design, production and installation are carried out by one team: the design department, in-house mould-making shops and an in-house installation division. This removes the split of responsibility between the decor supplier and the contractor.

What kinds of facade columns are there by shape and position?

Apart from the order, columns are distinguished by how they relate to the wall and what cross-section they have. This determines the construction, the fixing method and the number of moulds needed for production.

TypePositionWhere usedDesign features
Free-standingSet away from the wall, visible from all sidesPorticoes, galleries, entrance canopiesShell around the full perimeter, usually on a load-bearing post
Engaged, three-quarterAdjoins the wall, projecting by more than half the diameterFormal facades, projecting baysFixed to the wall, complex junction at the rear
Half-columnProjects by half the diameterArticulation of the wall, framing of entrancesOne half-shell, low weight
Paired columnsTwo columns on a common pedestalCorners of porticoes, accent axesCommon base or plinth, coordinated capitals
Giant-order columnUnites two or more storeysMain facades of public and residential buildingsShaft of many sections, intermediate fixings
Square column, pierRectangular sectionContemporary architecture, ground-floor arcadesFlat panels, simpler moulds and joints

The flat counterpart of a column is the pilaster: it repeats the proportions of the order but projects from the wall by only a fraction of its width. How a pilaster differs from a half-column, and when each element is more appropriate, is discussed in the article on pilasters and half-columns.

The choice of type is directly linked to the depth that can be allotted to decoration. A free-standing column with base and capital needs a portico projection of at least one and a half to two of its diameters; otherwise it is pressed against the wall and loses its volume. Where such depth is not available, for example along the street building line, it is more logical to change to half-columns or pilasters while keeping the same order and spacing.

What elements is a column assembled from?

A column consists of three essential parts, the base, shaft and capital, and is supplemented by a pedestal where required. In production and in the estimate these are separate units with their own moulds.

  • Pedestal. A rectangular support beneath the column; it raises the column above ground level and makes it possible to reduce its height and hence the diameter of the shaft.
  • Base. A moulded ring on a square slab, the plinth. It visually spreads the load and conceals the joint between the shaft and its support.
  • Shaft. The main volume, plain or fluted. Tall shafts are divided into sections along their height and into two or more half-shells around the circumference.
  • Capital. The crowning part with the abacus slab on top. It is the most labour-intensive element: a Corinthian capital requires a complex multi-part mould.

Joints between parts are placed at natural locations, beneath the necking of the capital and above the upper torus of the base, where they are hidden by the shadow of the moulding. The vertical joints of the half-shells are oriented towards the wall or to the sides, so that the shaft appears to be a single piece from the main viewpoint. The design and technology of crowning and supporting parts are covered in detail in the article on column capitals and bases.

When ordering, it is important to establish at once whether all the columns on the project are identical. The corner columns of a portico, columns at the wall and ordinary ones may differ in the orientation of joints and cast-in parts, although they look the same externally. Such differences are reflected in the marking, so that each section reaches its proper place during installation.

Where on a facade are columns appropriate, and on which projects?

A column is appropriate where there is something for it to carry visually: a canopy, a balcony, an entablature, a projecting volume. Placed simply in front of a flat wall, with nothing completing it above, it is perceived as an accidental detail.

  • Residential complexes. Columns and half-columns articulate entrances, ground-floor arcades with commercial premises and the upper tiers. A giant order spanning several storeys is used on main facades facing the street or the courtyard.
  • Hotels and boutique residences. A main entrance portico with a canopy for vehicle drop-off, colonnades of terraces and galleries.
  • Public and educational buildings. A portico on the axis of symmetry as the main accent of the facade; the order is usually severe and the scale large.
  • Business centres. More often piers of rectangular section to the height of the lobby, without details of the orders.
  • Private houses. A porch, a terrace, a balcony above the entrance; two to four columns are usually sufficient.

In every case the columns work together with the other elements of the entrance: steps, portal, door surround and canopy. How to bring these parts together into a single composition is described in the article on portals and entrance groups.

The number of columns also matters. A single column on a facade almost always looks unconvincing, whereas a pair or a row of four to six sets a rhythm. On the long facades of residential complexes the colonnade is usually confined to the central part or to corner accents, and on the rest of the surface the rhythm is maintained by pilasters.

What should there be above and below a column?

Above the column there is an entablature or a simplified substitute for it; below it, a support raised above the paving. Mistakes in these two zones are more noticeable than inaccuracies in the proportions themselves.

The architrave beam rests on the abacus of the capital, with the frieze and cornice above it. The classical rule is that the face of the architrave lies in the same plane as the upper part of the shaft, not the edge of the abacus; if the beam is wider, the capital appears crushed. The height of the entablature is selected to suit the order, usually within the range of one fifth to one quarter of the column height. A projecting facade cornice above the colonnade also protects the capitals from running water.

Three things matter at the bottom:

  • the plinth of the base is not set directly on the paving but raised on a step or pedestal, so that water and de-icing agents do not damage the foot of the column;
  • the top of the pedestal and the horizontal surfaces of the base are given a slight outward fall;
  • the axes of the columns are aligned with the axes of the piers or pilasters on the wall behind them, and the spacing is coordinated with the openings.

If a full entablature does not fit within the storey height, it is reduced to an architrave and cornice, but the horizontal element above the capital should not be removed altogether.

What data is needed for the design and costing of columns?

For a preliminary assessment, a dimensioned elevation and an indication of the order are sufficient. A working design and an accurate estimate require a fuller set of input data.

  1. Elevations, plans and sections of the installation zone: height from the support to the underside of the beam, spacing and number of columns.
  2. Information on the load-bearing core: section and material of the post and its actual deviations as surveyed.
  3. Type of column and order, presence of fluting, entasis and pedestal.
  4. Surface requirements: colour, texture, a sample of the adjacent finish.
  5. Site conditions: access for plant, lifting height, sequence of works.
  6. Construction area, to allow for wind load on free-standing elements.

This data is used to determine the division into sections, the number and type of moulds, the cast-in parts and the fixing scheme. The cost is made up of the production of models and moulds, the units themselves, metal components, delivery and installation; it is noticeably affected by the number of identical columns, since the cost of the mould is spread over the whole series. Design options are described on the page for GFRC columns and capitals. If the order and dimensions have not yet been determined, they are selected at the facade design stage together with visualisation.

Typical mistakes in designing columns on a facade

Mistakes are made most often not in the material but in the proportions and the relationship to the building. Listed below are mistakes that occur regularly and are difficult to correct after production.

  • Shaft too slender. The diameter is set by the section of the load-bearing post rather than by the height, and the column looks like a pipe with a capital.
  • Cylinder without entasis. Simplifying the form saves little, yet is immediately noticeable.
  • Capital of the wrong scale. A standard capital is placed on a shaft of a different diameter; the necking and abacus no longer match the shaft.
  • Mixing of orders. The base of one order, the capital of another and the entablature of a third.
  • Column without completion. A balcony or canopy slab begins immediately above the capital, with no architrave.
  • Deviations of the core not allowed for. The load-bearing post is off axis, and the shell has to be shifted or cut on site.
  • Forgotten services. A downpipe, lighting cable and earthing can conveniently be routed inside a hollow column, but only if this is provided for in advance.

Almost all of these mistakes are identified at sketch stage if the column is drawn to scale together with the entablature and support, rather than as a separate catalogue item.

The view of the column from below is checked separately. A capital located at the height of two or three storeys is perceived differently from the drawing: fine ornament merges, and the projection of the abacus appears smaller. For a giant order the details of the capital are enlarged and the number of leaves and volutes is reduced, while the overall silhouette is retained.

How should columns be used and maintained?

Columns of GFRC and stone do not require regular maintenance but do need periodic inspection. The weak points are the lower part of the shaft, the joints and the top of the capital.

  • Foot of the column. In the zone up to one and a half to two metres above ground, impacts from trolleys, cleaning equipment and vehicles are possible. In driveways and at drop-off areas, bollards or a stone pedestal are provided.
  • Joints. The sealant in the joints between sections and half-shells ages over time; its condition is checked, as a rule, once a year and it is renewed as necessary.
  • Top of the capital. The horizontal surfaces of the abacus and volutes collect dust and water; where exposed, they are protected with a water repellent or a flashing.
  • Cleaning. The surface is washed with water and neutral agents; abrasives and aggressive compounds are not used on limestone, marble or painted concrete.
  • De-icing agents. In winter, de-icing compounds should not be allowed to accumulate at the foot; chips and salt marks at the base are a signal to check the drainage of the paved area.

Local chips on GFRC are made good with a colour-matched repair compound, and painted surfaces are renewed over time with facade paint. A retained mould will be useful if an individual section has to be replaced, so the period for which it is kept is worth discussing with the manufacturer when ordering.

FAQ

Which columns are best for a private house facade?

Scale and style decide. For a one- or two-storey house the Tuscan or Doric order is usually chosen; as for material, GFRC or stone over a load-bearing post if the column supports a balcony or canopy.

Can an existing concrete column be clad?

Yes. The load-bearing column is enclosed with GFRC or stone half-shells, and the base and capital are installed separately. A survey of the actual geometry and a fixing design are required.

How do I choose column diameter for a given height?

As a guide, column height is about 7–10 lower diameters depending on the order: the more ornate the order, the more slender the column. Final dimensions are coordinated with the facade architecture.

Do decorative columns carry loads?

No. A decorative shell must not take loads from floor slabs; the load is carried by an internal post designed by a structural engineer.

What are facade columns made of?

GFRC, natural stone, cast-in-place concrete and, less often, polymers. For exterior use, frost resistance, non-combustibility and impact resistance are important.

What should be chosen for a facade: a column, a half-column or a pilaster?

A free-standing column is needed where there is a portico, canopy or gallery. A half-column and a pilaster articulate the wall surface and require no space in front of the facade. The less depth that can be allotted to decoration, the flatter the element chosen.

Can a downpipe or cable be routed inside a column?

Yes, if the column is hollow and this is provided for in the design. A duct is left inside, with a removable element or access panel for maintenance. Where there is a load-bearing post inside the shell, the solution is agreed with the structural engineer.

How many columns are needed for an entrance group?

The number is determined by the width of the porch and by the spacing, which is coordinated with the diameter. For a private house two or four columns are usually sufficient; in a portico an even number is customary, so that a bay rather than a support falls on the axis of the entrance.
