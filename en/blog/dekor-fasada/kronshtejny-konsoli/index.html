Facade Brackets and Corbels

Decorative facade brackets and corbels are projecting elements that visually support a cornice, balcony, bay window, pediment or sill ledge. A classical corbel has an S-shaped profile with volutes, often with acanthus leaves on its face; modillions are a row of small brackets under the crowning cornice.

The main selection criterion is to establish whether the element carries a real load. A decorative GFRC or stone bracket only represents a support; if a corbel must hold a balcony slab or a cornice with a large projection, the load is taken by a steel or reinforced concrete structure, and the decorative element clads it.

This article is for architects, structural engineers and clients designing balconies, bay windows and cornices in a classical style. Other decorative elements are in the section Architectural Facade Details.

Decorative vs Load-Bearing

Two types of bracket are found on facades, and confusing them is dangerous.

FeatureDecorative bracketLoad-bearing corbel
Functionrepresents a support, completes the compositiontakes load from a slab, cornice or bay window
Constructionhollow unit or claddingreinforced concrete or steel, as calculated
Designed byarchitect, decor manufacturerstructural engineer, with calculations
Fixinganchors to the wall sized for the unit weightembedded in the building's load-bearing structure

A hybrid arrangement is common in modern construction: the balcony slab or cornice rests on a concealed steel or reinforced concrete cantilever, and outside it is covered by a decorative GFRC bracket of the required profile. This achieves a classical look without the decorative unit working in bending.

It is important to record in the design which element carries what. If a decorative corbel props the slab tightly, it will become loaded when the slab deflects and may crack — so a movement joint is left between them.

Modillions Under the Cornice

Modillions are small brackets set in a row under the soffit of the crowning cornice. The term derives from "module": in the classical order their size and spacing followed the column module. Modillions are characteristic of the Corinthian and Composite orders; in the Doric order their place is taken by mutules — flat plates with guttae.

What matters when designing modillions:

  • Spacing. Modillions are arranged rhythmically, coordinated with the axes of columns, pilasters and windows; a modillion should fall above each axis.
  • Corners. At the cornice corner, a diagonal corner modillion or two modillions are placed — a classical layout problem solved on the drawing.
  • Coffers. The fields between modillions on the cornice soffit are often filled with rosettes.
  • Scale. A modillion must be legible from the ground: on tall buildings they are made larger and more widely spaced.

On modern facades modillions are usually decorative: the cornice is carried by its own brackets or substructure, and the modillions are fixed to its soffit or to the wall. GFRC units allow them to be light and geometrically identical around the entire building perimeter.

Corbels Under Balconies

A facade corbel under a balcony is the most expressive option: a large bracket with a volute visually carries the balcony slab. In new construction a balcony slab almost always cantilevers from the floor slab or rests on concealed steel elements, so the corbel is cladding.

  • Size of the corbel is coordinated with the balcony projection and slab thickness: the bracket's height is usually greater than its projection, otherwise it looks weak.
  • Number — at least two per balcony, at the ends; intermediate ones are added on long balconies.
  • Junction with the slab — via a movement joint so that slab deflection is not transferred to the decor.
  • Drainage — the top of the corbel must not collect water from the slab; a drip or flashing is formed along the slab edge.

The same logic applies to bay windows, canopies and sill ledges: decorative corbels emphasise the support, while the real load is carried by a structure designed by the engineer. In the refurbishment of old buildings the reverse can be true: historic stone corbels are genuinely load-bearing, and replacing them with hollow units is permissible only after the balcony has been strengthened.

For multi-storey buildings, decorative brackets are usually made of GFRC: they reproduce stone modelling, do not load the wall and are not restricted by fire safety rules as polymers are. If a load-bearing function is required, a steel element designed by the structural engineer is embedded inside the unit. Properties of specific units are in the data sheet.

Loads

A bracket is subject to its self-weight, the weight of the element it supports, snow, wind and — for balconies — imposed load from people. Design values are set by the current code on loads and actions; calculations are carried out by a structural engineer.

  1. Decorative bracket. Anchors are designed for its self-weight and wind; fixings are stainless or galvanised steel, set into the load-bearing substrate rather than the insulation.
  2. Cornice corbel. The cornice weight and snow load on its top surface are added; with a large projection, the load is transferred to steel brackets inside the units.
  3. Balcony corbel. The load path is the slab or a steel frame; the decorative corbel is separated from it by a joint.

The substrate is checked separately: anchors in hollow brick or aerated concrete behave differently from those in cast-in-place concrete and require special fixings or embedded plates. Which shapes of facade brackets and corbels are used most often

The shape of a bracket is determined by the style of the facade and by the element it visually supports. In classical architecture it is a curved profile with volutes; in contemporary architecture, simple geometric bodies without ornament. The main types are summarised in the table.

TypeAppearanceWhere used
Scrolled corbelS-shaped profile with two scrolls, an acanthus leaf or fluting on the front faceBalconies, portals, window pediments on principal floors
Straight bracketStepped or trapezoidal profile without scrollsSill shelves, string courses, simplified neoclassical designs
ModillionSmall, horizontally elongated corbelA row beneath the slab of the crowning cornice
DentilsSmall rectangular projections set in a close rowLower part of cornices and string courses
Triangular corbelPlain prism or plateCanopies and bay windows on contemporary facades
Paired bracketsTwo elements set close together or with a small gapBalcony corners, accent axes, entrance groups

Within one building, two or three sizes of the same shape are normally used: a large one under balconies and the cornice, a smaller one under window pediments and sills. This preserves the unity of the design while keeping the number of moulds reasonable.

How to choose the size and spacing of brackets under a cornice, pediment and sill

The size of a bracket is set from the element it supports: the projection of the corbel should be somewhat less than that of the slab above it, and the height sufficient for the support to look convincing. Exact proportions are set by the architect, but a few rules apply almost always.

  • Two brackets are placed under a window pediment on the axes of the architrave; their width is close to that of the side jamb of the surround. The types of such heads are covered in the article on window pediments.
  • Under a sill slab, brackets are placed symmetrically, set in from the edges, and made noticeably smaller than those under the pediment of the same window.
  • Under a cornice, the spacing of corbels is tied to the axes of windows and piers, and the soffit between them is left plain or filled with coffers. The larger the overhang, the larger and sparser the supports; the relationship is described in the article on cornice projection.
  • As the installation height increases, details are enlarged: fine carving at tenth-floor level cannot be read from the ground, whereas the depth of relief is, on the contrary, increased.

The layout is checked on the facade elevation and in the 3D model, especially at corners, bay windows and breaks in the cornice, where the spacing has to be adjusted.

What to give the manufacturer for producing corbels

To start work, a drawing or sketch of the bracket in three views and elevations showing the installation points are sufficient. The rest is clarified while the working documentation is developed, but the earlier the data listed below are known, the more accurate the estimate.

  1. Overall dimensions of the product: height, projection, width, the design of the side and front faces.
  2. Quantities by size, and whether left-hand and right-hand (mirrored) versions are needed.
  3. Wall material and facade build-up: in-situ concrete, brick, aerated concrete, insulation thickness, type of cladding.
  4. What is above the bracket — a balcony slab, cornice or pediment — and what gap between them is acceptable.
  5. Texture and colour, painting requirements.

If there is no ready profile, it is developed from an analogue or selected from the catalogue. TECHNOFORMA produces serial and bespoke elements together with other decor; related products are described on the page GFRC cornices.

How decorative brackets are fixed to different substrates

A decorative GFRC bracket is fixed mechanically — through embedded parts to the load-bearing substrate or to the facade subframe. Adhesive fixing is acceptable only as an auxiliary measure and only for small elements.

The arrangement depends on the wall construction:

  • In-situ concrete and solid brick. Anchors are set directly into the wall; the product is hung on studs or angles with adjustment for height and projection.
  • Hollow blocks and aerated concrete. Fixings intended for such materials are used, or the fixing point is transferred to the floor slab or a concrete ring beam. Load capacity is confirmed by on-site testing — this is covered in the article on anchor pull-out testing.
  • Ventilated facade. The bracket passes through the insulation layer and is fixed to the wall through a projecting steel element or to reinforced rails of the subframe. The junction of the cladding around the product is worked out as a separate detail.
  • Rendered facade with insulation. Fixing points are provided before the insulation is installed, so as not to damage the finished render or create unnecessary thermal bridges.

Large corbels are lifted by crane or winch and held on a temporary support until the fixings are finally tightened. The joint between the product and the wall is sealed so that water does not reach the embedded parts.

What mistakes are made in designing and installing brackets?

Mistakes with brackets usually stem from the element being regarded as minor and given little attention in the design. As a result, either the appearance or the reliability of the fixing suffers.

  • Uncoordinated scale. Corbels that are too small under a massive balcony look accidental; ones that are too large overload the facade.
  • Spacing not tied to the axes. Modillions that do not line up with windows and pilasters are noticeable at first glance.
  • No gap under the slab. The decorative element starts to act as a support and cracks.
  • Horizontal ledges without a fall. Water and dust collect on the top of the scroll, and streaks appear.
  • Fixing into insulation or cladding. The anchor must reach the load-bearing substrate.
  • Mirrored elements not provided for. An asymmetrical design needs separate moulds for left-hand and right-hand versions.

Most of these problems are removed at the layout stage: it is enough to show each bracket type on the elevation, in section and in the fixing detail. The general approach to the design of balcony slabs and balustrades is set out in the article on balcony decor.

Where facade corbels are appropriate: residential buildings, hotels, private housing

Brackets and corbels are justified where the facade has a projecting slab or shelf that needs visual support. Without such an element they look like an applied detail, so the set and number are chosen to suit the building type and its architecture.

  • Multi-storey residential complexes. The bulk of the products are modillions under the crowning cornice and corbels under the balconies of the principal facades. The run is large, so the shape is simplified to a design that can be read from the ground, and the fixing is standardised to one arrangement on all floors.
  • Hotels. Decor is concentrated on the lower tiers and the entrance group, where it is seen at close range. Finer relief and paired brackets on accent axes are acceptable here.
  • Public and administrative buildings. Large corbels under the cornice and the canopy of the main entrance, designed to be viewed from a square, are more common.
  • Private houses. A few elements are enough: under the balcony above the entrance and under the sill slabs of the main facade. An excessive number of small brackets on a small house fragments the facade.

In the oriental and neoclassical architecture of southern regions, corbels are often combined with arches and carved bands; in this case the profile is coordinated with the rest of the ornament so that the scrolls and plant motifs are executed in one manner. On contemporary facades the same role is played by plain triangular or stepped corbels without ornament.

Looking after brackets in service

Brackets and corbels are projecting details, so they get dirtier and wetter than the plane of the wall. Their condition is checked during scheduled facade inspections, with attention to three things: the joint at the wall, the gap under the slab and the surface of the scrolls.

Sealant that has lost its elasticity is replaced without waiting for water to start getting behind the product. Streaks and dust in the recesses of the relief are washed off with water at low pressure; stiff brushes and abrasives are not suitable for this. If peeling appears on painted elements, the coating is renewed in patches with the shade matched.

Rust marks at a fixing point are a reason to open up the detail and check the fixings, not to paint over the stain. On multi-storey buildings such work is carried out from cradles or by rope access technicians. An example of a facade with a large number of decorative GFRC details is the Al-Qasr hotel complex.

FAQ

What are modillions?

Small brackets arranged in a row under the soffit of the crowning cornice; characteristic of the Corinthian and Composite orders.

Can a decorative corbel support a balcony?

No, if it is a hollow GFRC unit or cladding. The load is carried by the slab or a steel structure designed by a structural engineer.

What are facade brackets made of?

Decorative ones from GFRC, stone and, less often, polymers; load-bearing ones from reinforced concrete or steel.

How are decorative brackets fixed?

With stainless or galvanised steel anchors into the load-bearing substrate through the unit's embedded fixings.

How does a corbel differ from a bracket?

In architectural decor the terms are close: both denote a projecting support. Corbel usually refers to a large element under a balcony or bay window, bracket to a smaller one.

Can decorative brackets be installed on a finished facade?

Yes, provided the load-bearing substrate is accessible. On a rendered or ventilated facade the finish will have to be opened up locally, the fixings set into the wall and the junction reinstated. It is simpler and more reliable to provide fixing points before finishing works begin.

Is a separate mould needed for each bracket size?

Yes, each size and each mirrored version is cast in its own mould. For this reason the number of sizes on a building is usually limited to two or three: this shortens production preparation and makes it easier to replace an element in future.

How much does a GFRC bracket weigh?

The product is hollow, so its mass is substantially lower than that of a stone or reinforced concrete equivalent of the same dimensions. The specific figure depends on the dimensions, wall thickness and embedded parts and is stated in the working documentation; the anchors and lifting method are selected from it.
