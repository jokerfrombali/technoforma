GFRC Columns: Construction and Installation

Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) columns are thin-walled hollow units made of a cement-sand mix reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre. They reproduce the shape and texture of a stone or concrete column but weigh several times less, so they can be hung on load-bearing posts and walls without strengthening the foundations.

The main selection criterion is not only appearance but the structural concept: whether the column will clad an existing support or be a stand-alone decorative element, how it is fixed and how the joints are handled. Wall thickness, breakdown into elements and installation time all depend on this.

The solution suits residential complexes, public buildings, hotels and private houses in classical and neoclassical styles. The place of columns among other decorative elements is described in the overview Architectural Facade Details.

How a Hollow Column Is Built

A GFRC column is a shell, not a solid. A typical design consists of several parts, each cast separately:

  • base — the lower profiled element, sometimes together with a plinth;
  • shaft — a hollow cylinder or polygon, usually split vertically into two half-shells and, along its height, into drums if the column is taller than the mould;
  • capital — a separate ornamented unit fixed to the load-bearing structure;
  • embedded fixings — steel plates, studs or loops cast into the unit during production, to which brackets are attached.

Wall thickness of GFRC facade units is typically around 10–15 mm; large elements have stiffening ribs cast on the inside or a steel frame. Exact values depend on unit size and mix design and are given in the working drawings.

According to TECHNOFORMA Group, the material consists of four components — glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement and water — and is non-combustible (NG class under Russian standards) with high frost resistance. This is why GFRC columns can be used on facades of multi-storey buildings where polymer decor is restricted by fire safety requirements. GFRC column cladding makes it possible to achieve fluting, entasis and a clean smooth surface that are hard to produce in cast-in-place concrete.

Cladding Load-Bearing Supports

A frequent task in new builds and refurbishment is to give an existing load-bearing support an architectural look: a cast-in-place gallery column, a post under a canopy, a balcony support. In this case the GFRC shell is designed around the support.

  1. The actual dimensions and vertical deviations of the support are measured — cast-in-place columns are almost never perfectly plumb.
  2. The inner diameter of the shell is set with a gap that covers the deviations; the gap is also needed for the fixing brackets.
  3. If the support is rectangular but the column is round by design, the outer diameter is determined by the diagonal of the support section plus the gap and wall thickness.
  4. The top of the shell is not brought tight against the slab, or a joint is left: the cladding must not take load from the building.

This approach allows supports to be clad without altering the structure. Strength remains with the reinforced concrete, while the decorative shell is responsible only for form and surface protection.

Dimensions

GFRC columns are made from bespoke moulds, so strictly speaking there is no standard size range: diameter and height come from the design. Limits are set by technology and logistics.

Proportions are set by the architecture: column height is roughly 7–10 lower diameters depending on the order, and the upper diameter is smaller than the lower because of entasis. When preparing working drawings, designers coordinate element sizes with fixing centres, joint positions and the permissible weight of a single unit for manual or crane installation.

Installation

A GFRC column is installed from the bottom up, with each element fixed to the load-bearing substrate independently.

  1. Setting out. Column axes and bottom levels are marked, and the substrate for the base is checked.
  2. Base. Installed on a levelled substrate, adjusted horizontally and fixed.
  3. Shaft. The half-shells are brought together around the support and fixed to it with steel brackets via the embedded fixings. Stainless or galvanised steel is used for fixings. Tall shafts are assembled drum by drum, checking each for plumb.
  4. Capital. Fixed to the structure with its own brackets and does not rest its full weight on the thin shaft wall.
  5. Joints. Vertical joints between half-shells and horizontal joints between drums are filled with cement-based repair compound or elastic sealant, as per the design.

In some projects the inner cavity is filled with grout to improve the impact resistance of the lower part of the column; the decision is made by the designer, taking weight and fixing into account. On TECHNOFORMA Group projects installation is carried out by the in-house installation division of more than 100 specialists, and fixing details are developed by the in-house design department.

Finishing

The final finish determines whether the column will look like stone, render or modern concrete.

  • Smooth surface for painting. The most common option: the unit is sanded at the joints and painted with a facade paint compatible with a cementitious substrate (vapour-permeable, alkali-resistant).
  • Coloured GFRC. Pigment is added to the mix, so the colour runs through the whole face layer; no painting is needed and minor chips are less visible.
  • Stone-look texture. Imitation of sandstone or limestone through the mould, aggregate and subsequent surface treatment.
  • Water-repellent treatment. Impregnation reduces water absorption and soiling, especially on the lower part of the column near the porch.

After filling, joints are colour-matched to the main surface. For painted columns, periodic renewal of the coating should be included in the maintenance schedule; intervals depend on the paint system and climate.

What input data is needed to design GFRC columns

To develop GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) columns, the manufacturer needs dimensioned architectural drawings and information about the load-bearing substrate to which the shell will be fixed. The more complete the input data, the fewer revisions at the working documentation stage and the more accurate the preliminary cost estimate.

The minimum set usually looks like this:

  • elevations and sections showing the bottom and top levels of the columns, their spacing and number;
  • a profile drawing of the base and capital, an indication of the order or a reference to an analogue;
  • details of the structural support: material, cross-section, an as-built survey showing actual deviations;
  • texture and colour requirements, and a reference sample if the columns must match the rest of the cladding;
  • installation conditions: availability of scaffolding or a crane, the weight limit for a single element, sequencing with other works.

These data are used to issue drawings of the products and fixing details — a package close in content to facade shop drawings. It records the division of the shaft, the position of embedded parts, the type of brackets and the assembly sequence. If the building has pilasters and half-columns in addition to free-standing columns, their profiles and joints are coordinated within one package so that bases and capitals line up by level.

How GFRC columns differ from stone, cast in-situ and polymer ones

A GFRC shell occupies an intermediate position: it is non-combustible and durable, like stone and concrete, yet its mass is closer to that of lightweight materials. The choice depends on building height, budget, the required relief and whether there is a structural support inside.

In practice the options are combined. The structural function is performed by in-situ concrete or steel, the architectural form is provided by the shell, and in the plinth and entrance zone, where impacts are possible, the lower part is sometimes made of stone. Where GFRC columns are used: solutions by building type

GFRC columns are used where a classical form is needed without increasing the load on the frame: at entrance groups, galleries, upper-floor loggias, porticoes and rotundas. The set of requirements changes with the purpose of the building.

  • Residential complexes. Columns usually stand on the podium and at the entrances, and on the upper tiers they frame loggias and terraces. Repeatability of elements and low weight matter here: one mould serves dozens of products, and the load on cantilevered slabs stays within the design value.
  • Hotels and public buildings. Tall porticoes rising two or three storeys are more common. The shaft is divided into sections, and the joints are hidden in bands and fluting; lighting is detailed separately.
  • Business centres. Pylons and columns of simplified design are in demand — rectangular in section, with a smooth or stone-like textured surface.
  • Private houses. Low porch and terrace columns are often assembled from one-piece shafts without a vertical joint, if there is no support inside or it can be passed over from above.

In refurbishment, shells make it possible to restore a lost order to a building without loading old walls and foundations. In this case the scope of work begins with a survey of the substrate, the results of which are used to choose the fixing points.

How are the junctions of a column with the plinth, floor and slab resolved?

The durability of a column is determined not so much by the products themselves as by the details where the shell meets other structures. There are three such places: the bottom at the floor or plinth, the top under the slab or entablature, and crossings with building services.

At the bottom, the base stands in the zone of splashes, meltwater and de-icing agents. Waterproofing and a fall of the paving away from the column are therefore provided beneath it, and the joint between the base and the floor is filled with an elastic sealant rather than a rigid mortar. On porches and terraces the base is often set on a stone plinth block, which tolerates wetting and mechanical impact better.

At the top, a gap is left between the capital and the slab, closed with an elastic material or a shadow joint. It accommodates slab deflection and the thermal movement of the shell itself. The size of the gap is set by the designer from the calculated movements of the structure.

The internal cavity must remain dry. If the shaft is not filled with mortar, openings are provided in the lower part to drain incidental moisture and ventilate the cavity. Where a rainwater pipe or a lighting cable runs through the cavity, penetrations and inspection hatches are formed in the shell in advance: cutting a finished product on site is undesirable.

Typical mistakes in manufacturing and installing GFRC columns

Most defects in GFRC columns arise not from the material but from decisions taken without a design or changed on site. Below are the mistakes encountered most often.

  • Designing to the theoretical dimensions of the support. Without an as-built survey the half-shells may fail to meet or may foul a projection of the concrete.
  • Rigid filling of all joints. When vertical and horizontal joints are filled with repair mortar only, thermal movement leads to hairline cracks along the joint line.
  • Resting the capital on the shaft. The thin wall is not designed for the weight of the element above; each product must have its own fixing.
  • Fixings made of ordinary black steel. Corrosion eventually shows as rust streaks and reduces the load capacity of the fixing.
  • Trimming products on site. Cutting exposes the structure of the material and loses the embedded part; dimensions are better corrected at the factory.
  • Painting over a damp or unprimed substrate. The coating peels within the first seasons, especially on the base.

A separate risk is overloading during unloading and storage. Half-shells are transported and stored in the position specified by the manufacturer, on spacers; tilting them over an edge causes chips that then have to be repaired.

What to check when accepting installed columns

Acceptance of columns is conveniently carried out against a short checklist, comparing the result with the working drawings and the approved sample. Concealed works — fixing to the support and corrosion protection — are presented before the cavity is closed and the joints are filled.

  1. Verticality of the shaft and alignment of the columns in a row, matching levels of bases and capitals.
  2. Presence and type of fixings at every point as designed, with signed records of concealed works.
  3. Width and filling of joints, absence of steps between half-shells and shaft sections.
  4. Conformity of colour and texture to the reference sample in daylight.
  5. Absence of chips, cracks and traces of repair on the face.
  6. The movement gap at the top and the sealing of the joint at the base.

Permissible deviations are taken from the design documentation and the manufacturer's technical specifications. The general handover procedure is described in the article on acceptance of facade works.

Maintenance and repair of the column shell

GFRC columns do not require special maintenance, but they do need periodic inspection, like the facade as a whole. It is usually combined with the spring inspection of the building: the condition of the joints, the coating and the lower part, which gets dirtiest and wettest, is checked.

The surface is washed with water and mild neutral agents; abrasives and aggressive acidic compounds damage the face layer and the paint. The sealant in the joints loses elasticity over time and is renewed locally without affecting the products. Painted columns are repainted as the coating fades, after cleaning the substrate.

Local damage — a chip on the edge of the base, a scratch from a trolley, a hairline crack — is made good with repair compounds matched in colour and texture. The procedure is covered in the article on how chips and cracks are repaired. If a large part of an element is damaged, it is usually simpler to replace it: the mould is kept at the factory, and independent fixing allows one half-shell to be removed without dismantling the whole column.

FAQ

What are GFRC columns?

They are hollow columns made of glass fibre reinforced concrete — a cement-sand composite reinforced with glass fibre. They are lighter than stone and concrete columns and are fixed to the load-bearing substrate on brackets.

Can existing columns be clad with GFRC?

Yes, this is one of the material's main uses. The shell is designed to the actual dimensions of the support with an installation gap.

Does a GFRC column carry load?

No, it is cladding or a decorative element. The load is taken by a reinforced concrete or steel support inside.

Do GFRC columns need painting?

Not necessarily: units can be produced in colour from the outset. Painting is used when an exact design colour is required or to even out shades after joint filling.

Are GFRC columns combustible?

According to the manufacturer, the material is non-combustible (NG class under Russian standards).

How much does a GFRC column weigh?

The weight depends on the diameter, height, wall thickness and the presence of ribs or a frame, so the exact figure comes only from the working documentation. As a guide, the mass per square metre of a thin-walled shell is usually several times lower than that of a solid concrete or stone element of the same size.

Can GFRC columns be installed in winter?

Mechanical fixing of the shell to the support is almost independent of temperature. The restrictions apply to wet processes: filling joints with mortar, sealing and painting are carried out at the temperature specified by the material manufacturer or postponed until the warm season.

Can a rainwater pipe or lighting cable be run inside the column?

Yes, the cavity of the shell allows a rainwater pipe or cable to be concealed. This is provided for in the design in advance: penetrations and inspection hatches are formed in the products, and the services are fixed to the structural support, not to the wall of the shell.
