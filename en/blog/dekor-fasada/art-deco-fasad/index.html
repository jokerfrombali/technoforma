An Art Deco facade is the architecture of the 1920s–1930s and its contemporary interpretations: vertical rhythms, stepped silhouettes, geometric ornament, stylised sunbursts, fans and zigzags, and noble materials contrasting smooth and relief surfaces. The style combines classical symmetry with an industrial aesthetic and luxury.

The main criterion for choosing decor is precise geometry and crisp arrises: Art Deco does not forgive blurred profiles or careless joints. The style is chosen for premium residential complexes, hotels, office and public buildings that need an expressive, recognisable image. Other styles and elements are in the section Architectural facade decor.

Features of the style

Art Deco took shape after the 1925 International Exhibition of Decorative Arts in Paris and became the international style of the interwar years — from New York skyscrapers to the resort buildings of Miami and apartment blocks in Europe. In Soviet architecture, similar features appear in buildings of the late 1920s and 1930s.

Features of the style on a facade:

  • verticality — piers, lesenes and narrow vertical windows draw the building upwards;
  • a stepped silhouette — setbacks at the top, 'ziggurats', tower crowns;
  • symmetry within a dynamic, 'soaring' composition;
  • geometry — straight lines, sharp angles, arcs, circle segments;
  • contrasting textures — smooth stone next to reeded, fluted and relief inserts;
  • metal — brass, bronze, blackened steel in railings, doors, light fittings;
  • a restrained palette — light stone, dark accents, gold.

Unlike classicism, Art Deco does not follow a strict order — it borrows its elements and reworks them into geometric forms.

Art Deco architecture uses relief not to imitate carving but to create rhythm and play of light. An Art Deco building often gets an accent entrance with a tall portal, relief panels and metal details, while the rest of the facade stays relatively concise.

Contemporary interpretations

In contemporary architecture Art Deco is returning as an alternative to neoclassicism for premium projects. Contemporary interpretations differ from historical examples:

  • More glazing — panoramic windows between vertical piers keep the rhythm of the style.
  • Simplified ornament — instead of complex bas-reliefs, reeded panels, geometric inserts and relief fins are used.
  • Large scale — elements are designed for 15–30-storey buildings, so verticals span several floors.
  • Lighting — architectural lighting emphasises the verticals and the stepped crown.
  • New materials — glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) allows large relief panels with sharp arrises, and metal elements replace brass with anodised aluminium or painted steel.

Restraint matters: contemporary Art Deco easily turns into a set of decorative clichés if ornament is applied without reference to the facade composition.

In residential complexes Art Deco works especially well on corner sections and tower crowns: a lit stepped silhouette becomes a local landmark. The standard sections can then be handled with restraint — a rhythm of piers and reeded inserts at the entrances.

Materials

Historically Art Deco used cladding stone (limestone, travertine, marble, granite), terracotta, render, bronze and glass. Today the choice is:

  • GFRC — for relief panels, fluted piers, stepped cornices and portals. The material reproduces sharp arrises and fine geometry, and products can be large and thin-walled. According to TECHNOFORMA, GFRC is rated non-combustible (Russian class NG).
  • Natural stone — travertine, marble and limestone for smooth surfaces and entrances, granite for the plinth. TECHNOFORMA supplies stone from India, Turkey, China and Iran; the range includes marble, travertine, limestone, granite, onyx and other types.
  • Metal — for railings, canopies and curtain-wall frames in dark and 'gold' finishes.
  • Porcelain stoneware — for plain areas where the main accent is made by decorative elements.

Combining smooth light stone with reeded GFRC panels in the same tone is one of the most recognisable techniques of the style.

Examples

Classic examples of the style are the New York skyscrapers of the 1930s (the Chrysler Building, the Empire State Building), the Art Deco district of Miami Beach and many interwar buildings in Europe. In Moscow, Art Deco features can be seen in a number of residential and public buildings of the 1930s and in the design of metro stations of that time.

In contemporary projects the style appears in:

  • premium residential complexes with a tall podium and vertical piers;
  • hotels, where Art Deco supports a 'golden age' atmosphere;
  • office centres using reeded panels and stepped crowns;
  • restaurant and retail frontages with accent portals.

Successful examples share strict geometry, repetition of elements and well-executed arrises: Art Deco loses its expressiveness with uneven joints and blurred relief.

If you are planning a facade in this style, start with the silhouette and vertical rhythm, then define 2–3 ornamental motifs and where they will repeat: the portal, the parapet, the band above the podium. The remaining surfaces are best left smooth.

How to design an Art Deco facade: the sequence of work

An Art Deco facade is designed from the large scale down to the small: first the silhouette and vertical divisions, then the panel module, and only after that the ornament. The reverse order, where decorative inserts are chosen for a finished building envelope, almost always produces an arbitrary result.

  1. Silhouette. Decide where the building receives setbacks, tower tops and accent verticals; check them in street-level views, not only on the orthogonal elevation.
  2. Vertical rhythm. Set the spacing of the piers in relation to the window axes and the structural grid; divisions are covered in more detail in the article on facade composition.
  3. Cladding module. Choose panel sizes and joint positions so that the joints coincide with the edges of piers, string courses and relief fields.
  4. Ornament. Select two or three motifs and assign them fixed locations: the portal, the parapet, the piers between windows, the spandrel inserts.
  5. Samples. Produce a full-size fragment of the relief and approve the colour, texture and profile depth in daylight.
  6. Working documentation. Develop the panel layout, the fixing details and the schedule of elements.

On projects with a large number of repeating elements it makes sense to rationalise the range as early as possible. The fewer unique moulds there are, the more predictable the production schedule and the easier it is to replace a damaged element in the future.

What relief depth and proportions work on a facade?

Art Deco relief is read through shadow, so its depth is set according to the distance from which the facade is viewed. A finely fluted surface that looks expressive on a hand-held sample merges into a flat tone at tenth-floor level.

Facade zoneViewing distanceWhat is usually specified
Entrance, podiuma few metresfine ornament, slender flutes, metal details
Middle floorstens of metresfluting at a wide pitch, piers with a marked projection, spandrel inserts
Top of the buildinga hundred metres and morelarge setbacks, enlarged fans and chevrons, silhouette elements

As a rule, the fluting pitch on the upper floors is made noticeably larger than at the entrance, and the projection of the piers from the wall plane is increased. The actual values depend on the height of the building, its orientation and the width of the street, so they are checked on a 3D model and on a full-size sample.

The second relationship is the ratio of pier width to the width of the wall strip and the window. Piers that are too wide turn the style into heavy neoclassicism, while piers that are too narrow are lost against the glazing. A practical approach is to set two sizes of vertical: main piers on the section axes and thinner pilaster strips between the windows.

Colour, texture and metal: how to build the palette

An Art Deco palette is built on two or three tones: a main light or dark mass, a contrasting plinth and metal accents. More than three materials on one facade usually fragment the composition.

  • Light range — beige and cream tones close to limestone and travertine; it shows shadow on the relief well.
  • Dark range — graphite, dark green, deep brown; it calls for a deeper profile, since shadow is less visible on a dark surface.
  • Plinth and portal — polished or honed stone of dense varieties that tolerates soiling and regular washing.
  • Metal — brass and bronze shades in railings, light fittings and the framing of glazed screens; used sparingly.

For GFRC panels the shade is obtained by integral pigmentation or by a coating; the difference between the methods is explained in the article on the colour of GFRC. Smooth surfaces at the plinth and the entrance are often made of natural stone, for example travertine for the facade combined with fluted panels of the same tone.

How relief panels and piers are fixed

Art Deco relief elements are installed as the cladding of a ventilated facade: an element with cast-in fixings is hung on a subframe secured to the load-bearing wall or the floor slabs. Adhesive fixing is not used for large panels.

GFRC facade panels for such work are usually made thin-walled, with stiffening ribs or a steel frame on the reverse side. This reduces the load on the building structure and allows storey-height elements without intermediate horizontal joints, which matters for an unbroken vertical line. Piers are assembled from U-shaped or L-shaped shells that enclose the subframe on three or two sides.

Points to watch in the details:

  • adjustment in three directions — without it the straight line of the edges cannot be held over the full height of the building;
  • joint width — set by the design with thermal movement in mind and kept the same across the whole facade;
  • water run-off — the horizontal ledges of setbacks and string courses are given a fall and a drip;
  • relief junctions — the pattern must pass from panel to panel without an offset, so the moulds are made from a common master model.

Art Deco lighting: what to allow for in advance

Art Deco is one of the few styles in which the evening image of a building is designed on equal terms with the daytime one. Light emphasises the verticals and the stepped top, so luminaire positions and cable routes are fixed before the element drawings are issued.

Panels and piers are given recesses, concealed ledges and cable openings, while the subframe provides fixing points for the fittings and access for maintenance. Grazing light from below strengthens the fluting but shows joint irregularities just as clearly, so the requirements for installation accuracy on illuminated areas are higher. The general principles are set out in the article on architectural facade lighting.

Which buildings suit Art Deco and what affects the budget

The style is justified where a building has to be recognisable and where the budget allows the quality of the edges to be maintained over the full height. On ordinary developments with tight cost limits it is usually applied selectively: at the entrances, the corner sections and the top.

  • Residential complexes — the decoration is concentrated on the podium and the upper floors, while the typical floors are handled with repeating piers and spandrel inserts.
  • Hotels — the emphasis is on the portal, the canopy and the evening lighting; the finish at the entrance must withstand intensive use.
  • Office buildings — a large vertical rhythm with a high proportion of glazing and ornament reduced to a minimum.
  • Public buildings — a symmetrical composition with a pronounced central volume and a formal staircase.

Cost is affected most by the number of sizes and moulds, the depth and complexity of the relief, the share of unique elements, the height of the building and the installation method. Repetition is the main source of savings: one mould used dozens of times costs noticeably less per element than a set of one-off moulds. It therefore makes sense to prepare the estimate at sketch stage with a panel layout, while elements of similar size can still be merged without affecting the appearance.

Common mistakes in Art Deco facades

Most failures are linked not to the material but to the order in which decisions are taken. The following mistakes occur most often.

  • Ornament without a system: motifs of different character on one facade, unrelated to axes and joints.
  • Relief on the upper floors that is too fine to be read from the ground.
  • Joints crossing the pattern at random points because the layout was produced after the ornament had been approved.
  • Flat horizontal ledges without a fall, which collect streaks and dirt bands.
  • A sample approved indoors under artificial light, so the colour looks different on the facade.
  • Too many unique elements, which increases lead times and tooling costs.

Complex geometry with a varying relief is easier to check in a digital model before the moulds are started; the possibilities of the technology are described in the article on parametric 3D panels.

FAQ

How does Art Deco differ from neoclassicism?

Neoclassicism relies on the order and classical proportions; Art Deco on geometry, vertical rhythms, stepped forms and stylised ornament.

What elements does an Art Deco facade need?

Vertical piers, reeded or relief panels, an accent portal, a stepped crown, metal details.

What is Art Deco facade decor made of?

GFRC, natural stone, metal. GFRC is convenient for relief panels with sharp arrises.

Is Art Deco suitable for a residential complex?

Yes, especially for premium projects with a tall podium and an expressive entrance.

Can an Art Deco relief be made to an architect's sketch?

Yes, a master model, mould and sample for approval are made from the sketch.

What relief depth do Art Deco panels need?

There is no single value: the depth is chosen according to the height of installation and the viewing distance. A fine profile is sufficient at the entrance, while the relief is enlarged on the upper floors. The final parameters are approved on a full-size sample in daylight.

Can Art Deco be combined with a ventilated facade?

Yes. Relief panels and piers are hung on the subframe as ordinary ventilated facade cladding, with insulation and an air gap behind them. It is important to provide adjustable fixings and a uniform joint width.

When should the lighting be resolved in the project?

Before the element drawings are issued. Recesses, cable openings and luminaire fixing points are built into the panel mould, and adding them later without rework is difficult.
