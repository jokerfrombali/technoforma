Window Decor on the Facade: The Full Set of Elements

Window decor on a facade is a set of elements around the opening: a window surround along the outline, a pediment or cornice above the window, a sill slab with a drip, reveals, and sometimes a keystone, brackets and apron panels. Together they protect the window junctions from water and set the character of the facade — from strict classicism to a concise contemporary frame.

The main selection criterion is the balance of function and scale: elements must shed water, avoid thermal bridges and match the size of the opening and the number of storeys. For multi-storey housing and public buildings the decor is usually made of glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) and stone; for private houses lightweight materials are also acceptable. An overview of all types is in the section Architectural facade decor.

Window surround, pediment, sill

The three main window framing elements solve different tasks.

ElementPositionFunction
Window surroundAlong the outline of the opening (sides and top)Frames the window, covers the installation joint, sets the style
Pediment (window hood)Above the window, over the surroundSheds water from the head of the opening, creates an accent; can be straight, triangular or segmental
External sillBelow the windowSheds water from the wall, protects the bottom junction
KeystoneAt the centre of the top of the surround or archDecorative accent, historically the locking element of an arch
BracketsUnder the sill or pedimentVisual support for projecting elements

On classical facades pediments alternate by floor: triangular on the principal floor, straight or segmental on the others. In contemporary projects the surround may become a continuous GFRC frame, a 'portal' projecting from the wall plane.

Reveals

Reveals are the inner side and top surfaces of the opening between the window and the facade plane. Facade reveals are often underestimated, although water enters the wall through them and a thermal bridge runs along them.

Requirements for external reveals:

  • cover the insulation and the window installation joint without leaving gaps;
  • the head reveal has a drip or slope so that water does not run onto the frame;
  • the material is compatible with the rest of the cladding in thermal movement;
  • joints with the window are sealed allowing for joint movement.

When a facade is clad in GFRC, reveals are often made of the same material — L- or U-shaped elements that combine surround and reveal in one product. This reduces the number of joints and simplifies installation. In ventilated rainscreen systems reveals are made of metal or porcelain stoneware, with the decorative surround fixed on top.

Composition across the facade

Window decor only works as a system. Isolated surrounds on one floor with no link to bands and cornices look random. Design takes into account:

  • vertical axes — windows and their surrounds line up in rows, and the decor emphasises the axes;
  • floor hierarchy — lower floors and the principal floor get richer decor, upper floors more restrained decor;
  • horizontal links — sill slabs can join into a continuous band, pediments can echo the floor cornice;
  • scale — surround width and pediment projection are proportioned to the opening size and the distance from which the facade is seen;
  • repetition — the more identical elements, the lower the cost per piece, since one mould serves the whole series.

It is best to check the composition on a 3D visualisation of the whole facade rather than on a drawing of one window: that shows whether the facade is overloaded and whether elements read on upper floors. The decor colour relative to the wall is chosen there too: a contrasting frame emphasises the window rhythm, tone-on-tone makes the facade calmer.

Materials

Exterior window decor is made of several materials:

  • GFRC. Non-combustible (Russian class NG, according to TECHNOFORMA), frost-resistant, reproduces complex profiles, allows large products with an integrated reveal and drip. The main option for multi-storey buildings.
  • Natural stone. Sill slabs, surrounds and pediments in granite, limestone, travertine. Durable, but heavy and expensive to work into complex shapes.
  • Polyurethane and polymer concrete. Light, installed with adhesive, suitable for private houses; on multi-storey buildings restricted by fire safety requirements.
  • Coated expanded polystyrene. The cheapest option for rendered facades of private houses; vulnerable to impact and combustible.
  • Metal. For contemporary facades — portal frames and drips in painted steel or aluminium.

Specific properties — strength, water absorption, frost resistance — depend on the mix design and are stated in the product data sheet.

Junction details

A junction is where the decor meets the window and the wall. Mistakes here lead to leaks, freezing and deterioration of elements.

What to look out for:

  • Sill. Slope away from the window, tucked under the frame or the window drip, end upstands or caps, a drip on the bottom edge, projection beyond the wall plane.
  • Pediment. A sloped top with protection against water running behind the product; if necessary, a metal flashing on top.
  • Insulation. On insulated facades the decor must not interrupt the insulation layer; fixing goes through the insulation on thermally broken anchors or to the substructure.
  • Joints. Between window and decor — an elastic sealant designed for movement, not rigid mortar.

Junctions are worked out in the working drawings before the products are made: they determine the profile, dimensions and cast-in fixings of each element.

The typical site sequence: install the windows and installation joint, insulate the facade, install sill slabs and reveals, then surrounds and pediments, after which joints are sealed. If decor is fitted to a finished facade, the window junction is first opened up and checked: there is no point covering an already leaking joint with decor.

Windows on upper floors and corner openings, where wind load is higher, need special attention. Fixing is calculated separately for them, and the projection of pediments and sills is sometimes reduced to cut wind exposure and anchor loads.

Which window surround suits the style of the facade?

The type of surround is determined by the style of the building: in classical work the window receives a full set of profiled elements, in contemporary architecture a plain frame or simply an expressive reveal. Mixing devices on one facade almost always looks like a mistake.

StyleComposition of the surroundCharacter of the profileFeatures
ClassicalArchitrave, window head moulding on brackets, sill slab, keystoneComplex, with several mouldingsHierarchy by floor, richer on the principal floor
NeoclassicalArchitrave, straight head moulding or shelf, sillSimplified, large-scaleOne type for most of the facade, serial production
Art DecoVertical ribs, stepped frames, panels between windowsGeometric, steppedWindows are combined into vertical strips
ContemporaryPortal frame, deep reveal, finsSmooth, without mouldingsPlay of depth and shadow, concealed flashings
OrientalPointed and ogee arched surrounds, ornamental insertsFlat relief, ornamentThe arch is often decorative over a rectangular window

Within one building two or three types of surround are acceptable, differing in richness but built on a single profile. Arched heads and flat arches are emphasised with a keystone; on rectangular openings it is used less often and only in classical schemes.

The richness of the decor changes with height. The lower floors are viewed at close range, so a complex profile and brackets under the sill slab are appropriate here; from the tenth floor the same details are not legible, and the surround is simplified to a frame with an increased projection. On courtyard and end elevations the same profile is kept in a reduced set — usually without head mouldings.

Sizes of the architrave, head moulding and sill slab: guidelines

Arched, bay and panoramic windows: what is specific about their treatment

Non-standard openings require separate units and separate moulds, so their number should be limited at the design stage. Every new arch radius means new tooling.

  • Arched windows. The archivolt is divided into segments of equal length, and the joint on the axis is covered by a keystone element. Radii are standardised: two or three for the whole project instead of an individual one for each window.
  • Bay windows. The surround turns with the wall; corner elements are cast in one piece to avoid a joint on the arris. A bay needs its own flashing or cornice beneath it.
  • Panoramic and double-height glazing. Instead of an architrave, a frame two storeys high is used with a panel or transom at floor slab level; there may be no sill as a separate part — a threshold flashing replaces it.
  • Windows with a Juliet balcony. The surround is coordinated with the guard: the embedded parts for the railing are fixed into the wall, not into the decor.
  • Paired windows. The narrow pier between them is covered by a shared half-column or pilaster, with a common head moulding above.

On serial residential buildings a simple rule pays off: one surround for one standard opening size. The fewer the window sizes, the fewer the moulds and make-up pieces and the more even the facade looks.

How is window decor fixed on different facade systems?

Window decor is fixed to the load-bearing wall or to the subframe, not to the window frame and not to the insulation. The specific scheme depends on the construction of the facade.

Facade systemFixing of the surroundRevealWhat to watch
Ventilated facadeTo the subframe rails or on separate bracketsA GFRC or metal element forming part of the systemFire-protection casing around the perimeter of the opening, ventilation gap
Rendered insulation systemLight elements — on the reinforcing layer; heavy ones — anchors through the insulationInsulated, rendered or in GFRCLoad must not be transferred to the insulation
Brick or in-situ concrete wall without insulationAnchors directly into the wallRender or a decor elementQuality of the substrate, waterproofing of the top face
Refurbishment of an existing facadeAccording to survey resultsTo suit site conditionsCondition of lintels and old render

In a ventilated facade the surround of the opening is at the same time an element of fire protection: the perimeter of the window is closed so that fire cannot enter the air gap. In this case the decorative architrave is placed over a non-combustible casing, and the material of the decor itself must be non-combustible — GFRC meets this requirement. The construction of such details is described in the article on junctions of a ventilated facade with windows.

The joint between the decor and the window unit is not made rigid. A gap is left between the frame and the element and filled with sealing tape and a UV-resistant sealant: the window and the wall respond differently to temperature, and the detail must accommodate these movements.

What to prepare for a quotation for window surrounds?

A quotation requires a schedule of openings: how many windows of each size there are and on which floors they are located. It is used to determine the number of unit types, moulds and the total volume.

  1. Elevations with window marks and a schedule of openings with clear dimensions.
  2. A wall section: material, insulation thickness, position of the window within the wall depth.
  3. The desired composition of the surround by floor: where an architrave with a head moulding, where a frame only.
  4. Profiles or references, colour and texture.
  5. The type of facade system and the subframe spacing, if it is a ventilated facade.
  6. Information on flashings, insect screens, external blinds and air-conditioning units that will be next to the decor.

Measuring the openings after the windows are installed is essential: deviations of 10–20 mm in width occur constantly and are absorbed by gaps and make-up pieces. Units are manufactured with an installation tolerance rather than exactly to the size of the opening.

Typical defects of window decor and how to avoid them

Defects in window surrounds almost always appear in the first two or three seasons and are related to water. The most common are streaks beneath the ends of the sill, cracks at the joint between the architrave and the wall, and darkening of the top of the head moulding.

  • Streaks at the sides of the sill — there are no end upstands or the projection is too small; water runs onto the wall from the ends of the slab.
  • A crack around the architrave — the joint is filled with rigid mortar rather than an elastic sealant.
  • Darkening and moss on the head moulding — the top face has no fall and no flashing.
  • Chips on the lower floors — elements within reach without a margin of strength; stone or reinforced units are more appropriate here.
  • Variation in tone — elements from different batches without matching; solved by a single batch and a control sample.

How to arrange the lower window detail correctly is explained in the article on external sills and flashings. If the facade has string courses and bands in addition to surrounds, their profile is conveniently chosen from one series — it is presented on the page GFRC mouldings, string courses and bands.

In service it is enough to inspect window decor once a year, together with a check of the flashings and sealant. Dirt beneath sills is washed off with water and a soft brush; the sealant in the joints loses elasticity over time, and renewing it costs incomparably less than repairing a soaked wall.

FAQ

What exterior window decor lasts longest?

Natural stone and GFRC. Both withstand frost and precipitation; GFRC is lighter and allows complex profiles.

What is the difference between a window pediment and a surround?

A surround frames the opening along its outline; a pediment is a projecting element above the window that sheds water and creates an accent.

What are facade reveals made of?

GFRC, metal, porcelain stoneware, render systems. With GFRC cladding the reveal is often made as one product with the surround.

Can window decor be installed on a finished facade?

Yes, but the substrate must be checked and fixing through the insulation, if any, must be planned, as well as the junctions with the window.

Does an external sill need a drip?

Yes, it breaks the flow of water off the product and stops it running down the wall.

How wide should an architrave be?

The guideline is one sixth to one fifth of the opening width; for a standard window this is usually 120–250 mm. On upper floors and large buildings the profile is enlarged; on narrow piers it is reduced so that a visible plane of wall remains between adjacent architraves.

Do windows need surrounds on all elevations of the building?

Sills with flashings are needed on all windows, because they protect the wall. Architraves and head mouldings on courtyard and end elevations can be simplified while keeping the same profile: the elevations should read as parts of one building.

Can window decor be replaced without replacing the windows?

Yes. The old elements are removed, the installation joint and reveals are checked, waterproofing is reinstated where necessary, and the new surround is then installed with mechanical fixing. Measurements are taken from the actual openings.
