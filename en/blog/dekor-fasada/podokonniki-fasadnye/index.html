External Window Sills and Drips in Stone and GFRC

External window sills in stone and glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) are slabs below the window that shed rain and meltwater away from the wall and protect the bottom junction of the window frame. Unlike steel drip flashings, stone and concrete sills also act as an architectural element: they cast a shadow under the window, continue the line of a band and frame the opening.

The main selection criterion is water shedding: the right slope, a drip, projection beyond the wall plane and a sealed junction with the frame. The material is determined by the facade style and climate: dense stone and GFRC suit any region, while porous stones need a frost-resistance check. Such sills are used on both multi-storey buildings and private houses. Other elements are in the section Architectural facade decor.

Functions

An external sill performs several functions:

  • Shedding water. Water running down the glass and frame collects on the sill and is carried beyond the facade plane, without reaching the wall or the installation joint.
  • Protecting the junction. The bottom of the window opening is the most leak-prone spot; the sill covers the installation joint and the insulation.
  • Architectural role. The sill slab creates a horizontal shadow, emphasises the opening and can link windows into a band.
  • Keeping the facade clean. With enough projection and a drip, dust and dirt from the frame do not leave streaks on the wall.

A drip flashing is a narrower concept: a thin element (usually galvanised or painted steel, or aluminium) that sheds water from under the frame. Facades with decorative stone or GFRC sills often use both a window flashing (under the frame) and a sill slab (below it): the flashing protects the joint, the slab protects the wall. In some solutions the GFRC slab is designed to act as the flashing itself.

Slope and drip

Slope and drip are the two details without which an external sill does not work.

Slope. The top surface of the sill must slope outward from the window so that water does not stand or run under the frame. It is usually set within a few degrees; the specific value is determined by the design and the recommendations of the window and product manufacturers. The slope can be formed in the product itself (bevelled top) or by installing the slab at an angle.

Drip. A groove or ridge on the underside of the sill, a few centimetres from the front edge. Water running along the underside reaches the drip and falls away from the product in drops instead of running back to the wall. Without a drip, dark streaks appear on the facade below the windows.

Installation

An external sill is installed after the window and, as a rule, after the facade is insulated.

  1. Substrate preparation. Check the window installation joint and waterproofing; if necessary, apply vapour-barrier and waterproofing tapes.
  2. Support. Heavy stone and concrete slabs are set on brackets, anchors or mortar on the load-bearing structure, not on the insulation.
  3. Setting to slope. The slab is set with an outward slope and the required projection, tucked under the frame or the window flashing.
  4. Fixing. Mechanical fixing, with adhesive or mortar as a supplement if needed.
  5. Sealing. The joint with the frame and reveals is filled with an elastic sealant designed for joint movement.

On ventilated rainscreen facades the sill is fixed to the substructure or to separate brackets, and the air gap beneath it must not be completely closed — this is a design decision agreed with the rainscreen contractor. TECHNOFORMA takes measurements with its own surveyors and carries out installation with its own installation team.

Junction details

Junctions are where the sill meets the window, the reveals and the wall. Typical details worked out in the working drawings:

  • Frame junction. The sill or flashing is tucked under the frame or the window's sill adaptor profile; the joint is sealed and water must not enter the installation joint.
  • Reveal junction. The sill ends have upstands or fit into a groove in the reveal; the vertical joint between reveal and slab is sealed.
  • Insulation junction. The insulation below the sill is protected from wetting, and the thermal bridge through the fixing is minimised with a thermal break.
  • Rainscreen junction. The sill meets the cladding without blocking ventilation of the gap or letting water behind the cladding.
  • Multi-piece sills. For a wide opening the slab is assembled from several pieces; joints are sealed and laid to fall.

A poorly executed junction is the main cause of leaks under windows, even with a high-quality sill.

External window sill dimensions: projection, thickness, length

The dimensions of a sill slab are set by three parameters: the depth of the opening including insulation, the required projection beyond the cladding plane, and the permissible mass of the unit per support point. There is no universal size: slabs are made for specific openings from site measurements.

ParameterUsual practiceWhat it depends on
Projection beyond the wall planeas a rule 30–50 mm, more on decorative profilesfacade design, presence of a string course or brackets under the window
Stone slab thicknessusually 30–40 mm, more for long and cantilevered slabsstone type, span between supports, projection
Section of a GFRC unithollow profile with a visible height set by the architectprofile of the string course or architrave the sill joins
Length of one elementfor an opening of standard width the slab is usually made in one piece; wide openings are made up of partssize of the blank, mass, lifting conditions
Tuck under the framea few centimetres, according to the window manufacturer's detaildesign of the sub-sill profile

The width of the slab (the depth from the frame to the edge) is calculated from the actual position of the window in the wall. On insulated facades it is the sum of the distance from the frame to the outer face of the wall, the thickness of the insulation with its finish, and the projection. An error of a few centimetres is critical here: too narrow a slab leaves the drip above the cladding, and water runs down the wall.

The lateral extension of the slab beyond the opening — the so-called «horns» — is chosen according to the design of the surround. If the window has an architrave, the sill is usually made wider than the opening by the width of the side profiles, so that they rest on the slab.

How to choose the profile and finish of a stone or GFRC sill?

The edge profile and surface finish are chosen according to the style of the facade and to how the unit will age under rain and dust. A horizontal external surface gets dirty faster than a vertical one, so practicality matters more here than gloss.

  • Straight edge with a chamfer — a neutral option for contemporary facades and business centres.
  • Half-round or quarter-round edge — a classical solution; it softens the line and chips less.
  • Moulded edge with fillets and a cove — for neoclassical buildings; the sill reads as a continuation of the string course.
  • Slab on brackets — two decorative consoles are placed under the window and visually carry the sill.

For stone on external sills, a honed, ground, flamed or bush-hammered surface is most often used. A polish on marble and limestone in the open air dulls over time, whereas on granite it lasts longer. The differences between the methods are described in the overview of types of stone finishing. With GFRC the texture is set by the mould: smooth, sandstone- or travertine-like, with a slight roughness. When a window receives a full set of decoration, the sill part is designed together with the side and top profiles — as a single GFRC window surround.

Colour also affects performance in service. On light limestone and white concrete, traces of dust and streaks from the frame are noticeable; on dark granite — limescale marks from hard water and efflorescence. Neutral grey and beige tones of medium saturation, as a rule, keep a tidy appearance longer. A sample should be assessed not in the office but on the facade, next to the cladding and the window profile, in daylight.

Long sills and ribbon windows: where to place the joints

A sill longer than one and a half to two metres is usually made in sections: a single slab of that length is heavy, awkward to install and sensitive to thermal movement. Joints between the parts are not a defect but a designed element of the detail.

The joint is placed on the axis of a mullion or symmetrically about the opening, so that it reads as part of the design. The joint width is taken from the design — as a rule a few millimetres — and filled with an elastic weather-resistant sealant matching the colour of the unit. Waterproofing or a backing flashing is provided under the joint: sealant ages over time, and water must not reach the wall even if it is damaged.

On dark stones and on south-facing facades heating is stronger, so the length of elements is reduced. The slab is not clamped rigidly between the reveals: a gap for sealant is left at the ends, otherwise on expansion the stone spalls the render or cracks itself.

A sill on a ventilated facade and on a rendered facade: what is the difference?

The difference lies in what the slab rests on and how moisture is drained beneath it. On a rendered facade the sill lies on a bed prepared on the load-bearing wall, and the insulation is trimmed to the fall; on a ventilated facade it hangs on the subframe brackets and does not touch the insulation.

  • Rendered (wet) facade. The slab is installed before the finishing coat is applied to the reveals, so that the render meets the end upstands from above. No voids are left under the slab: condensation collects in them, and in frost the mortar breaks down.
  • Ventilated facade. The support brackets are calculated for the weight of the slab and the snow load. A slot is kept under the sill for air to leave the cavity, and above the window for it to enter; they must not be closed off completely.
  • Masonry and stone cladding in mortar. The slab is taken a few centimetres into the reveal masonry and separated from it by waterproofing, so that moisture does not pass into the wall by capillary action.

On high-rise buildings sills and drips are additionally checked for wind uplift: light metal drips rattle and tear off when poorly fixed, while heavy slabs require mechanical fixing, not adhesive alone. In the zone of the fire barriers of a ventilated facade the detail under the window is coordinated with the system requirements — the material and thickness of the elements here are set by the technical document for the specific subframe.

What to state in an order for sills and drips

For a calculation, a schedule of openings and the detail of the junction with the window are sufficient. The fuller the input data, the more accurate the schedule and the less cutting on site.

  1. Schedule of openings: clear width, number of identical windows, levels by storey.
  2. Section through the wall: position of the frame, thickness of insulation and finish, type of facade system.
  3. Material and type of stone, or the colour and texture of the GFRC.
  4. Edge profile and projection; presence of «horns», end upstands, a drip.
  5. Method of support: brackets, mortar on a load-bearing substrate, subframe of a ventilated facade.
  6. Delivery and lifting conditions: number of storeys, availability of scaffolding or hoists.

On large projects a control sample for one opening is made first and approved on the facade, after which the series is started. The general procedure for working with a supplier is described in the piece on how to order stone for a project; for plinth storeys and entrances, where loads are higher, granite for facades and plinths is usually chosen.

Defects of external sills in service and how they are remedied

Most problems are connected not with the material but with water that has found a way round the fall and the drip. It is convenient to combine the inspection of sills with the seasonal inspection of the facade.

SignProbable causeWhat is done
Dark streaks under the windowno drip, insufficient projection, drip blockedthe drip is cleaned out or cut; if necessary the slab is replaced with a wider one
White bloom on the stone and below itefflorescence from mortar or masonry when wettedthe source of moisture is eliminated, then the surface is cleaned
Sealant peeling at the frame and revealsageing, no allowance for expansionthe old sealant is removed and the joint resealed on a clean dry substrate
Crack across the slabuneven support, rigid restraintthe support is assessed; the slab is, as a rule, replaced
Moss and algae on the north sidestanding water, reverse fallthe fall is corrected and a biocide treatment applied

Porous stones — limestone, sandstone, travertine — are treated with a water repellent after cleaning; the choice of products is covered in the article on protecting natural stone. The nature of salt stains and the methods of removing them are examined in the piece on efflorescence on stone and concrete. In winter, snow and ice are knocked off sills without metal tools: a chip on the edge at the drip disrupts water run-off.

The scope of planned maintenance is small: once a year check the sealant, clean out the drip and make sure the slab has not acquired a reverse fall through settlement of its support. If defects recur on several windows of one vertical stack, the cause is sought in the detail rather than in individual units — in that case it is useful to open up one sill and compare the workmanship with the working drawings.

FAQ

Which stone is best for an external window sill?

Dense stones with low water absorption — granite, basalt. Marble, travertine and limestone are also used, taking porosity and climate into account.

Can shell limestone be used for a facade sill?

In a mild climate, or after checking the frost resistance of the specific stone and applying water-repellent treatment; in regions with frequent freeze–thaw cycles dense stones or GFRC are better.

What is a GFRC sill?

A sill slab or drip made of GFRC, cast in a mould with a slope, drip and upstands; it can repeat the profile of a band or window surround.

Why does a sill need a drip?

So that water breaks away from the underside of the product and does not run down the wall leaving streaks.

What is the difference between a facade sill and a drip flashing?

A flashing is a thin metal element under the frame; a sill is a solid stone or GFRC slab that both sheds water and frames the opening.

Can an external sill be replaced without replacing the window?

In most cases, yes. The old slab is removed, the installation joint and the waterproofing under the frame are checked, and a new slab is installed tucked under the sub-sill profile. It is harder if the previous sill was bedded in mortar under the frame — then the detail is opened up carefully so as not to disturb the window fixings.

How is the sill width determined for an insulated facade?

Add the distance from the frame to the outer face of the load-bearing wall, the thickness of the insulation with its finishing layer, and the projection beyond the cladding plane. The measurement is taken after the windows are installed, and on projects with a ventilated system — allowing for the design offset of the cladding.

Does a stone sill need a protective treatment?

For dense stones such as granite and basalt it is usually not essential. Porous limestone, sandstone and travertine should be impregnated with a water repellent, and the treatment renewed at the interval stated by the product manufacturer.
