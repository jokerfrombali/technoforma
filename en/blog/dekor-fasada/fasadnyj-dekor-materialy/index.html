What Facade Décor Is Made Of: Comparing Materials

Facade décor is made from five main groups of materials: natural stone, glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC), ordinary and polymer concrete, polyurethane, and expanded polystyrene with a reinforced protective coating. Metal, ceramics and timber are used less often.

The main selection criterion is the element's working conditions: the building's height and fire safety requirements, the projection and weight of the element, the climate and the expected service life. For multi-storey buildings, non-combustibility and reliable fixing come first; for a private house, the balance of price, appearance and durability.

This comparison will be useful to architects, developers and private clients. An overview of décor is in Architectural facade décor.

Overview of materials

A brief look at each material.

  • Natural stone — granite, marble, limestone, sandstone, travertine, shell limestone (coquina). Travertine is a sedimentary rock, a calcareous tufa with a characteristic porous structure and banding, in warm beige or cream colours. Shell limestone is a porous limestone made of compressed shells; as a building material it is used for wall masonry, fences, cladding and décor, and is easy to work.
  • GFRC — a cement-sand mix reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre; thin-walled elements of any shape.
  • Concrete and polymer concrete — solid cast elements; polymer concrete contains a polymer binder.
  • Polyurethane — lightweight cast polymer elements, usually for interiors and sheltered areas of a facade.
  • Coated expanded polystyrene — cut profiles covered with a reinforced render layer.

Searches for facade materials also turn up "EGGER fronts" and "chipboard fronts" — these are furniture industry terms for the front panels of cabinets and kitchens made of laminated board. They have nothing to do with architectural décor on buildings.

GFRC

GFRC is the main material for architectural décor on multi-storey buildings. According to TECHNOFORMA, GFRC consists of four components (glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement, water), is non-combustible (Russian class NG), has high frost resistance that is improved with admixtures, and allows almost any shape.

Practical advantages:

  • thin wall — the element weighs several times less than stone or cast concrete of the same shape;
  • detail close to stone and the ability to reproduce the texture of natural rock;
  • non-combustibility — important for multi-storey construction;
  • large elements: cornices, columns, storey-height panels.

Limitations: mould cost for unique elements, the need for factory production and a fixing calculation. Exact properties depend on the mix design and are stated in the product data sheet.

GFRC elements are fixed to the rainscreen facade substructure or directly to the load-bearing wall via cast-in inserts. On ventilated facades the décor becomes part of the system: cornices, string courses and surrounds hang on the same substructure as the panels, so the details are designed together.

Natural stone

Natural stone is the most durable and "honest" material for décor. Granite and basalt barely wear, marble and limestone are easy to work, travertine and shell limestone give a warm porous texture. The downsides are high weight, difficulty making large profiles and the high cost of machining complex shapes.

That is why stone is used primarily on plinths, entrance areas, steps, ground floors and sills, and as cladding slabs; on the upper floors of multi-storey buildings, complex décor is more often made of GFRC with a stone texture.

TECHNOFORMA has worked with natural stone for more than 15 years: it imports stone from India, Turkey, China and Iran, stocks these stones and supplies stone to order from all over the world. The range includes shell limestone, granite, basalt, dolomite, limestone, marble, onyx, sandstone and travertine; the company has extensive experience with Dagestan stones. Its equipment processes both soft stones (sandstone, shell limestone, dolomite, limestone) and hard ones.

Polyurethane and polystyrene foam

Polyurethane and expanded polystyrene are light, inexpensive materials widely used for décor on private houses and low-rise buildings.

Polyurethane décor — cast elements with good detail. Installed with adhesive and fixings, then painted. On a facade it needs UV protection by painting; because of its high coefficient of thermal expansion, expansion joints are required.

Coated expanded polystyrene — a foam profile covered with a reinforced layer of render or a special compound. The most affordable option and quick to install. Impact resistance and durability depend on the thickness and quality of the coating; damage to the protective layer exposes the foam to moisture and UV.

The main limitation of both materials is combustibility: the use of combustible materials on facades is governed by fire safety requirements and depends on the building's functional fire hazard class and height. For multi-storey buildings their use is, as a rule, restricted.

For a private house, polyurethane and foam remain a workable option if the conditions are met: a good reinforced coating, sealed joints, falls on top surfaces and regular repainting. At ground floor level, where elements are exposed to impacts, it is better to replace them with stronger materials.

Which facade décor is best depends on the building: for a multi-storey residential complex, GFRC and stone; for a private house, any of the materials with proper protection and installation.

When comparing costs, consider not only the element price but also installation, fixings, painting and maintenance over the whole service life. A cheap element that has to be repaired or replaced after a few years may end up costing more.

How to choose a facade décor material by building type?

The material is chosen from the constraints of the building rather than from appearance: first, everything that fails on fire-safety requirements and loads is ruled out, and only then is an option selected from what remains by texture and budget. A cornice of the same design on a three-storey mansion and on a twenty-storey block is two different products with two different fixings.

Building typeWhat drives the choiceWhat is usually used

For high-rise blocks, access to the element after handover is assessed as well. The higher and less accessible a detail is, the more its repair costs, so saving on the material at the upper levels is rarely justified. General requirements for such buildings are covered in the article on the facade of a multi-storey residential building.

Concrete, polymer concrete and architectural concrete: when they are justified

Cast concrete and polymer concrete are justified where the element rests on a support and its mass is not a problem: balusters, railing pedestals, parapet copings, planters, column bases at ground level. For projecting details with a large overhang the same weight becomes a drawback, because it calls for heavy brackets and a check of the wall.

The differences between the three kinds of concrete décor come down to the binder and the thickness of the product:

  • Wet-cast concrete on grey or white cement gives massive reinforced products. It is non-combustible and resists impact well, but it reproduces fine relief less accurately, and large pieces have to be lifted by crane.
  • Polymer concrete is a mineral filler in a resin binder. It gives a dense surface with low water absorption and sharp edges; its behaviour in fire and under ultraviolet light depends on the resin, so it is checked against the documents of the specific manufacturer.
  • Architectural concrete is concrete with a specified colour and texture of the exposed face, more often in the form of large panels or monolithic elements cast on site.

Gypsum deserves a separate mention: it is not used outdoors, because it softens when wet and breaks down on freezing. If ‘plaster’ mouldings are found on the facade of an old building, they are most often lime or cement mortars, and when replaced they are usually reproduced in glass fibre reinforced concrete.

In practice the materials are combined: the balustrade and parapet copings are made of cast concrete or stone, while the cornices and surrounds above them are made of thin-walled glass fibre reinforced concrete. Two solutions that look alike are compared in the article on how GFRC differs from polymer concrete.

How much décor in different materials weighs and how this affects the fixing

The weight of an element determines the fixing detail and the demands on the wall more than any other property. The density of granite is about 2,600–2,700 kg/m³, of limestone and travertine roughly 2,000–2,600, and of cast concrete about 2,300–2,400. GFRC has a comparable density, in the order of 1,900–2,100 kg/m³, but the product is a shell usually 10–20 mm thick, so the mass of the piece is several times lower. Polyurethane and expanded polystyrene are tens of times lighter than stone.

Low weight also brings indirect savings. Light elements are raised by winch or by hand from the scaffolding, they are installed by a gang of two or three people, and the load-bearing wall does not have to be strengthened. Massive stone and concrete pieces require a crane, supporting corbels and a tighter delivery schedule, since storing them on site is more difficult.

Whatever the material, the fixings must reach the load-bearing substrate rather than the insulation or the cladding layer. For projecting elements at height, a calculation is made for self-weight, wind and icing. Figures and examples are given in the article on how much GFRC weighs.

How décor materials behave in frost, rain and sun

The main enemy of facade décor is water that enters pores and joints and then freezes. Durability therefore depends not only on the material itself but also on its water absorption, on the number of freeze–thaw cycles it withstands, and on how water is drained from the upper surfaces.

  • Dense stones such as granite and basalt absorb almost no water and are stable in any climate. Porous limestones, travertine and shell limestone require a water-repellent treatment and caution on the plinth, where there is splashing and de-icing salts.
  • GFRC and good-quality concrete are designed for repeated freezing; the frost-resistance grade is stated in the product documents. The weak point here is not the material but unsealed joints and horizontal ledges without a fall.
  • Polyurethane does not absorb water, but it ages under ultraviolet light if the paint film is damaged, and it changes size noticeably when heated, which makes the joints of long profiles open up.
  • Expanded polystyrene is protected only by a thin coating: once that is damaged, moisture gets beneath the layer and delamination accelerates after each winter.

For southern regions with strong sun, colour stability and thermal movement matter more; for northern and coastal regions it is the frost resistance of the material and the corrosion protection of the fixings.

Which décor is easier to repair and maintain?

Mineral materials — stone, concrete and glass fibre reinforced concrete — are the easiest to restore: a chip is filled with a colour-matched repair mortar, the surface is washed and, where necessary, the protective impregnation is renewed. Polymer décor is quick to repair but more often requires the whole piece to be repainted.

When assessing running costs, three things are usually taken into account: how often the paint has to be renewed, whether a single damaged element can be replaced without dismantling its neighbours, and whether the manufacturer will still have the mould for making more. For factory-made products the mould is, as a rule, kept in storage, so the piece can be cast again. The sequence of work is described in the article on repairing chips and cracks.

Service life should also be compared with maintenance in mind. Stone and glass fibre reinforced concrete last for decades without replacement provided that joints and flashings are sound. Painted polymer décor usually needs its coating renewed every few years and, if the protective layer is damaged, replacement of the affected section. For a building where facade work after handover means hiring access platforms and making arrangements with residents, this difference is significant.

Soiling is a separate matter. Light-coloured relief décor beneath cornices and sills collects streaks if no drips are provided in the profile. This is resolved at the design stage, not with cleaning agents.

What to request from the manufacturer before choosing a material

It makes sense to compare facade décor materials on the basis of documents and samples, not descriptions. The minimum set worth obtaining before a decision is made:

  1. documents on the fire performance of the material and, if the décor is part of a ventilated facade system, confirmation that its use in that system is permitted;
  2. strength, water absorption and frost resistance values with test reports;
  3. a texture and colour sample, preferably as a fragment of the actual profile;
  4. a fixing drawing stating the weight of the element and the load per anchor;
  5. a list of buildings where such décor has been in service for several years, with the option of inspecting them;
  6. warranty terms and care recommendations.

If the facade has many repeating details — architraves and window surrounds, string courses and bands — it is also useful to ask for a schedule by type. It shows how many different moulds will be needed and how that will affect the budget.

FAQ

What is facade décor made of?

Natural stone, GFRC, concrete and polymer concrete, polyurethane and expanded polystyrene with a protective coating.

Which facade décor is best?

For multi-storey buildings, non-combustible GFRC and stone; for private houses the choice is wider and depends on budget and required durability.

What is shell limestone used for?

Walls, fences, facade cladding, décor and landscaping elements; the stone is porous and easy to work.

What kind of material is travertine?

A sedimentary rock, a calcareous tufa with a porous structure and banded pattern, usually beige or cream.

How is a ventilated facade with décor built?

Brackets and a substructure are fixed to the wall, insulation is installed, then cladding and décor elements are hung on designed fixings.

Can décor in different materials be combined on one facade?

Yes, this is common practice: stone is used on the plinth and entrances, GFRC on the upper floors, and cast concrete for balustrades and parapet copings. It is important to agree colour and texture from samples and to provide joints where materials with different thermal expansion meet.

Which décor material should be chosen for refurbishing an old building?

GFRC is chosen most often: a mould is made from a survey of the surviving details and the profile is replicated, while the low weight does not overload the old masonry. Lost stone elements on listed buildings are restored in stone in line with the restoration design.

Does GFRC décor need to be painted?

Not necessarily. The colour is obtained either with an integral pigment or by painting with facade coatings for mineral substrates; the choice depends on the design and the required shade. A painted surface is renewed over time, whereas integrally coloured GFRC needs only washing and a protective impregnation.
