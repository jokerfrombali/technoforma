Pediments and Attics

A pediment is the crowning of a facade, a gable end, a portico or an entrance, bounded by the roof slopes above and a cornice below. In classical architecture it is a triangle or segment above a colonnade; in private construction the whole triangular part of the end wall under a pitched roof is often called a pediment. An attic is a wall above the crowning cornice that hides the roof and finishes the facade horizontally.

The main selection criterion is structure and loads: pediments and attics sit at the very top of the building, take wind and snow, and their weight is transferred to the walls or the top-floor slab. So the form is chosen together with the load-bearing scheme, and the decoration by its weight and fixing method.

This article is for architects, structural engineers and clients designing the top of a facade. An overview of all elements is in the Architectural facade decoration section.

Types of Pediments

Facade pediments are classified by shape and by how closed they are.

TypeDescriptionWhere it fits
Triangularclassical pediment with a horizontal and two raking cornicesporticoes, central projections, private houses
Segmentalthe upper cornice is an arcprojections, entrances, alternating with triangular ones
Brokenthe raking cornices are interrupted at the apex, with a vase, cartouche or coat of arms in the gapBaroque, expressive accents
Steppeda gable end with stepshistoric Northern European styles
Gableend wall under a pitched roof without a horizontal corniceprivate construction

The inner field of the pediment, the tympanum, is left smooth, clad, or filled with a relief, a coat of arms or a round oculus window. The slope of the raking cornices of a classical pediment is usually shallow, much lower than the pitch of a private house roof, which is why a classical pediment and the gable end of a house look different even though they share a name.

A pediment is often confused with a gable. Strictly speaking, a gable is the upper part of the end wall between the roof slopes, not separated from below by a cornice; a pediment is an architecturally framed crowning, closed around its perimeter by cornices. In private construction the words are used interchangeably, but in design documents and when ordering decoration the difference matters: a pediment needs a horizontal and raking cornices, a gable needs only cladding and eave detailing.

Besides shape, pediments differ by purpose: the main one sits above a portico or central projection; small ones are placed over windows (window surround pediments and hood mouldings), over entrances and on dormers. One facade usually keeps a single type of main pediment, while small ones alternate: triangular and segmental pediments over every other window are a classical rhythm device.

The dimensions of a classical pediment derive from the width of the portico or projection: the height of the tympanum is noticeably less than half the width of the base, and the cornices have a shallow slope. A pediment that is too tall reads as a roof; one that is too low gets lost behind the projection of the cornice. That is why proportions are checked on the facade elevation and in 3D from real viewpoints.

Attic and Parapet

An attic is a wall above the crowning cornice of a building. In antiquity, inscriptions were placed on the attics of triumphal arches; in neoclassical architecture the attic hides the roof, increases the height of the facade and provides space for sculpture or urns.

Attics and parapets are often confused. The difference:

  • A parapet is a barrier along the edge of a flat roof and is functional: it prevents falls, hides roofing details and helps organise drainage. It may have no decoration.
  • An attic is an architectural element above the cornice that continues the composition of the facade, with its own divisions, pedestals, profiles and sometimes balustrade inserts.

On modern buildings the attic is usually combined with the parapet: the wall along the roof edge acts as a barrier and is clad on the outside as an architectural attic. An attic can be solid or interrupted by balustrades, giving the facade a light silhouette. Its height is chosen to suit the proportions of the building and coordinated with roof guarding requirements if the roof is accessible.

Construction

Structurally, pediments and attics consist of a load-bearing base and cladding.

  1. Masonry. The pediment is built of brick or blocks on the top-floor wall. Tall thin walls need reinforcement and ties to the roof structure, otherwise they lose stability under wind.
  2. Cast-in-place reinforced concrete. Attics and parapets of multi-storey buildings are often cast together with the top floor slab.
  3. Steel frame. A light pediment or attic is assembled on a steel frame fixed to the slab or walls; the cladding is hung on the frame.

Cladding options are GFRC panels and profiled elements, stone, render on insulation or rainscreen panels. In a framed scheme the tympanum and cornices in GFRC are fixed to the frame on brackets, so the load on the walls below is minimal.

In any option the top of the pediment and attic is covered: raking cornices with roofing or a metal flashing, the top of the attic with a coping sloping inwards towards the roof. The junction with the roof is made by turning the waterproofing up onto the vertical wall. Cladding joints on raking cornices are arranged so that water cannot run into the joint from above.

Typical mistakes when building pediments and attics:

MistakeConsequence
Tall unreinforced masonry without ties to the slabcracks along joints, the wall losing stability under wind
Attic top without a coping or sloping outwardswater runs down the facade, soaks the cladding, causes streaks and efflorescence
Roof waterproofing not turned up onto the wallleaks on the top floor at the parapet
Heavy decoration on masonry without calculationcracks in the upper walls, risk of elements falling
No movement joints on a long atticcladding cracks from thermal movement
Insulation not taken up to the topcold bridge at the roof junction, condensation

Insulating the pediment is essential if there is a heated space behind it, such as a mansard or a plant floor. The attic and parapet of a multi-storey building are insulated according to the design to prevent the slab junction from freezing. If there is a cold loft behind the pediment, ventilation matters more: the roof space is ventilated through dormers or vents in the pediment, otherwise condensation builds up on the cold side of the structure.

For pediments and attics the weight of the cladding is especially important: it sits at the very top, and heavy elements increase the load on the walls and require strong fixings. That is why GFRC is the more common choice for cornices, tympana and attic balustrade inserts on multi-storey buildings. Stone is used on low-rise buildings and where the base is designed for its weight. Properties are given in the product data sheet.

Windows in a pediment, whether round, semicircular or dormer, need separate detailing: the opening requires a surround, a sill flashing and a junction between the cladding and the frame. In GFRC the surround can be made as one piece, which reduces the number of joints at the most vulnerable point.

Checklist before ordering pediment or attic cladding:

  1. Survey or working drawings are available, with levels, slopes and opening sizes.
  2. The load-bearing base (masonry, concrete or steel frame) is defined, and its capacity to carry the cladding weight is confirmed.
  3. Details are resolved: coping, roof junction, drainage.
  4. The cladding fixing scheme and embed locations are known.
  5. Material, colour and texture are approved against a sample.
  6. Access for work at height is planned: scaffolding, platforms, a cradle or a crane.

Loads on the Roof

Pediments and attics act on the roof and the top of the building in several ways.

  • Self-weight is transferred to the walls or the top-floor slab. For heavy cladding, the capacity of the base is checked.
  • Wind: pressure is higher at the top of the building, and corners and edges see increased peak loads. Tall attics and pediments are designed against overturning and fixed to the frame.
  • Snow: snow drifts form at attics and parapets on the roof, as snow accumulates against the wall and creates an additional load on the roof and the wall itself.
  • Water: an attic prevents free runoff, so drainage is provided by internal roof outlets or scuppers through the wall.

Design loads are set by the current Russian code of practice on loads and actions (SP) and depend on the construction region and building height. The calculation is done by a structural engineer; in reconstruction, after a survey of the roof and walls. When historic buildings are reconstructed, the old stone or rendered pediment often turns out to rest on weakened masonry. In such cases restoring the appearance with lightweight GFRC decoration on a separate frame takes some of the load off the walls, but the decision is made only after a survey and calculation.

How is a pediment divided into GFRC units?

The cladding of a pediment is not cast as a single piece: it is broken down into elements that can be transported, lifted and set in position. These are usually tympanum slabs, straight sections of the raking and horizontal cornices, corner elements and a separate apex piece — the ‘key’ at which the two raking cornices meet.

The layout is governed by three conditions:

  • Size and weight. The length of cornice sections is most often limited to 2–3 metres, and tympanum slabs to a size convenient for transport and lifting; exact dimensions are set by the design.
  • Position of joints. Tympanum joints are tied to the axis of symmetry and to the axes of the windows or pilasters below, so that the joint pattern reads as intended rather than accidental.
  • Corner details. The points where the raking cornice meets the horizontal one are the most complex in geometry. They are made as one-piece corner elements rather than cut to fit on site.

Relief in the tympanum — a coat of arms, a cartouche, foliage ornament — is designed separately. A large composition is divided into several panels so that the joints run through the background or along the outlines of the design rather than cutting across the main details. The depth of the relief is chosen with the viewing distance in mind: fine detail several storeys up cannot be read from the ground, so the forms are enlarged and the shadows made deeper. A round or semicircular window in the tympanum is given its own surround, which forms part of the overall layout.

A segmental pediment adds a radius: curved cornice sections are cast in separate moulds, and the number of unit types grows. The profile of the raking parts is kept the same as that of the crowning cornice, which is why GFRC cornices for the pediment and for the main facade are usually designed together. The layout, marking and details are recorded in the shop drawings used by both the factory and the installers.

Installation sequence for pediment and attic cladding

Installation proceeds from the bottom up and from the corners towards the middle: the horizontal cornice is set first, then the tympanum is filled in, and only after that is it closed off with the raking cornices. This sequence allows tolerances to be distributed across the joints rather than accumulated in the last element at the apex.

  1. As-built survey of the substrate. Actual levels, slopes and deviations of the wall or frame are compared with the design; discrepancies are taken up by the length and adjustment of the brackets.
  2. Setting out the axes and fixing the brackets into the load-bearing substrate, with proof tests of the anchors where necessary.
  3. Installation of insulation and wind barrier, if the pediment or attic is insulated.
  4. Hanging the horizontal cornice and corner elements, aligned by string line or level.
  5. Fitting the tympanum slabs or attic panels from the axis of symmetry towards the edges.
  6. Installing the raking cornices from the bottom up, followed by the closing apex element.
  7. Sealing the joints, fitting copings, flashings and drips, and handing the work area over to the roofers.

The work takes place at the highest level of the building, so the lifting method is decided in advance: a crane, a facade hoist or scaffolding with additional lifts. In strong wind the hanging of large panels is stopped — the wind load on a slab at height creates a risk both for people and for the product.

Before hanging begins, a trial assembly is worthwhile: the corner elements and the apex key are laid out on the ground or on a workshop jig to check how the profiles meet. Adjustment at height takes several times longer and almost always leaves a mark on the exposed face. The units are lifted in their factory packaging and in installation order, so that they do not have to be rehandled on the roof. The storage load on the slab is agreed with the structural engineers: pallets of panels are not placed at mid-span without a check.

Who is responsible for the junction between facade and roof?

Most leaks at pediments and attics arise not from the material but at the boundary between two contractors — the facade contractor and the roofing contractor. Before work begins it is therefore established who carries out each layer of the detail and in what order.

Part of the detailWho usually carries it outWhat to agree in advance
Turning the roof membrane up the inner face of the atticroofing contractorupstand height and the method of fixing the edge
Attic coping, flashing to the raking cornicesfacade or roofing contractor — as set out in the contractmaterial, fall, projection of the drip
Cladding of the outer and inner facesfacade contractorlevel of the bottom edge of the cladding above the roof
Sealing of cladding jointsfacade contractortype of sealant and its compatibility with the coating
Lightning protection, guardrails, equipment fixingsrelated tradespenetration positions and cast-in fixings

Penetrations through the cladding — guardrail posts, holders for the lightning protection mesh, lighting cables — are included in the design before the units are manufactured. Drilling finished panels on site is acceptable only where the engineers have provided for it. Typical solutions are collected in the article on roof junction details.

The sequence is also recorded in writing. As a rule, the brackets and insulation are installed first, then the roofer turns the waterproofing up the inner face of the wall, after that the cladding is hung, and only at the end is the coping fitted. If the coping is fitted before the cladding is finished, it will have to be removed, and a second installation is rarely as watertight. Concealed layers are signed off with photographic records before they are covered: they cannot be checked later without dismantling.

Movement joints in long attics

A long attic must not be clad as a continuous band: the wall, the frame and the cladding respond differently to heating and cooling, and the top of the building heats up more than any other part. Without movement joints the stresses are released as cracks at corners and around openings.

The general rules are as follows. Cladding joints are aligned with the movement joints of the building — they must not be bridged by a rigid panel. A gap is left between panels, its width set by the design with regard to the length of the element and the material of the subframe; it is filled with an elastic sealant over a backing rod. Each panel is fixed with one fixed point and the rest sliding, so that the unit is free to lengthen. The metal attic coping is likewise divided into separate lengths with sliding connections, otherwise it buckles and tears out its fixings.

At the corners of the building and where the attic changes height, the joint is moved some distance away from the corner, and the corner element is made in one piece. This reduces the risk of a joint opening up in the most visible place.

Thermal movement is particularly noticeable on south and west elevations and on dark surfaces, which heat up in the sun more than light ones. In regions with sharp daily temperature swings the joints work almost every day, so the sealant must meet requirements for elasticity and resistance to ultraviolet light, and its condition is checked at every inspection. Rigid filling of a joint with mortar instead of sealant is a common mistake: the mortar crumbles within one or two seasons and opens the way for water.

Pediments and attics on different building types

The purpose of the element changes with the type of building: on a private house the pediment remains part of the roof structure, while on a multi-storey block it is a hung decorative element that must be light and non-combustible.

Building typeWhat is usually doneWhat to look out for
Multi-storey residential complexattic-parapet with GFRC panels, small pediments over entrances and projecting bayswind loads at height, access for maintenance, fire-safety requirements
Business centretall attic concealing rooftop plantventilation behind the cladding, louvres, loads from equipment
Hotelpediment over the main entrance, attic with lettering or lightingcast-in fixings for signage and luminaires, concealed cable routes
Public buildinglarge portico pediment with relief in the tympanumdivision of the relief into panels, approval of the design
Private housegable or pediment beneath a pitched roof, dormer windowinsulation of the attic storey, roof overhang, junction with the gutter

In the refurbishment of historic buildings the task is reversed: the pediment or attic has to be reproduced from surveys and archive drawings without overloading the old walls. Surviving fragments are measured or scanned, a model and a mould are made from them, and the new cladding of thin-walled elements is hung on a light frame. In seismic areas additional requirements apply to the fixing of crowning elements, since the top of the building undergoes the largest displacements; such details are calculated by the structural engineer.

On residential complexes, attics are often assembled from the same unit types as the main facade, which reduces the number of moulds. The usual solution is GFRC facade panels combined with profiled finishing elements.

How should pediments and attics be maintained?

The top of the building is inspected more closely than the rest of the facade: it receives the most water, wind and temperature variation, and a defect can go unnoticed from the ground for a long time. The routine inspection is usually combined with the spring and autumn roof inspection. The following are checked:

  • the condition of the sealant in the joints, especially at the apex of the pediment and at the corners of the attic;
  • the fixing and integrity of copings, flashings and drips;
  • damp patches and streaks beneath the cornices — a sign of a reverse fall or a failed flashing;
  • cracks and chips on projecting parts, and the condition of the paint;
  • ice build-up and icicles in winter: a place where they form persistently points to heat loss or standing water.

Inspection results are best kept in a log with photographs of the same details each time: this shows whether a defect is developing or remains stable. Minor work — renewing sealant, touching up paint, clearing drips and drainage channels — is done straight away, without waiting for major repairs. The cladding is cleaned with soft brushes and water at moderate pressure; aggressive acid cleaners are not used without a trial on an inconspicuous area.

If open joints, displaced elements or rust streaks from fixings are found, a visual inspection is not enough — an instrumental facade survey with opening-up of the detail is required. Projecting pediment cornices are also checked after strong winds; how the load relates to the geometry of the profile is explained in the article on cornice projection.

What determines the cost of cladding a pediment and an attic

The cost is calculated from the design: it is made up of manufacturing the units, the subframe and fixings, installation at height and associated roofing work. The total is influenced most by:

  • the number of unit types and moulds — a one-off tympanum relief and curved sections cost more than straight repeating elements;
  • the area and height of the attic, the thickness of insulation, the projection of the cornices;
  • the type of substrate and the bracket spacing, which are determined by calculation;
  • the lifting method and whether plant can reach the roof;
  • the finish — painting, integral pigment, stone-effect texture;
  • the region of construction and logistics.

The budget can be reduced without loss of appearance by standardising profiles with the main facade, by using straight sections instead of curved ones where the difference is not visible from the ground, and by agreeing the layout early — before the moulds are ordered.

For the estimate, the manufacturer is usually given the elevation drawings and sections through the top of the building, information on the load-bearing substrate, a roof plan with levels, colour and texture requirements, and the desired delivery dates. If there is no design for the crowning elements yet, it is developed from the architect’s sketch together with the fixing calculation. The earlier the facade designer is involved, the fewer changes are made after the moulds are ordered, and those are the ones that cost the most.

FAQ

What is a pediment?

The crowning of a facade, portico or gable end, bounded by the roof slopes and a cornice; a classical pediment is triangular or segmental.

What is an attic?

A wall above the crowning cornice that hides the roof and finishes the facade; it is often combined with a parapet.

What is the difference between an attic and a parapet?

A parapet is a functional barrier along the roof edge; an attic is an architectural element that continues the composition of the facade.

What types of pediments are there?

Triangular, segmental, broken and stepped; in private construction, gables under a pitched roof.

What should pediment cladding be made of?

GFRC, stone or render; for the upper parts of multi-storey buildings, low weight and non-combustibility are important.

What is the difference between a pediment and a gable?

A gable is the upper part of the end wall between the roof slopes, with no cornice below. A pediment is an architecturally framed crowning, closed around its perimeter by a horizontal and raking cornices. In private construction the words are often used interchangeably.

Can GFRC pediment cladding be installed in winter?

Hanging the units on the subframe is a dry process and is possible at sub-zero temperatures. The restrictions concern joint sealing and painting: these are carried out within the temperature range stated by the material manufacturer. Work is also stopped in strong wind and icing.

Is a ventilation gap needed behind attic cladding?

Yes, if the attic is insulated or clad on a ventilated facade system. A gap with air entering at the bottom and leaving beneath the coping removes moisture from the insulation and from behind the panels. Without it condensation accumulates and shows up as streaks and efflorescence.

How tall can an attic be?

The height is set by the design: architectural proportions, the requirements for the roof guarding and the wind load. A low attic can act as a cantilevered wall, whereas a tall one usually requires a frame with raking struts or ties to the floor slab and a separate calculation.
