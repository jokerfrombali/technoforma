Facade Rustication: Types and Execution

Rustication is the decorative division of a wall into large blocks with recessed joints between them, imitating dressed stone masonry. Facade rustication makes the lower floors look heavier and more solid, emphasises the building's corners and serves as a base for classical elements above.

The main selection criterion is the scale of the blocks and the type of rustication relative to building height and viewing distance: fine rustication is lost on a tall building, while oversized blocks look ponderous on a private house. The execution is chosen according to the facade system — render, GFRC panels, natural stone or a rainscreen facade.

This article is for architects, developers and private clients choosing how to treat a plinth, ground floor or the corners of a house. Other elements of classical decor are in the section Architectural Facade Details.

What Is Rustication

In Russian, the word for rustication denotes both the stone with a worked face and the joint between such stones; in current practice the term refers to the decorative division of a wall into blocks. Historically, rustication was masonry of roughly dressed stones, where the joints and the rough projecting face emphasised the massiveness of plinths and fortress walls. Later the device moved into render and cladding and became purely decorative.

Rustication is characterised by three parameters:

  • joint shape — square, V-shaped or chamfered;
  • treatment of the block face — smooth, rock-faced (rough), diamond-pointed (faceted like a pyramid) or vermiculated/tooled;
  • pattern — horizontal joints only (banded rustication) or horizontal and vertical (block rustication).

Corner quoins are the most common option in low-rise and multi-storey construction: blocks alternating in length on the two sides of the corner visually strengthen it and separate one facade from another. On private houses, quoins are often made of the same material as the plinth, tying the base and corners into one system.

Corner Quoins and Full Rustication

Several types of rustication are distinguished by their position on the facade.

Quoins and full rustication are often combined: full rustication of the ground floor turns into quoins on the upper floors. Full rustication is appropriate for a height of no more than one or two floors or the plinth — over the entire height of a multi-storey building it makes the facade monotonous and heavy. Banded rustication works well on modern facades where classical modelling is wanted without complex decor.

Rustication Block Sizes

Rustication sizes are not standardised and are chosen by the architecture. In practice the following guidelines are used.

  • Block height. Linked to the storey height and window openings: it is convenient when a whole number of courses fits the storey height and the joints align with the bottom and top of the windows. On multi-storey buildings the blocks are larger than on private houses.
  • Block length. In quoins, blocks alternate long and short to imitate a masonry bond. The length-to-height ratio is chosen visually; a block is usually noticeably longer than it is tall.
  • Joint width and depth. The joint must cast a distinct shadow from the viewing distance. A joint that is too shallow disappears in sunlight; one that is too deep in render risks cracking.
  • Block projection. For rustication with a textured face, the face projects beyond the wall plane; for flat rustication the joints are simply cut into the surface.

Facade rustication must be coordinated with the windows: joints running into the middle of a reveal or passing through a pediment look random. The block layout is therefore drawn on the facade elevation, starting from the corners and openings rather than from ground level.

Execution

The method of execution depends on the chosen option.

  1. Render rustication. Render is applied to a levelled substrate, the joints are formed with battens or templates, and then the finish coat and paint are applied. On insulated facades, rustication is made within the thickness of the reinforced layer or cut into the insulation and then reinforced as per the system design.
  2. GFRC panels on a rendered facade. Blocks are installed on anchors with embedded fixings and adhesive, starting from corners and openings according to the layout; joints between blocks are filled or left open with sealant set back in the joint.
  3. On a ventilated facade. Rusticated panels are hung on the substructure like ordinary cladding; the rustication is formed by the relief of the panels themselves and the width of the joints between them.
  4. Stone. Blocks are fixed on anchors or laid on mortar with ties, depending on thickness and system.

The key requirement is an accurate layout: rustication does not forgive misaligned joints. At TECHNOFORMA Group the layout is prepared by designers, and for stone after measurements taken by the company's own surveyors.

Which joint to choose for facade rustication: profile, width, depth

The pattern of rustication is largely determined by the joint: its profile sets the character of the shadow, while its width and depth set the distance from which the division reads. With the same block size, changing the joint from rectangular to triangular shifts the image from severe to soft.

Joint profileHow it looksWhere it is usedParticular features
Rectangulara crisp dark band with a sharp shadowplinths, ground floors, strict classical workdust and water linger at the bottom of the joint; the lower face needs a fall
Triangulara soft shadow depending on the height of the sunbanded rustication, contemporary facadesself-cleans well, simpler to form in render
Trapezoidala shadow with a gradual transitionGFRC panels, a universal solutionconvenient for demoulding the units, hides small deviations
With a chamfer on the block edgethe block appears convex, like a «cushion»quoins, principal facadesthe chamfer protects the arris from chipping

In practice the joint width is most often taken within 15–30 mm and the depth within 10–25 mm; on large buildings and for distant viewing the values are increased. These are guidelines, not a standard: the final dimensions are checked on a full-size mock-up. Too shallow a joint «fills in» after painting and does not read at a distance; too deep a joint on an insulated facade weakens the protective layer.

In GFRC units the joint is of two kinds. A false joint is formed in the body of the panel, and one panel contains several «blocks»; an installation joint runs between panels and is filled with sealant. The layout is arranged so that installation joints coincide with the rustication pattern and do not differ in width from the false ones.

Rustication on different types of building: from residential complex to private house

Rustication is appropriate on any type of building, but it solves different tasks: on a multi-storey block it forms the base of the composition, on a business centre it sets the rhythm, on a private house it emphasises the corners and the plinth.

  • Residential complexes. Continuous rustication on the lower one or two storeys, with quoins or a plain wall above. The blocks are made large, with a repeating panel size, to speed up installation. The device is characteristic of buildings in the neoclassical style.
  • Business centres. Most often banded rustication with a triangular joint: it gives a horizontal rhythm and combines well with glazing.
  • Hotels and public buildings. A rusticated plinth tier in stone and portals with voussoir blocks; rock-faced or diamond-pointed rustication on accent areas.
  • Private houses. Quoins and a rusticated plinth; the blocks are small, in proportion to the window. Continuous rustication over the full height of a two-storey house is usually excessive.

For high-rise buildings the non-combustibility and weight of the cladding matter, so rusticated areas above the lower storeys are usually made of GFRC on a subframe, and stone is kept for the plinth.

When deciding where rustication is needed on a facade, it is useful to assess the viewpoints. From the pavement the relief of the ground floor is seen at close range, and here a deep texture and a deep joint are justified. The upper tiers are perceived from a distance and at an angle, so banded joints or quoin blocks of simplified profile are sufficient there.

What to combine rustication with: string courses, architraves, keystones

A rusticated wall rarely exists on its own: at the top it is finished with a horizontal moulding, and the openings within it receive their own surround. Without these elements the rustication looks cut off.

The upper boundary of a rusticated tier is a moulding or a string course, which separates the heavy base from the plain upper part and at the same time sheds water away from the joints. The projection of the string course is chosen to be greater than the depth of the block relief, so that the drip works.

Windows in a rusticated wall are treated in two ways. The first is a plain architrave which the joints meet at a right angle. The second is a flat arch of voussoirs: the blocks above the opening fan out, and a keystone is placed in the centre. In the second case the layout starts from the axis of the window, and the course height must divide the height of the opening without remainder. Doors and carriage arches are treated in the same way, but with larger blocks.

Rustication is tied to the plinth by material or colour. A common solution is a granite plinth and a rusticated ground floor in a lighter material; the principles of choosing stone for the lower zone are set out in the article on cladding a plinth with natural stone.

How does a rusticated surface age and how should it be maintained?

Relief ages more noticeably than a plain wall: the joints collect dust, and traces of running water remain on the projecting faces. Sound joint geometry and surface protection remove most of the problems.

  • The lower face of a horizontal joint is given a slight outward fall so that water does not stand.
  • A string course with a drip is placed above the rusticated tier, and window drips are projected beyond the plane of the blocks.
  • The surface is treated with a water repellent: it reduces wetting and makes washing easier.
  • The lowest courses by the perimeter paving are protected from splashes — by a damp-proof course and a dense plinth material.

A rusticated facade is washed with water at moderate pressure, directing the jet from top to bottom so that dirt is not driven into the joints. Cracks in rendered rustication most often run along the bottom of the joint, where the layer is thinner; they are raked out and filled with an elastic compound before painting. With GFRC and stone panels the sealant in the installation joints is monitored: it ages faster than the units themselves.

On the south and west sides the relief is emphasised by raking light, and any deviations from plane are more noticeable. Tolerances for installing rusticated panels are therefore set more tightly than for plain cladding, and acceptance is carried out under side lighting.

Typical mistakes that make facade rustication look accidental

Almost all rustication mistakes are mistakes of layout, not of material. They are hard to correct after installation, so the check is carried out on the drawing.

  • Layout from ground level. The courses do not coincide with windows and string courses, and a cut block is left at the top.
  • Different course heights on adjacent facades — the joints do not meet at the corner.
  • Quoin blocks of equal length: the imitation of masonry bonding is lost.
  • A joint cut into the insulation without additional reinforcement — cracks run along the bottom.
  • Installation joints of the panels do not coincide with the pattern and read as accidental lines.
  • Painting blocks and joints in contrasting colours: the relief begins to look drawn on.
  • Rainwater pipes, cables and external air-conditioning units are placed over a rusticated corner.

Most of these shortcomings are eliminated at the elevation stage, when the block layout is coordinated with windows, string courses and services on a single drawing.

What to prepare for a calculation of GFRC or stone rustication

To assess the quantities, facade elevations and an understanding of the wall build-up are needed. From these the area of rusticated zones, the number of corner elements and the number of sizes are calculated.

  1. Elevations with dimensions, storey levels and openings.
  2. Type of substrate and facade system: rendered facade with insulation, ventilated facade, masonry.
  3. Desired block size, joint profile and texture of the face.
  4. Colour: integral or surface-applied, a sample for approval.
  5. Building height and construction area — for the fixing calculation.

Corner elements are better made as single L-shaped units: a joint on the arris is the weakest point of a rusticated corner. At TECHNOFORMA the layout and fixing details are developed by the design department, and the units are produced by the company's own factory; the available versions are gathered on the page for GFRC pilasters and rustication. For stone rustication on the plinth and ground floor, stones are selected by density and water absorption; among light options, limestone for facades is often considered.

If facade rustication is carried out over an existing finish during refurbishment, the results of a wall survey are added to the input data: the condition of the render, the load-bearing capacity of the substrate for anchors and the actual deviations from vertical. These determine whether the panels can be fixed directly or a levelling subframe will be required.

FAQ

What is rustication on a facade?

The decorative division of a wall into blocks with recessed joints, imitating dressed stone masonry.

How do corner quoins differ from full rustication?

Quoins are applied only at the building corners; full rustication covers the entire surface of the plinth or lower floors.

What can corner quoins on a house be made of?

Render, GFRC panels or natural stone; on a ventilated facade, rusticated panels. The choice depends on the facade system.

What size should rustication blocks be?

The size is chosen according to storey and window heights so that joints align with openings. On tall buildings the blocks are larger than on private houses.

Can rustication be applied to an insulated facade?

Yes: in a render system, as per the insulation system design; on a ventilated facade, with rusticated panels.

Can rustication be carried out on a ventilated facade?

Yes. The rusticated surface is made up of GFRC or stone panels on a subframe. Some joints are formed in the panels themselves and some arise between them; the layout is arranged so that both kinds of joint match in width and form a single pattern.

Which joint profile gets less dirty?

Triangular and trapezoidal: they have no horizontal ledge on which dust and water linger. A rectangular joint is more expressive but requires a fall on the lower face and more frequent washing, especially on the lower storeys.

Does GFRC rustication need painting?

Not necessarily. The units can be integrally coloured, in which case the colour is retained when the surface suffers minor damage. Surface painting is used when an exact shade from a colour chart is needed or a single colour with adjacent rendered areas.
