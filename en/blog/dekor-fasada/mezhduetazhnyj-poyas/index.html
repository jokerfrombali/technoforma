String Courses and Mouldings on the Facade

A string course on a facade is a projecting horizontal profile that separates floors or groups of floors, sheds water from the wall above and sets the horizontal rhythm of the building. Mouldings are slimmer horizontal profiles with the same purpose that frame windows, divide surfaces and emphasise the lines of the facade.

The main selection criterion is the string course's place in the composition: it must follow the logic of the facade (podium, floors, window lines) and the cladding joints, and its profile must be coordinated with the cornice and window surrounds. Technically a string course is simpler than a cornice: the projection is smaller, but the drainage and fixing requirements are the same.

This article is for architects and clients. All décor is covered in Architectural facade décor.

What a string course does

A string course on a facade solves architectural and technical tasks:

  • Dividing by height. It separates the plinth or podium from the main volume, emphasises the upper floors or attic storey, and breaks a tall facade into perceptible parts.
  • Scale. On a multi-storey building, horizontals "compress" the height and make the facade human in scale.
  • Drainage. Like a small cornice, a string course throws off run-off, protecting the windows and cladding below.
  • Cladding joints. A string course is conveniently aligned with horizontal panel joints, movement joints or a change of material — it covers the joint and makes it part of the architecture.

Mouldings play the same role on a smaller scale: they emphasise the sill line, frame piers between windows and form panels and frames.

Where string courses are placed on a multi-storey building: above the ground floor or a podium with commercial premises; at floor-slab level every equal number of floors to break a tall volume into tiers; below the top one or two floors, marking them out as an attic or "crown". How many string courses there are depends on the style: a neoclassical facade allows several horizontals, a contemporary one one or two.

Profiles

The profile of a string course is simpler than that of a crowning cornice but built from the same mouldings: fillet, cavetto, quarter round, cyma recta, cyma reversa. The main options:

TypeDescriptionWhere appropriate
Flat bandA rectangular projection without a complex profileContemporary and minimalist facades
Profiled string courseA combination of fillets and curved mouldings, with a dripNeoclassical, classical
Cornice-type string courseA developed profile with noticeable projectionAbove the podium, below the attic storey
MouldingA narrow profile with small projectionSill lines, panels, frames
Ornamental bandWith meander, dentils, reliefAccent levels of the facade

Profile dimensions are set according to the scale of the building and viewing distance. For ease of production, the string course profile is standardised across the building where possible: one mould produces linear elements, while corners and ends are made separately.

Flexible moulds are used for ornamental bands with relief, rigid ones for simple profiles. The length of a linear element is chosen according to weight, ease of installation and the substructure bracket spacing: joints should not fall mid-span between supports.

Materials

String courses and mouldings are made of the same materials as other décor:

  • GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) — the main option for multi-storey buildings: hollow linear elements in lengths convenient for installation, non-combustible (Russian class NG, according to the manufacturer), detail of any complexity.
  • Natural stone — for plinth and ground-floor bands where resistance to mechanical damage matters.
  • Polyurethane and coated expanded polystyrene — for private and low-rise houses; combustible, their use is restricted by fire safety requirements.
  • Run plaster mouldings — run in situ with a template over insulation or the wall; the traditional method for restoration and rendered facades.

On facades with rainscreen cladding, it makes sense to make the string course from the same material as the rest of the décor, so that colour, texture and thermal movement behaviour match.

Plaster mouldings over insulation and polymer profiles are appropriate on small houses, but at ground floor level, where impacts are possible, they are better replaced with stronger materials or protected. For multi-storey buildings, the key arguments for GFRC and stone are non-combustibility, UV resistance and the possibility of reliable mechanical fixing.

Fixing

Linear string course elements are fixed on the same principle as cornices, but the loads are lower:

  1. To the rainscreen substructure via cast-in inserts in the element and adjustable brackets. This allows the string course line to be set around the entire perimeter with high accuracy.
  2. With anchors into the wall — for light elements on masonry and concrete substrates.
  3. Adhesive and plugs — only for light polymer mouldings on low-rise buildings.

Joints between linear elements are spaced to accommodate thermal movement and are filled with an elastic sealant or left open with a flashing beneath. The top of the string course is given a fall and, for large projections, covered with a metal flashing. The underside has a drip.

The main installation task is a perfect horizontal: deviations in the line on a long facade are clearly visible, so setting out is done by survey and brackets are adjusted into position.

At building corners the string course is assembled from separate corner pieces — external and internal, cast in their own mould or cut from linear elements at an angle. Corner pieces are fixed at least as securely as standard ones: wind suction is higher at corners.

Combining with cladding

A string course links the different materials of a facade. Typical combinations:

  • A GFRC string course on porcelain stoneware cladding — it covers the horizontal substructure joint and adds sculptural quality to a flat facade.
  • A string course at the boundary between stone and GFRC — separates a stone plinth from floors clad in GFRC.
  • A string course on a rendered facade — marks levels on an external insulation and render system; fixed through the insulation to the substrate.

It is important to coordinate string course levels with the cladding layout and windows in advance: a string course running through the middle of a panel or along the top of a window without allowing for the flashing creates complex and vulnerable details. The colour of the string course is chosen either to match the cladding (for a soft division) or to contrast (for a graphic facade).

Another combination is string courses and mouldings as a frame for panels: horizontal mouldings together with vertical lesenes or pilasters form a grid into which cladding slabs fit. This hides differences in tone or texture between cladding batches and at the same time gives the facade an expressive rhythm. How to choose the height and projection of a string course

The height and projection of a string course are set from the scale of the building: the profile must be visible from the street yet remain subordinate to the crowning cornice. There are no universal dimensions, but the relationships between facade elements are fairly stable and can be relied on when sketching.

  • Hierarchy. The largest horizontal element is the cornice, followed by the band above the podium or plinth, then the ordinary string courses and, thinnest of all, the sill mouldings.
  • Profile height. It is related to the storey height: too tall a band eats into the wall below the window, while too low a one is lost against the cladding joints.
  • Projection. It is usually less than or comparable with the height of the profile; a large overhang turns the band into a cornice and calls for a different fixing and flashing.
  • Installation height. On the upper tiers the profile is enlarged and simplified so that the shadow can be read from the ground.
  • Relationship with windows. The underside of the band is conveniently tied to the top of the window surround or to the sill line of the next floor.

The layout is checked on the elevation and in the 3D model together with the other divisions. The general principles for arranging horizontals and verticals are set out in the article on facade composition.

How to drain water from a string course so that the wall does not streak

Streaks appear beneath a band when water from the top face runs down the front surface and returns to the wall. Three details prevent this: an outward fall on the top ledge, a drip on the lower edge and a sealed junction with the wall at the top.

DetailPurposeWhat happens without it
Fall on the top faceSheds water and melting snow outwardsStanding water, dirt stains, wetting of the joint at the wall
DripBreaks the flow at the lower edge of the profileWater runs under the band and leaves stripes on the cladding
Metal flashingProtects the top of a band with a large projection and the joints between elementsWater enters the joints between linear products
Sealing of the top junctionKeeps water from getting behind the productWetting of the insulation and fixings

The metal flashing is matched to the colour of the band or the cladding and is taken under the tile above or into a chase with sealant. The ends of the band at window reveals and at corners are closed so that water does not run sideways onto the wall. On bands of small projection a flashing is often omitted, relying on the fall, water-repellent treatment and careful joint filling.

What to consider when forming a string course on a ventilated facade

On a ventilated facade the band becomes part of the system: it crosses the air gap and the insulation layer, so its detail is coordinated with the subframe design. The main task is to fix the product without disturbing ventilation or the fire-protection measures.

The following issues are usually worked through:

  • maintaining air movement behind the cladding above and below the band — through gaps, perforated elements or a bypass behind the product;
  • combining the band with horizontal fire barriers, if the system provides for them at this level;
  • separate brackets for the band that take account of its weight and projection, rather than hanging it on ordinary rails without checking by calculation;
  • continuity of the insulation and the wind barrier behind the product;
  • alignment of the band joints with the vertical joints of the cladding, or a deliberate offset from them.

The solutions are issued as separate sheets within the working documentation. Details of similar logic at windows and parapets are examined in the article on ventilated facade junctions.

In what order are string courses and mouldings installed?

The band is installed after the subframe and insulation but before the adjoining courses of cladding — this makes the flashing and joints easier to form. The sequence of work at one level is as follows.

  1. Setting out: transferring the level of the underside of the band around the whole perimeter and checking wall deviations.
  2. Installing support brackets or anchors at the designed spacing, with corrosion protection.
  3. Fixing the corner elements — the ordinary runs are taken from them towards the middle of the work zone.
  4. Hanging the linear products and adjusting height, projection and the line of the front edge.
  5. Fitting the make-up piece, whose size is confirmed on site and produced at the factory or trimmed within the permitted zone.
  6. Filling the joints, fitting the flashing, sealing the top junction.
  7. Cladding the adjacent courses, final painting or water-repellent treatment where specified.

Thin mouldings are installed in the same way, but more often after the cladding, on fixing points brought out in advance. Before the details are closed, the fixings are presented to the site supervisor as concealed works.

It is convenient to carry out the work in zones, one facade at a time, starting with the most visible side of the building: in this way deviations accumulated during setting out fall on secondary areas. Before hanging begins, the completeness of the batch is checked on site against the labelling, together with the condition of the products after transport, and linear elements are stored on level spacers to rule out sagging and chipped edges.

What mistakes are most often made with string courses and mouldings?

Most defects are built in at the design stage, when the band is drawn on the elevation without being checked in section. Typical miscalculations are listed below.

  • The band does not coincide with the cladding joints. Slabs have to be cut into narrow strips above and below the profile.
  • Conflict with windows. The profile runs into a sill flashing or covers the head of the opening and has to be stopped at every window.
  • No fall and no drip. After one or two seasons dark stripes appear beneath the band.
  • Runs that are too long without movement joints. Thermal expansion opens the joints or forces out the filling.
  • A different line on adjacent work zones. The lack of a single setting-out produces a step that shows in the shadow.
  • Corners made from trimmed ordinary elements without reinforcement. Wind loads are greatest at the corner, and weak fixing here is unacceptable.
  • Colour variation. Products from different batches without a common reference sample differ in shade.

If the lower floors are rusticated, the band must finish the rustication logically, along the top of the last course of blocks; the combination of these devices is described in the article on facade rustication.

How do string courses and mouldings behave in service, and what should be done with them?

A string course is one of the facade details most exposed to the weather: snow lies on its top face, dust settles there, and the lower edge constantly acts as a drip. For this reason it is checked first during building inspections, even if the rest of the cladding looks faultless.

  • Soiling of the top ledge. Dust and leaves retain moisture; during scheduled facade washing the ledge is cleaned and the flashings are checked for integrity and secure fixing.
  • Condition of the joints. Sealant in movement joints loses elasticity over time. It is replaced in sections without waiting for leaks behind the product.
  • Dark stripes beneath the band. This is usually a sign of a blocked or missing drip or of a disturbed fall on the flashing. The cause is remedied, after which the cladding is washed.
  • Ice and icicles. On bands with a large projection above entrances and pavements their appearance is monitored during thaws; if ice forms regularly, the flashing detail is reviewed.
  • Mechanical damage. On the lower tiers chips from machinery and equipment are possible; small defects are repaired in place with colour and texture matched.

Painted bands are renewed at the same time as the rest of the decor to avoid a difference in shade. Inspection results are recorded with reference to grid lines and floors: this simplifies the planning of work from cradles and the comparison of condition from year to year.

What to prepare for an estimate of GFRC string courses and mouldings

For a preliminary estimate, elevations showing the levels of the bands and a section of the profile are sufficient. From these the perimeter length, the number of corners and ends, the types of mould and the fixing arrangement are determined.

  • facade elevations and dimensioned plans, including bay windows, breaks and curved sections;
  • the profile of the band and mouldings with dimensions, or a reference to an analogue from the catalogue;
  • the wall construction and type of facade system, insulation thickness;
  • colour and texture requirements, whether painting is needed;
  • installation conditions and the preferred sequence of delivery by section.

Curved sections and radiused corners are priced separately: they need their own moulds. The range of serial profiles and the available versions are given on the page GFRC mouldings, string courses and bands, and the rules for choosing a section in the article on facade mouldings. FAQ

What are facade mouldings?

Narrow horizontal profiles with a small projection that emphasise the lines of the facade and frame windows and piers.

Why does a facade need a string course?

It divides the facade by height, sheds water and covers horizontal cladding joints.

How does a string course differ from a cornice?

A string course sits between floors and has a smaller projection and simpler profile; a crowning cornice finishes the facade.

What are string courses on multi-storey buildings made of?

Most often GFRC or stone — non-combustible, durable materials.

How is a string course fixed on a ventilated facade?

Via cast-in inserts in the element to adjustable substructure brackets, with the line set out by survey.

How long are the elements of a string course?

The length of a linear product is chosen so that installers can lift and position it and so that the joints fall on supports. As a rule these are lengths of one to several metres; the specific size depends on the section, the weight and the bracket spacing and is stated in the working documentation.

Is a metal flashing needed on a string course?

On bands with a noticeable projection and at joints a flashing is desirable: it protects the top face and the joints from water. On narrow mouldings a fall on the top ledge, a drip and water-repellent treatment of the surface are usually enough.

Can lighting be built into a string course?

Yes, linear luminaires are placed on the top ledge or behind the lower edge of the profile. The recess for the luminaire, cable penetrations and access for maintenance are included in the product drawings before the moulds are made.
