Keystones and Arch Surrounds

A keystone is the central wedge-shaped stone at the crown of an arch or lintel. In stone masonry it literally locks the arch: once it is set, the voussoirs thrust against each other and the arch becomes stable. On modern facades a keystone is usually decorative — an accent element above a window, door or gateway, projecting from the plane of the surround.

The main selection criterion is scale and consistency with the opening surround: the keystone must be proportionate to the archivolt or architrave and project enough to cast a shadow without competing with the pediment and cornice. The material is chosen by facade system and weight.

This article is for architects and clients designing arched windows, entrances and gateways. Other elements of the classical facade are in the section Architectural Facade Details.

The Role of the Keystone

In a true stone arch, each wedge-shaped stone (voussoir) is a truncated wedge, and the keystone is the last, central one, set at the crown. Until it is placed, the arch is held by centring formwork; afterwards, the arch works in compression and transfers thrust to its supports.

In modern architecture the keystone's role is mainly compositional:

  • Accent on the axis of the opening. The keystone fixes the centre of the window or entrance and sets the vertical axis of the composition.
  • Link to the elements above. The keystone often appears to prop up a pediment, string course or cornice, visually transferring support to them.
  • Decoration. Its face may carry a mascaron, cartouche, acanthus leaf or monogram, or be left smooth with rustication.

A keystone is also used on rectangular openings — at the centre of a flat lintel built of voussoirs (a so-called flat arch). In both cases the keystone projects slightly from the plane of the surround and is usually taller than the adjacent voussoirs.

Arch Surrounds

An arch surround is a set of elements framing a semicircular, segmental or elliptical opening.

ElementPurpose
Archivolta profiled band following the curve of the arch
Keystonean accent at the crown of the arch
Impostsprofiled blocks at the springing of the arch, where the curve starts from the supports
Voussoirsrusticated framing of the curve imitating masonry
Pilasters or columns at the sidesframe the arch externally and carry the entablature above it
Spandrelsthe surfaces between the curve and the entablature, often with rosettes or relief

In practice, one of two approaches is chosen. The first is profiled: the arch is outlined by an archivolt with a keystone at the crown and imposts at the springing, with plain wall around it. The second is rusticated: the curve is formed by voussoirs with deep joints, and the keystone is distinguished by its size. The first approach is typical for upper-floor windows and calm facades, the second for ground floors, gateway arches and plinths, where massiveness is appropriate.

Archivolts

An archivolt is the profiled surround of an arched opening — essentially an architrave bent along the curve. Its profile usually follows the architrave of the order: two or three stepped fasciae separated by narrow mouldings, with a crowning profile along the outer edge.

  • Archivolt width is related to the width of the opening and to the width of the surrounds of adjacent rectangular windows, if any, so that all surrounds read as one system.
  • The keystone is placed over the archivolt and projects above and in front of its profile.
  • Imposts at the springing are horizontal profiles on which the archivolt appears to rest; their level coincides with the start of the curve.
  • Division of the archivolt into elements is radial — the joints point to the centre of the curve, like the joints of voussoir masonry. This division is inconspicuous and practical to produce.

For elliptical and segmental arches the archivolt is designed to the exact geometry of the curve; otherwise gaps will appear when it is installed on the real opening. This is why openings are measured on site before manufacture.

Manufacturing

Archivolts and keystones are made to a drawing or sketch. For GFRC units the process includes:

  1. Measuring the openings. The actual geometry of arches on site almost always differs from the design; the surround is made to measurements.
  2. 3D model and layout. The curve is divided into radial segments, the keystone and imposts are separate units, and embedded fixings are worked out.
  3. Model and mould. A segment mould is made for the linear archivolt profile, and a separate master model for an ornamented keystone.
  4. Casting with embedded fixings, demoulding and geometry inspection.

At TECHNOFORMA Group this chain is carried out by in-house departments: designers and 3D visualisers, mould-making shops where models and moulds are produced, and production with quality control at every stage. For stone execution, the company provides measurements by its own surveyors, layout and stone processing — for both soft stones (limestone, sandstone, dolomite) and hard ones.

The choice between GFRC and stone is usually determined by the floor level and the facade system. For ground-floor arches, gateways and plinths, stone gives natural texture and impact resistance. For upper-floor windows and rainscreen facades, GFRC is more convenient: hollow units are lighter and easier to fix, and according to the manufacturer the material is non-combustible (NG class) and frost-resistant. Exact properties are in the product data sheet or the stone test report.

Installation

  1. Imposts are installed first: they set the springing level on both sides.
  2. Archivolt segments are installed symmetrically from the springing to the crown and anchored to the wall through embedded fixings; joints are kept radial.
  3. The keystone is set last, aligned to the axis of the opening. If it is large and projecting, it is fixed with its own anchors rather than resting on the adjacent segments.
  4. Joints are filled with repair compound or sealant and colour-matched.
  5. The top of a projecting keystone and archivolt is protected from water: a fall, a drip or a metal flashing.

On ventilated rainscreen facades, arch surround elements are fixed to the load-bearing wall with separate brackets through the insulation, leaving the ventilation cavity clear. Fixings are stainless or galvanised steel, designed for the element weight and wind load.

What size should a keystone be: proportions and projection

The size of a keystone is derived from the width of the archivolt and the span of the arch, not chosen arbitrarily. As a rule, the keystone is 1.3–1.8 times taller than the archivolt is wide, and its width at the bottom is close to that of a single voussoir or to 1/8–1/12 of the span. These are guidelines from the classical tradition rather than a standard: the final dimensions are checked on the facade elevation and in the 3D model.

ParameterGuidelineWhat to consider
Keystone height1.3–1.8 of the archivolt widthThe top of the keystone must not butt against a pediment or string course without a joint gap
Width at the bottom1/8–1/12 of the arch spanFor narrow windows take the upper limit, otherwise the keystone gets lost
Widening towards the topSide faces point to the centre of the curveIn a segmental arch the centre lies below the springing line, so the wedge is less pronounced
Projection from the archivolt planeUsually 20–60 mm, more for gateway archesThe higher the storey, the bolder the relief so that it reads from the ground

On upper storeys details are seen at an angle and from a long distance, so fine ornament on the front face blurs. For such openings a plain or faceted keystone with bold relief is chosen. At entrance level, by contrast, detailed modelling is appropriate: a mascaron or a cartouche is viewed at close range.

If a facade has both arched and rectangular windows, the keystones above them are made as one series: the same projection and pattern, different widths. Then the window pediments and the arch surrounds read as a single system rather than a set of random details.

Arch shape and surround construction: what changes

The shape of the curve determines the number of mould types, the layout of segments and the cost of the set. A semicircular arch is the most efficient to produce: all archivolt segments are identical and are cast in one mould. The more complex the curve, the more bespoke elements there are and the stricter the requirements for survey accuracy.

Arch shapeSurround featuresWhere it is used
SemicircularOne radius, identical segments, symmetrical keystoneClassical and neoclassical buildings, windows and arcades
SegmentalShallow curve of large radius, imposts often replaced by architrave earsWindows of residential buildings, lintels above shopfronts
Elliptical (three-centred)Two or three radii, segments of different types, setting-out from site survey onlyGateways, wide ground-floor openings
PointedTwo curves meet at the apex; a joint or finial instead of a keystoneOriental and Gothic styles
Horseshoe, ogeeThe curve extends beyond the springing line, complex impostsOriental architecture, entrance portals

In pointed and ogee arches a keystone in the classical sense is often absent: the apex is finished with an ornamental insert or left as a clean joint between two curves. Such solutions are covered in more detail in the article on oriental-style facades.

For an arcade, that is, a row of identical arches, the moulds are reused many times and the cost of one set falls. A single arch of non-standard curvature costs more per linear metre precisely because of the tooling, not the material.

How much does an arch surround weigh and what is it fixed to

Weight determines both the fixing method and the requirements for the wall. A hollow GFRC keystone with a 10–20 mm shell usually weighs from a few kilograms to 30–40 kg depending on its dimensions; an element of the same size in solid limestone or granite is several times heavier. Exact values are given in the shop drawings for the product.

  • Cast-in-place concrete and brick walls. Anchors are set directly through embedded parts; for brickwork the pull-out capacity of the anchor is tested beforehand.
  • Aerated concrete and hollow blocks. Chemical anchors are needed, or the fixing is transferred to reinforced concrete lintels and ring beams; heavy stone keystones are, as a rule, not used on such walls.
  • Insulated wall. The element is carried by a bracket passing through the insulation, not by adhesive applied to the insulation.

A stone keystone with a large projection rests on a steel cantilever or is built into the masonry, while a GFRC element is more often hung on a subframe. A comparison of the two materials in terms of weight and fixing details is given in the article GFRC or natural stone.

What mistakes are made when designing arch surrounds?

Most problems originate at the sketch stage and show up during installation. The most common mistakes are listed below.

  1. The keystone is scaled together with the window. When the opening is reduced, the keystone is reduced proportionally, and on narrow windows it turns into an indistinct appliqué. It is better to keep the projection and change only the width.
  2. Finish thickness is not taken into account. The archivolt is designed from the structural opening, but the reveal has shifted after insulation and rendering, so a step appears between the reveal and the profile.
  3. Segment joints fall in a conspicuous place. A joint on the arch axis without a keystone, or a joint next to the impost, is noticeable; it is better to tie the subdivision to the keystone and the springing points.
  4. No water run-off. A horizontal impost ledge and a keystone top without a fall collect water, and streaks appear beneath them.
  5. Voussoirs are not coordinated with the wall rustication. The horizontal joints of the facade rustication must meet the joints of the voussoirs, otherwise the pattern falls apart.
  6. The arch is ordered separately from the rest of the decor. Different batches and different manufacturers produce discrepancies in colour and texture.

All these mistakes are cheaper to correct on paper. It therefore makes sense to draw arched openings at a scale of 1:10 or 1:20 together with adjacent elements, and only then issue dimensions for production.

What to prepare for a keystone and archivolt estimate

For a preliminary estimate, an elevation with opening dimensions and a profile sketch are sufficient; a site survey is needed to start production. The more complete the input data, the fewer approval iterations.

  • facade elevations with levels and the number of identical openings;
  • arch shape: radius or rise, springing level;
  • archivolt section and keystone type as a drawing, sketch or photograph of a reference;
  • wall material and facade build-up, insulation thickness;
  • required colour and texture, a sample of adjacent cladding for matching;
  • installation conditions: scaffolding or cradles, number of storeys, sequence of work sections.

Based on these data the designers prepare the layout and determine the number of moulds and the fixing details. Standard sections can be selected from the range of GFRC window surrounds, while bespoke ornament is made from a master model. For a stone version the rock type is also chosen: soft limestone for facades carves well, whereas granite is appropriate on plinths and gateways where impacts are possible.

How to look after an arch surround in service

An arch surround requires no special maintenance, but projecting details collect dust and water sooner than the rest of the wall, so they are inspected first. A scheduled inspection once a year is sufficient, usually in spring after the freeze-thaw season.

  • Joints. The radial joints between segments and the junction of the keystone with the archivolt are checked for opening and for sealant debonding; damaged sections are raked out and refilled.
  • Top faces. There must be no standing water, moss or dirt deposits on the impost ledge and the top of the keystone; where necessary the fall is restored or a metal flashing is fitted.
  • Surface. Streaks and atmospheric soiling are washed off with water, a neutral detergent and a soft brush; abrasives and acidic products are not used on painted GFRC or limestone.
  • Protective coating. The water repellent is renewed at the interval specified by the product manufacturer, as a rule once every few years.
  • Fixings. A crack along a joint or displacement of the keystone from the axis of the opening is a reason to call the contractor and check the anchors.

Chipped edges, which most often occur at gateway arches because of vehicles, are repaired with a compound matched to the colour of the element. At gateways, up to the height of a lorry body, it is sensible to provide stone imposts or bump rails from the outset and to place the decorative keystone and archivolt above the zone of possible impacts.

FAQ

What is a keystone?

The central wedge-shaped stone at the crown of an arch or lintel. In stone masonry it locks the arch; on modern facades it is a decorative accent.

How should an arch on a facade be framed?

With an archivolt, keystone and imposts, or with rusticated voussoirs. The choice depends on the floor level and the character of the facade.

Can a keystone be placed above a rectangular window?

Yes, at the centre of a flat lintel or the top of the window surround.

What is an archivolt?

The profiled surround of an arched opening — an architrave bent along the curve.

Why are arch surrounds made to site measurements?

The actual geometry of arches on site differs from the design, and curved units cannot be adjusted by trimming without spoiling their appearance.

How does a keystone differ from a voussoir?

Voussoirs form the entire curve of the arch; the keystone is one of them, the central one. It is emphasised by size, projection and decoration, so it is taller than the adjacent wedges and projects from the plane of the surround.

Is a keystone needed if the arch has no archivolt?

Not necessarily. A keystone can be placed on a plain wall, but then it needs compositional support: rusticated voussoirs, a string course or a pediment above it. A lone keystone without a surround usually looks accidental.

Can an arch surround be installed on an already finished facade?

Yes, provided the wall can take anchors and the finish allows local opening-up for fixings. Before ordering, the openings are surveyed and the substrate is checked; after installation the joints and junctions are sealed and tinted.
