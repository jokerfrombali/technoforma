Window Pediments: Types and Purpose

A window pediment (hood moulding) is a small projecting cornice above a window or door opening. It can be straight, triangular or segmental (arched) and does two jobs: it throws rainwater clear of the head of the opening and completes the window surround, giving it a finished architectural look.

The main criteria when choosing a pediment are drainage and projection: the element must project far enough for water from its drip to fall clear of the reveal and frame, and its top surface must have a fall. The shape (triangle, arc, straight shelf) is set by the facade style and by the order and window surrounds used.

This article is for architects and contractors detailing window surrounds, and for owners of classical-style private houses. More on ornament in general is in Architectural facade ornament.

What Is a Window Pediment

A window pediment is a scaled-down cornice that follows the logic of the building's main crowning cornice at the scale of a single opening. It rests on top of the window surround or on brackets at the sides and consists of the same parts as a cornice: a projecting corona (drip shelf), mouldings beneath it and, for triangular and segmental types, a tympanum — the flat field inside the small gable.

Facade pediments are used:

  • above the windows of the principal floor, where an accent is needed;
  • above balcony doors and entrances;
  • on every window floor by floor, in neoclassical multi-storey buildings where pediments create a horizontal rhythm;
  • above niches and decorative panels.

A pediment is not required in every composition: on restrained facades it is replaced by a simple moulding above the surround or a small cornice shelf. But on windows with no canopies or balconies above, a pediment noticeably reduces wetting of the head reveal and the window unit.

Triangular and Segmental

The three main pediment shapes come from classical architecture and live on in contemporary neoclassicism.

ShapeDescriptionCharacter and use
Straight (horizontal)a cornice shelf across the opening width, sometimes with a frieze belowaustere, versatile; suits all floors
Triangulara small gable with a tympanumceremonial; principal floors, entrances
Segmentala shallow arcsoft, sculptural; often alternates with triangular
Brokentriangular or segmental with a gap at the apex, sometimes with a vase or cartouchebaroque, expressive; single accents

A classic device is alternating triangular and segmental pediments along the windows of one floor. It enlivens a long facade but demands precise symmetry about the axes. The pitch of a triangular pediment is usually shallow, within the range typical for gables; the rise of a segmental one follows an arc matched to the window width. Too steep a triangle looks like a house roof; too flat an arc looks like a manufacturing error.

Pediment dimensions are set from the opening and surround, not from a catalogue. Practical guidelines:

  • width — the outer line of the surround plus a small overhang on each side, so the shelf overlaps the frame and does not look shorter than the window;
  • projection from the wall — enough for the drop from the drip to fall beyond the plane of the sill; on a rainscreen facade it is measured from the cladding face, not the structural wall;
  • moulding height — in proportion to the surround width and window height; on upper floors elements are often enlarged so they read from the ground the same as lower ones;
  • distance to the cornice or string course — enough that the pediment does not merge with the floor band; if space is short, a straight shelf is chosen instead of a gable;
  • brackets (consoles) under the pediment — their height is matched to the surround and their projection must equal the shelf projection.

Dimensions are checked on the facade elevation and a 3D model from real viewpoints: from the pavement, a pediment on floors 5–10 is seen at a steep angle from below.

Drainage

Drainage is the functional basis of a pediment, and mistakes here lead to leaks and streaks on the wall.

  • Fall on the top surface — away from the wall. A level shelf with no fall collects water, dust and snow.
  • Drip (throat) — a groove or projection along the lower edge of the overhang. It breaks the running drop so it cannot creep back along the soffit to the wall.
  • Junction with the wall — the top joint between pediment and wall is sealed or covered with a metal flashing tucked under the cladding or render.
  • Projection — enough for the drop to fall beyond the plane of the window frame and sill.
  • The tympanum of a triangular pediment is made vertical or slightly recessed so water does not collect on it.

On GFRC pediments the fall and drip are formed in the element itself. On stone pediments the drip is cut. Metal flashings on the top surface are used when the joint needs extra protection or when the element is painted and the coating should last longer.

A pediment projects from the wall and acts as a cantilever, so the weight of the material affects the fixings. GFRC pediments are hollow and light and easy to repeat on dozens or hundreds of windows with identical geometry. Stone is appropriate on lower floors and in private houses. The properties of specific products are given in their data sheet.

If a pediment is painted, its top surface is better protected by more than paint: water and snow stay there longest and the coating wears there first. Options are integrally coloured GFRC with no paint, a water-repellent treatment or a thin metal flashing in the facade colour. The pediment material is also coordinated with the surround: if the surround is GFRC and the pediment stone, the difference in texture and colour will show even from a distance, so such combinations are checked on samples.

Fixing

A pediment is a cantilevered element over an opening, and its fixing is designed as carefully as a cornice fixing.

  1. Anchors and cast-in plates. Steel parts are cast into the GFRC element during forming, and brackets or threaded rods are attached to them. Anchors go into the structural wall or lintel, not into the insulation.
  2. Brackets. For pediments with a large projection or made of stone, steel brackets are used, hidden inside or beneath the element.
  3. On a rainscreen facade. The pediment is fixed to the structural wall through the insulation with separate brackets, keeping the ventilation cavity and detailing the junction with the cladding.
  4. Joints. A sealed movement joint is left between pediment and surround and between pediment and wall.

Fixings are stainless or galvanised steel. The fixing design accounts for the element's weight and the wind and snow load on the shelf. At TECHNOFORMA Group the details are designed by its own design department, the elements are made in its own workshops and installation is done by its own installation teams.

Typical mistakes in designing and installing pediments:

  • fixing into insulation or the facing leaf of masonry without reaching the structural wall;
  • a rigid joint with no movement joint, so cracks appear along the junction over time;
  • the top surface falling towards the wall because of inaccurate installation, so water runs to the window;
  • no flashing or sealing above the pediment on a rendered facade;
  • pediment not aligned with the reveal and frame on the axes: a 1–2 cm offset shows along a row of identical windows;
  • projection calculated without the thickness of the rainscreen cladding, so the shelf ends up almost flush with the tiles and stops working.

How a window pediment is coordinated with the surround, keystone and sill

A window pediment rarely works alone: it finishes the window surround at the top, so its profile, width and projection follow the other details of the opening. When the elements are chosen separately, the head of the window ends up heavier than the base or, conversely, is lost against a wide frame.

A typical set includes the side and head GFRC window surrounds, the pediment itself, a sill moulding or flashing and, where needed, supporting brackets. The practical rules of coordination are as follows:

  • the pediment profile is taken from the same family as the crowning cornice and the string course, but at a reduced scale, so that the facade reads as one system;
  • a plain frieze is often left between the head surround and the shelf: it separates the frame from the cornice part and gives room for a rosette or an insert;
  • brackets under the ends of the shelf are set on the axes of the side surrounds rather than at random;
  • the keystone in the lintel should not butt against the underside of the shelf; a gap or a frieze field is left between them;
  • the projection of the pediment is made greater than that of the lower details, otherwise water from the upper element falls on the lower one.

The base of the opening is resolved together with the head: the external sill and flashing receive the water that the pediment has diverted from the frame, and they also need a fall and a drip. When the head and base of the window are drawn at the same scale and matched in projection, the surround looks complete from any viewpoint.

Pediments on a multi-storey facade: hierarchy by floor

On a multi-storey building pediments are not repeated identically at every level: their shape and richness change with height and show which floors are the principal ones. This hierarchy comes from classical architecture and works well in the contemporary neoclassicism of residential complexes.

A common distribution scheme is as follows:

  • lower floors with rustication or stone cladding: openings without pediments or with a simple straight shelf, so as not to compete with the heavy plinth;
  • principal floors above the plinth: the most developed forms, triangular and segmental pediments on brackets, with a frieze and a keystone;
  • middle floors of the typical part: straight pediments of a simplified profile or only the head surround;
  • the top floor under the crowning cornice: minimal framing, since a large cornice works nearby and an extra detail conflicts with it.

Scale also changes with height. A detail designed to be seen from a few metres away merges into a blur on the upper floors, so the profile there is enlarged, small mouldings are removed and the projection is increased. In practice this means two or three versions of one profile for different tiers rather than mechanical repetition of a single unit.

Axes and accents are resolved separately. Above entrances, balcony doors and windows on the central axis the pediment is made larger and more complex than above ordinary windows, while on end walls and courtyard facades the set is reduced. This approach keeps the budget under control: developed forms remain where they are seen, and the bulk of the volume consists of simple repeatable units.

For paired and triple windows two solutions are possible: a common pediment for the whole group or separate shelves above each opening. A common element unites the windows into one mass and emphasises the axis, but it is long and has to be divided into parts. Separate shelves are simpler to produce and install, yet enough distance must remain between them, otherwise the ends visually merge.

Which pediment suits different wall types and facade systems?

The construction of a pediment is defined not only by its shape but also by the substrate it is fixed to. A unit that looks identical is designed differently for a brick wall, a rendered facade over insulation and a ventilated system: the embedded parts, the brackets and the junction detail all change.

Substrate or systemFeatures of the detailPoints to watch
Brick or stone masonryAnchors into the body of the masonry, anchor type selected by brick typeHollow brick and weak joints call for chemical anchors and pull-out tests
In-situ reinforced concreteThe most reliable substrate, anchoring into concretePosition of reinforcement, deviations of openings from the design
Aerated concrete and other cellular blocksAnchor load capacity is limitedFixings are taken to the lintel, ring beam or frame; heavy elements are undesirable
Rendered facade over insulationThe unit is fixed through the insulation on brackets to the wallThe pediment must not rest on the insulation board; the bracket penetration through the render is sealed
Ventilated facadeSeparate brackets or a reinforced section of the subframeKeeping the ventilation gap, fire boxes and cavity barriers above the window

In a ventilated facade the head of the opening is a crowded junction: the window reveal, the fire box, the insulation and the cladding all meet here. The pediment must not close the air gap or interfere with the cavity barriers, so its position is built into the system design rather than added after the cladding is installed.

For lightweight substrates and high-rise buildings, mass matters. A thin-walled GFRC unit is, as a rule, noticeably lighter than a solid stone or concrete element of the same section, which reduces the load on the anchors and simplifies lifting to the floor level.

Division into parts, joints and standardisation of sizes

A short pediment over an ordinary window is most often produced in one piece, while long and complex ones are assembled from several parts. The boundaries between parts are set in advance so that joints fall in logical places rather than in the middle of a visible surface.

  • A straight pediment is divided into a central part and two end elements with a return, that is, with the profile turned back to the wall.
  • A triangular one is assembled from two raking cornices, a lower shelf and the tympanum field; the joint at the apex requires precise fitting.
  • A segmental one is cast to a radius: for a large span the arc is divided into segments of equal length.

Joints between parts are left as movement joints and filled with sealant over a backer rod. The top joint is protected with particular care because water reaches it directly. The joint width is set in the design, allowing for thermal movement and installation tolerances.

A multi-storey building has hundreds of window openings, yet the number of pediment types should be small. The fewer the sizes, the fewer the moulds, the shorter the production and the simpler the supply of each work section. Openings are usually grouped by width, and small differences in size are taken up by make-up inserts or by the joint width. This work is done at the stage of preparing the facade shop drawings, where every unit receives a mark and a position on the elevation.

What to prepare for the design and production of window pediments

Elevations with opening dimensions and information on the wall build-up are enough for an estimate and for the start of production. The more complete the input data, the fewer clarifications and reworks on site.

  1. Facade elevations with levels and window setting-out, preferably in an editable format.
  2. A sketch or profile of the pediment: a dimensioned section or a photograph of a reference from which the designer will construct the profile.
  3. The wall build-up layer by layer: material of the load-bearing part, insulation thickness, type of finish or facade system.
  4. Actual opening dimensions from an as-built survey, if the structure has already been erected.
  5. Requirements for colour and texture: integral colour, painting, stone imitation.
  6. Number of storeys and installation conditions: scaffolding, cradles, lifts, time constraints.

On the basis of these data the unit drawings, fixing details and schedule are developed. If the profile is non-standard, a model and a mould are made first, followed by a control sample, which is approved before the series is launched. The number of identical units affects the cost directly: tooling costs are spread over the run, so a hundred identical pediments cost noticeably less per piece than ten different ones.

Installation sequence for a pediment above a window

Window pediments are installed after the window units are fitted and before the final finish of the adjacent wall, or together with the cladding; the sequence depends on the facade system. The general order of work is as follows.

  1. Setting-out: the axes of the openings and a single level for the underside of the pediments on each floor are marked, so that the shelves of neighbouring windows fall on one line.
  2. Checking the substrate and installing anchors; on weak and non-uniform substrates control tests are carried out.
  3. Installing brackets with adjustment in three planes.
  4. Hanging the unit on the embedded parts, checking for level and for projection from the wall.
  5. Final tightening of the fixings, protection of exposed metal parts against corrosion.
  6. Finishing the junction with the wall: sealant and, where necessary, an apron or flashing over the top surface.
  7. Filling the joints between parts, touching up at fixing points, checking the fall and the drip.

At handover, attention is paid to the line of the shelves along the facade, uniform projection, absence of chips on the edges and the fact that the drip is nowhere filled with sealant or render. The simplest check of the detail is a water test: the water should break off at the edge rather than run along the underside towards the wall.

In service: what happens to a pediment after several years

A pediment is one of the most weather-exposed details of a facade: snow and dust lie on the top surface, water breaks off at the edge, and ice may form in winter. For this reason the condition of these elements is checked first during scheduled facade inspections.

  • Junction with the wall: cracks and debonding of the sealant are the main route for water behind the unit.
  • Joints between parts: sealant ages over time and needs renewal.
  • Top surface: dirt and biological growth appear where the fall is small or water stands.
  • Fixings: rust stains beneath the unit indicate corrosion of the metal and require the detail to be opened up.
  • Birds: anti-perching devices are sometimes fitted on wide shelves, especially above entrances.

Streaks under the ends of the shelf most often mean that the drip does not run to the end or that the end has no profile return. Such defects are corrected locally, but it is cheaper to provide for them in the drawing. The surface of the units is washed with water and mild non-abrasive agents; more detail is given in the article on care of glass fibre reinforced concrete. Water-repellent treatment and paint are renewed at the intervals set by the coating manufacturer.

The same rules of water shedding and fixing apply to larger details, in particular to GFRC cornices, with which pediments are usually produced in one order and in one texture.

FAQ

What is a window pediment?

A small projecting cornice above an opening — straight, triangular or segmental. It sheds water and completes the window surround.

How does a pediment differ from a window surround?

A surround frames the opening around its perimeter; a pediment is a separate cornice above it, often forming a complete frame together with the surround.

Why does a pediment need a drip?

The drip breaks the water running along the lower edge and stops it creeping back to the wall and window.

What are facade pediments made of?

GFRC, natural stone, run plaster mouldings and, less often, polystyrene foam. For multi-storey buildings non-combustibility and fixing strength matter.

Can pediments be installed on a ventilated rainscreen facade?

Yes, on separate brackets fixed to the structural wall, keeping the ventilation cavity.

What size should a window pediment be?

Width follows the outer line of the surround with a small overhang; projection is set so the drop from the drip falls beyond the sill plane; moulding height is proportioned to the surround and the floor. Final dimensions are checked on the facade elevation and a 3D model.

Can pediments be installed on a facade that is already finished?

Yes, but the finish has to be opened up at the fixing points: the anchors are taken to the load-bearing wall, not to the render or the insulation. After installation the junction is sealed and the finishing layer is reinstated. On a ventilated facade this means removing part of the cladding and adding brackets.

Are brackets needed under a pediment?

Decorative brackets under the ends of the shelf are a matter of architecture: they visually support the cornice and are appropriate where the projection is large. The load-bearing function is performed by concealed steel brackets and embedded parts, which are present in any case and are calculated in the fixing design.

What determines the production time for pediments made to a sketch?

The availability of a ready mould, the complexity of the profile and the size of the run. For a new profile a model, a mould and a control sample are made in sequence, and the series is launched only after approval. Standard straight pediments made with existing tooling are produced faster than curved and assembled ones.
