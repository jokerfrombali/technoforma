Window Pediments: Types and Purpose

Updated: 2026-09-25 · TECHNOFORMA editorial team

A window pediment (hood) is a small projecting cornice above a window or door opening. It can be straight, triangular or segmental (arched) and performs two tasks: it sheds rainwater away from the head of the opening and completes the window surround, giving it a finished architectural look.

The main criterion when choosing a pediment is drainage and projection: the element must project far enough for water from its drip to fall clear of the reveal and frame, and its top surface must have a fall. The shape (triangle, arc, straight ledge) is determined by the facade style and by the order and window surrounds used.

This article is aimed at architects and contractors working out window framing, as well as owners of classical-style private houses. More on decor in general is in the section Architectural Facade Details.

What Is a Window Pediment

A window pediment is a scaled-down cornice that follows the logic of the building's main crowning cornice, but at the scale of a single opening. It rests on the top of the window surround or on brackets at the sides and consists of the same parts as a cornice: a corona (drip ledge) with a projection, profiles beneath it and, for triangular and segmental versions, a tympanum — the flat field inside the pediment.

Facade pediments are used:

  • above windows on the principal floor, where an accent is needed;
  • above balcony doors and entrances;
  • on every window floor by floor — in neoclassical multi-storey buildings, where pediments create a horizontal rhythm;
  • above niches and decorative panels.

A pediment is not essential in every composition: on restrained facades it is replaced by a simple profile above the frame or a cornice ledge. However, on windows without canopies or balconies above them, a pediment noticeably reduces wetting of the head reveal and window unit.

Triangular and Segmental

The three main pediment shapes come from classical architecture and remain in use in modern neoclassicism.

ShapeDescriptionCharacter and use
Straight (horizontal)a cornice ledge the width of the opening, sometimes with a frieze beneathaustere, versatile; suits all floors
Triangulara small pediment with a tympanumstately; principal floors, entrances
Segmentalan arc with a slight risesoft, sculptural; often alternated with triangular
Brokentriangular or segmental with a gap at the apex, sometimes with an urn or cartoucheBaroque, expressive; isolated accents

A classical device is to alternate triangular and segmental pediments across the windows of one floor. It enlivens a long facade but requires precise symmetry about the axes. The pitch of a triangular pediment is usually shallow, within the range typical of classical pediments; the rise of a segmental one follows an arc coordinated with the window width. A triangle that is too steep looks like the roof of a little house; an arc that is too flat looks like a manufacturing error.

Drainage

Drainage is the functional basis of a pediment, and mistakes here lead to leaks and streaks on the wall.

  • Fall on the top surface — away from the wall. A horizontal ledge without a fall collects water, dust and snow.
  • Drip — a longitudinal groove or nib along the lower edge of the projection. It breaks off the running drop, preventing it from travelling back to the wall along the soffit.
  • Wall junction — the top joint between pediment and wall is sealed or covered with a metal flashing tucked under the cladding or render.
  • Projection — sufficient for the drop to fall clear of the window frame and sill flashing.
  • The tympanum of a triangular pediment is made vertical or slightly recessed so that water does not collect on it.

On GFRC pediments the fall and drip are cast into the unit. For stone pediments the drip is cut. Metal flashings on the top surface are used when the joint needs extra protection or when the unit is painted and the coating must last longer.

A pediment projects from the wall and acts as a cantilever, so the weight of the material affects fixing. GFRC pediments are hollow and light and easy to replicate across dozens or hundreds of windows with identical geometry. Stone is appropriate on lower floors and in private houses. Properties of specific units are stated in their data sheet.

If a pediment is painted, its top surface is better protected by more than paint alone: water and snow linger there longest and the coating wears out there first. Options are coloured GFRC without paint, water-repellent treatment or a thin metal flashing in the facade colour. The pediment material is also coordinated with the window surround: if the frame is GFRC and the pediment is stone, differences in texture and colour will be noticeable even from a distance, so such combinations are checked with samples.

Fixing

A pediment is a cantilevered element above an opening, and its fixing is designed as carefully as that of a cornice.

  1. Anchors and embedded fixings. Steel fixings are cast into the GFRC unit, to which brackets or studs are attached. Anchors are set into the load-bearing wall or lintel, not into the insulation.
  2. Brackets. For pediments with a large projection or made of stone, steel brackets are used, concealed inside or beneath the unit.
  3. On a ventilated facade. The pediment is fixed to the load-bearing wall through the insulation with separate brackets, keeping the ventilation cavity clear and detailing the junction with the cladding.
  4. Joints. A movement joint with sealant is left between the pediment and the surround, and between the pediment and the wall.

Fixings are stainless or galvanised steel. The fixing design takes into account the unit weight and the wind and snow load on the ledge. FAQ

What is a window pediment?

A small projecting cornice above an opening — straight, triangular or segmental. It sheds water and completes the window surround.

How does a pediment differ from a window surround?

A surround frames the opening around its perimeter; a pediment is a separate cornice above it, often forming a full surround together with the frame.

Why does a pediment need a drip?

The drip breaks off running water at the lower edge and stops it from flowing back to the wall and window.

What are facade pediments made of?

GFRC, natural stone, render mouldings and, less often, foam. For multi-storey buildings, non-combustibility and secure fixing are important.

Can pediments be installed on a ventilated facade?

Yes, on separate brackets fixed to the load-bearing wall, keeping the ventilation cavity clear.
