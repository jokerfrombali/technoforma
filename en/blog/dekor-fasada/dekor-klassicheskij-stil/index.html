Classical-Style Facade: What Decor You Need

Updated: 2026-09-25 · TECHNOFORMA editorial team

A classical-style facade is built on symmetry, a clear three-part composition (base — wall — crowning) and the architectural order: columns or pilasters, cornices, pediments, window surrounds. Decor here is not ornament 'to taste' but a language with rules: proportions, a hierarchy of floors, elements that match one another.

The main criterion for choosing decor is how well it matches the building's scale and the logic of the order; second is a material that can withstand the outdoors without constant repair. Classicism is chosen for private houses, premium club houses and residential complexes, and public buildings. All element types are collected in the section Architectural facade decor.

Features of a classical facade

A classical house facade is recognised by several stable features:

  • Symmetry about a central axis: an entrance or projecting bay in the centre, identical wings to the sides.
  • Three-part vertical division: a massive plinth or rusticated ground floor, the main body, and a crowning cornice with frieze and attic or pediment.
  • Order elements — columns, engaged columns, pilasters with bases and capitals.
  • Opening surrounds — window surrounds, pediments, keystones, sill slabs on brackets.
  • Horizontal divisions — floor bands, string courses, cornices.
  • A calm, light palette — shades of limestone, sandstone, light render.

A single-storey classical house needs particularly careful selection of decor: on a low building a heavy cornice and large columns quickly 'press' the house into the ground. Such buildings suit an entrance portico, a light crowning cornice, window surrounds and rusticated quoins; a multi-storey building takes the full set of divisions scaled to its height.

Order and symmetry

An order is a system of proportions and elements of the column and entablature (architrave, frieze, cornice). Classical architecture uses the Doric, Ionic and Corinthian orders, as well as their Roman variants — Tuscan and Composite. Each order sets the ratio of column diameter to height, the size of the capital and the cornice profile.

In practice this means:

  • you cannot arbitrarily change a column's height without changing its diameter and capital — the proportions break;
  • the cornice and frieze must match the order of the columns that 'carry' them;
  • where there are several orders on one facade, they are placed from heavier at the bottom to lighter at the top.

Symmetry determines the position of all elements: the axes of windows, pilasters and balconies. In classicism symmetry is broken only deliberately — for example, in an asymmetric corner building where the axis moves to a corner tower or rotunda.

A private house rarely needs a full order. It is enough to follow its logic: portico columns proportioned to the entrance height, an entablature of matching profile above them, and wall pilasters that repeat the column module. With the proportions right, even simplified elements without carving read as classical.

The minimum set of decor for a classical facade depends on height and budget, but there is a core without which the style does not read.

Materials

Classical facade decor is made from:

  • Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC). Accurately reproduces cornice profiles, column fluting and carved capitals; non-combustible (Russian class NG, according to TECHNOFORMA) and frost-resistant. Allows the whole set of elements to be made in one material and colour.
  • Natural stone. Limestone, sandstone, travertine and marble are the traditional materials of classicism. Granite and basalt are used for plinths, steps and high-wear areas. TECHNOFORMA has worked with stone for more than 15 years and processes both soft and hard rocks.
  • Polyurethane and coated expanded polystyrene — budget options for small private houses, with limitations on combustibility and strength.

Materials are often combined: a granite plinth and steps, walls with GFRC decor. This gives durability where people and water are in contact and reduces the weight and cost of the rest of the decor.

Styling mistakes

The most common mistakes when styling a facade as classical:

  1. Wrong column proportions. Thin, tall columns without a matching capital, or short, thick ones under a light cornice.
  2. Decor without a system. Surrounds of different profiles on neighbouring windows, a cornice unrelated to the bands.
  3. Small decor on a large building. Elements designed for a private house get lost on a multi-storey facade.
  4. No crowning cornice. The building looks unfinished and water runs down the wall.
  5. Mixed styles. Classical columns next to high-tech glazing with no transitional elements.
  6. Unsuitable material. Plaster or unprotected foam outdoors breaks down within a few seasons.

Most mistakes are caught at the 3D facade visualisation stage, before the moulds are made.

Another common problem is economising on hidden parts. Decor with the right profile but without drips, flashings and well-designed fixing quickly darkens with streaks, and cracks appear at the joints. A classical facade is meant to last for decades, and technical details matter as much as proportions.

Finally, classicism does not tolerate 'partial' execution. If the budget is limited, it is better to build the full set of core elements in simple profiles than expensive columns at the entrance with bare walls on the rest of the house. Secondary decor can be added later if its locations and fixings are built into the design in advance.

FAQ

What decor is essential for a classical-style house facade?

A crowning cornice, window surrounds with sill slabs, horizontal bands and a pronounced plinth. Columns and pediments as designed, for the entrance or main facade.

How does a neoclassical facade differ from a classical one?

Neoclassicism keeps the symmetry and proportions but simplifies the decor: less carving, larger divisions, more smooth surfaces and modern materials.

Is the classical style suitable for a single-storey house?

Yes, with restrained decor: an entrance portico, a light cornice, window surrounds, rusticated quoins. Heavy elements look bulky on a low house.

What are the best materials for facade columns?

GFRC or natural stone. Load-bearing columns are reinforced concrete, clad in GFRC or stone.

Can a contemporary facade have classical elements?

Yes, that is the task of neoclassicism: classical proportions and divisions with a minimum of detail.
