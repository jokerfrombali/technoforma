Pilasters and Half-Columns on the Facade

Facade pilasters are flat vertical projections of a wall that repeat the divisions of a column: base, shaft and capital. A half-column is the same column set halfway (sometimes two-thirds) into the wall. Both elements divide the facade into vertical fields and emphasise corners, piers and entrances without taking up space in front of the building.

The main selection criterion is the depth of relief and the proportions relative to the storey and openings. A pilaster gives a graphic, restrained pattern; a half-column is more sculptural, with deep shadows. The material is chosen by weight, fixing method and facade system: rendered facade, ventilated rainscreen facade or masonry.

The solution suits multi-storey neoclassical residential complexes, public buildings and private houses. Other elements of classical decor are collected in the section Architectural Facade Details.

Pilaster vs Column

What is a pilaster in structural terms? It is a flat projection of rectangular section that visually represents a column but projects only slightly from the wall — usually by a fraction of its width. Unlike a column, a pilaster does not stand free and does not create space behind it.

ElementSectionProjection from wallTypical use
Columnround or polygonalfree-standingporticoes, galleries, entrances
Half-columnhalf a circle or moreabout half the diameter or moreprincipal facades, expressive piers
Pilasterrectangularsmall, often 1/10–1/4 of the widthdividing the wall plane, corners, piers
Lesene (pilaster strip)rectangularsmallthe same, but without base and capital

Historically, pilasters could act as a thickening of the wall, increasing its stability. On modern facades they are almost always decorative elements fixed to the load-bearing wall or substructure. If a real structure is needed behind a pilaster — for example a beam or a pier — it is designed separately, and the cladding simply follows its outline.

Proportions

Pilasters follow the same classical proportions as columns: pilaster height, including base and capital, is roughly 7–10 times its width depending on the order. The difference is that a pilaster usually has no entasis — the shaft is straight.

  • One storey or several. A giant order spans two or three storeys and unites them into one composition; a minor order is applied storey by storey.
  • Spacing. Pilasters are coordinated with window axes: they stand in the piers rather than covering openings. Pilaster width is chosen so that a field of wall remains between its edge and the window reveal.
  • Projection. A small projection is enough for a restrained pattern; on shaded facades and at great heights it is increased, otherwise the pilasters disappear.
  • Entablature. A cornice or string course must run above the pilasters, otherwise the capital appears to float in the wall plane.

Half-columns need more depth. On a facade with a ventilated rainscreen system, their projection adds to the thickness of insulation and substructure — this must be accounted for in the window junction details and in the cornice projection.

Materials

The choice of material depends on the facade system and the complexity of the profile.

  • GFRC. Allows pilasters and half-columns to be made hollow, with fluting and capitals, at low weight. According to TECHNOFORMA Group, the material is non-combustible (NG class under Russian standards) and frost-resistant, so it is used on multi-storey buildings, including as part of rainscreen facades.
  • Natural stone. Pilasters are built up from slabs and profiled blocks; the texture is genuine, but weight and processing costs are higher. Appropriate for plinths and entrances.
  • Masonry and render. The pilaster is laid in brick together with the wall and rendered; complex capitals are made as separate units.
  • Polymer decor. Light and inexpensive, but restricted by fire safety requirements and less resistant to impact and UV.

Exact product properties (weight, strength, water absorption) depend on the mix design or the stone quarry and are stated in the product data sheet or test report.

Fixing

Fixing depends on where the pilaster is installed.

  1. On a load-bearing wall (rendered facade, masonry). GFRC units are anchored to the wall through embedded fixings and additionally bedded in adhesive or mortar. Light elements may be bonded with mechanical back-up fixing.
  2. As part of a ventilated rainscreen facade. The pilaster is hung on the substructure or on separate brackets passing through the insulation to the load-bearing wall. It is important not to block the ventilation cavity: airflow must be maintained behind the unit.
  3. Half-columns. The half-shell is fixed to the wall with brackets; the capital and base are fixed separately.

Fixings are stainless or galvanised steel. Joints between pilaster segments are placed at profile levels (above the base, below the capital, at string courses) so that they are unnoticeable. The fixing design, including anchor calculations for wind load and weight, is completed before the units go into production — at TECHNOFORMA Group this is done by the in-house design department.

Combining with Rustication

Rustication consists of recessed horizontal or horizontal-and-vertical joints imitating dressed block masonry. In a classical composition, rustication and pilasters are distributed by tier: a rusticated plinth or ground floor serves as the base, with smooth walls and pilasters above. This combination conveys heaviness below and lightness above.

  • A rusticated ground floor with giant-order pilasters on the second and third floors is a typical neoclassical device.
  • Rusticated pilasters (with joints across the shaft) give a rougher, fortress-like character and suit plinths and gateways.
  • Rusticated quoins instead of pilasters are an alternative when a corner needs emphasis without an order.

The main rule is not to mix rustication and pilasters on the same tier in the same plane: a rustication joint must not run into a pilaster shaft. They are separated by a string course or cornice. Types of rustication are covered in more detail in a separate article in this section.

Pilaster or half-column: which suits a particular facade?

The choice depends on three things: how much depth the facade has available, the distance from which it will be viewed, and the load the substrate can take. A pilaster is appropriate where the wall should remain a plane with light relief; a half-column is appropriate where an accent with pronounced light and shade is needed.

Type of buildingWhat is usually usedWhat to watch
Multi-storey residential complexgiant-order pilasters on the lower tiers, plain lesenes aboveweight of elements, speed of installation, repeatability of sizes
Business centreflat pilasters and piers with a restrained profilejunctions with glazing and the cladding system
Hotel, public buildinghalf-columns at the entrance and on the main facade, pilasters on the side elevationsprojection beyond the site boundary, lighting
Private housepilasters at the corners and by the entrancescale: shaft width in proportion to the window and storey height

On tall buildings the relief of the upper storeys reads less clearly, so fine capital details above the fifth or sixth storey are, as a rule, simplified, and the projection of the shaft is slightly increased. The opposite case is a narrow street where the facade is seen only at an acute angle: here a deep half-column hides the neighbouring windows, and a flat pilaster works better. How the elements of the orders come together in an overall image is covered in the article on the classical-style facade.

The parts of a facade pilaster

A pilaster is assembled from the same parts as a column, only in flat form: a pedestal (not always), a base, a shaft and a capital. At the factory each part is made as a separate unit, and a long shaft is divided into segments convenient for transport and lifting.

  • Pedestal — a rectangular block under the base; used when the order starts above the plinth and height must be gained without lengthening the shaft.
  • Base — a set of horizontal mouldings (tori, fillets, scotias) on a plinth block. It is the base that takes splashes and snow, so it is often made of a denser material or protected with a water repellent.
  • Shaft — plain or fluted. A pilaster usually has an odd number of flutes, so that a flute runs along the axis and plain margins remain at the edges.
  • Capital — Tuscan and Doric capitals are simpler to produce; Ionic and Corinthian ones require complex tooling and careful packing.

The length of a GFRC shaft segment is most often set within one storey height or half of it — this depends on the section, the mass and the lifting method. A corner pilaster consists of two flanges at a right angle; it is better cast as a single L-shaped unit so that no joint falls on the arris itself. The forms of the upper and lower parts are described in detail in the piece on column capitals and bases, and ready solutions are shown on the page for GFRC pilasters and rustication.

Hollow GFRC units have a thin wall reinforced with stiffening ribs at the cast-in fixings. A stone pilaster is built differently: it is made up of slabs and profiled pieces, while the base and capital are carved from solid stone or bonded from several blanks. For stone, therefore, deep relief turns out noticeably heavier and harder to work than for a cast material.

What input data is needed to design pilasters?

To develop shop drawings it is enough to have architectural elevations with levels, information about the wall and about the insulation system. The more accurate the data, the less adjustment on site.

  1. Facade elevations with grid lines, floor levels and opening dimensions.
  2. Material and thickness of the load-bearing wall: in-situ concrete, brick, aerated concrete, frame with infill. This determines the anchor type and the permissible load per fixing point.
  3. Build-up of the facade system: insulation thickness, type of subframe, offset of the cladding from the wall.
  4. A sketch of the base and capital profiles, or a reference to an order and precedents.
  5. Requirements for surface colour and texture, a sample for approval.
  6. Data on the construction area: wind zone, seismicity, building height.

From this data, working drawings of the units and details are issued — the facade shop drawings set, in which every segment has a mark, a mass and a layout of cast-in fixings. For aerated concrete and hollow brick, the load is usually transferred to floor slabs or in-situ concrete bands, because such masonry holds an anchor poorly in pull-out. Before the moulds are started, it is useful to carry out a check survey of the finished structure: actual wall deviations often exceed those assumed in the design.

Typical mistakes in designing and installing pilasters and half-columns

Most defects are built in at the sketch stage and appear after one or two seasons. Below is what occurs most often in practice.

  • A pilaster without a termination. The shaft runs into the cornice without a capital or breaks off under a window — the element looks like an accidental overlay. At least a simplified profile is needed at top and bottom.
  • Unprotected horizontal ledges. Water lingers on the upper surface of the base and capital; without an outward fall and a drip, streaks appear on the shaft, and ice in winter.
  • Rigid filling of joints. The units and the wall respond differently to temperature, so joints between segments are filled with elastic sealant rather than mortar.
  • Ignoring the building's movement joints. A pilaster must not bridge a joint with a continuous element; it is either shifted or cut at the same place.
  • Fixing through insulation without calculation. A long anchor acts as a cantilever, and under its weight the unit sags over time.
  • Shade variation. Segments from different mixes or stone batches differ in tone; batches are made up for one whole pilaster.

A separate group of mistakes concerns water at the foot: a base placed directly on the paving or porch absorbs moisture and salts. It is raised on a plinth block of dense stone or separated by waterproofing.

How pilasters meet the cornice, plinth and windows

A pilaster always works together with horizontal elements: at the bottom it rests on the plinth or a string course, at the top it visually carries the entablature or cornice. At a pilaster the cornice is usually broken forward — a projection the width of the capital is made, otherwise the capital «sinks» under the overhang. Breaking the cornice forward increases the number of corner units, and this is allowed for in the schedule.

At windows the distance from the edge of the shaft to the reveal matters: the architrave and the pilaster must not overlap. If the pier is narrow, one of the two is chosen — either the order or a developed window surround. On ventilated facades the ventilation gap is kept behind the pilaster, and the top of a half-column is closed so that water cannot enter the hollow unit. Large half-columns are close in construction to free-standing supports, and the same principles apply to them as described in the article on GFRC columns.

In seismic areas and on tall buildings each segment is given its own fixing: the lower unit must not carry the weight of those above. A joint is left between segments to accommodate movement of the frame, and load-bearing and restraint brackets are separated by function — some take the weight, others hold the unit against overturning by wind.

Care of pilasters in service

Pilasters made of GFRC and stone do not need regular maintenance, but they should be inspected once a year together with the rest of the facade. The condition of the joints, the absence of cracks at fixing points, and traces of wetting under the capitals and at the base are checked.

Dirt is washed off with water at moderate pressure and a neutral detergent; abrasives and acid cleaners are unsuitable for limestone, travertine and concrete surfaces. The water-repellent impregnation is renewed at the interval stated by the product manufacturer — usually once every few years. Sealant in the joints ages faster than the units themselves, so its replacement is the most frequent repair operation. Detailed recommendations are gathered in the article on the care of GFRC products.

After winter, particular attention is paid to the lowest metre of the facade: here the units are in contact with de-icing agents, and the first chips or efflorescence appear precisely on the bases.

FAQ

What is a half-column?

A column set halfway or more into a wall. It has a base, shaft and capital, but is fixed to the wall rather than standing free.

What is a pilaster?

A flat rectangular projection of a wall with a base and capital that represents a column. On modern facades it is most often a decorative element.

How does a pilaster differ from a lesene?

A lesene is the same flat vertical projection, but without a base and capital.

Can pilasters be installed on a ventilated facade?

Yes, on the substructure or on separate brackets fixed to the load-bearing wall, keeping the ventilation cavity clear. The details are developed in the design.

What are facade pilasters made of?

GFRC, natural stone, rendered brickwork and, less often, polymers. For multi-storey buildings, non-combustibility of the material is important.

At what height can GFRC pilasters be installed?

The material itself imposes no height limit: GFRC is non-combustible and comparatively light. The limits are set by the fixing calculation — wind load increases with height, so the spacing and type of anchors on the upper storeys are determined separately.

Can pilasters be added to an existing building?

Yes, provided the wall can take the additional load. Before design the substrate is surveyed and, as a rule, anchors are pull-out tested; on insulated facades the fixing is made to the load-bearing wall, not to the render layer.

Which is more economical: a pilaster or a half-column?

A pilaster is usually more economical: it uses less material, the mould is simpler and the load on the fixings is lower. The exact difference depends on the height, the capital profile and the number of identical units in the project.
