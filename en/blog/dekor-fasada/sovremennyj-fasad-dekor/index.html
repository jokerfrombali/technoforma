Contemporary Facade: Sculpted Forms, Fins, Large Panels

A contemporary facade is a building image built not on historical ornament but on the sculpting of the volume, rhythm, texture and quality of materials. Instead of cornices and window surrounds there are large-format panels with a minimum of joints, vertical fins and ribs, deep reveals, relief and perforated surfaces, and considered lighting.

The main selection criterion is precision of execution: in minimalist architecture every uneven joint and step between planes is visible, so materials and substructure must deliver strict geometry. Such facades are chosen for office centres, residential complexes, public buildings and private houses. A general overview of decorative solutions is in Architectural facade ornament.

Trends

The main directions shaping contemporary facades of houses and public buildings:

  • Form instead of ornament. Expression comes from folds, cantilevers, bay windows and deep loggias rather than applied elements.
  • Vertical rhythm. Fins, piers and ribs set the rhythm and work as solar shading.
  • Large format. Panels a storey high or more, a minimum of visible joints.
  • Texture. Relief, fluted and "parametric" surfaces, play of light and shadow.
  • Natural tones. Stone and stone-look materials, concrete, warm metals, timber and its imitations.
  • Curved forms. Rounded corners, wavy parapets, complex three-dimensional panels where the technology allows.
  • Integrated services. Air-conditioning enclosures, vent grilles and light fittings are built into the cladding.

A minimalist facade does not mean no details — the details become technical: fine joints, concealed fixings, neat junctions.

Colour deserves a separate word. Contemporary facades are usually built on a restrained base — shades of grey, beige, sand, graphite, white — plus one accent: warm metal, dark window frames, a coloured entrance. A monochrome scheme emphasises form because relief reads through shadow rather than colour contrast. A practical rule is no more than two main cladding tones and one accent; dark colours on south elevations heat up more, and this is allowed for in movement joints.

Glazing is part of the image: large floor-to-ceiling windows, corner glazing, deep reveals, frames and fins in the same colour. A slim frame and a deep reveal in the same material as the cladding make the facade whole. Lighting brings out relief and verticals: linear fittings in recesses, fins lit from below, a lit entrance canopy. Positions for fittings and cable routes are built into the cladding layout in advance, otherwise they are cut on site.

Fins and ribs

Fins (louvres) are narrow vertical or horizontal elements set at intervals in front of the facade plane or windows. Ribs are piers and lesenes projecting from the plane. They do several jobs at once:

  • set rhythm and scale and hide mismatched windows and balconies;
  • work as solar shading, reducing overheating of rooms;
  • create privacy without blank walls;
  • change the look of the facade with viewing angle and light.

Fins are made of aluminium, steel, timber and its composite substitutes, fibre cement and GFRC. Concrete fins in GFRC look more solid than metal ones, allow complex cross-sections (trapezoid, triangle, rounded) and are non-combustible. However, they are heavier than metal, so support spacing and fixings are calculated for self-weight and wind load, especially with a large projection.

Fins on tall buildings need particular attention to wind: not only strength but also freedom from vibration and noise must be checked.

Fin spacing is chosen by facade orientation: on the south side horizontal elements block high sun more effectively; on east and west elevations vertical ones, including rotating fins, work better. Fin rhythm is best coordinated with the window grid, otherwise the elements will cover openings at random.

Large-format panels

Large-format panels are the basis of the contemporary look: the fewer the joints, the more unified and premium the facade appears. For rainscreen facades the following are used:

The advantage of GFRC is a large panel of complex shape: with a corner, reveal, relief or perforation. Such elements are made in bespoke moulds, so they pay off when repeated across a large facade. Properties of a specific mix and permissible sizes are given in the manufacturer's technical documentation.

Materials

Cladding houses with contemporary materials is usually done as a ventilated rainscreen facade: substructure, insulation, air cavity and cladding. One building often combines 2–3 materials — for example GFRC panels for form and accents, porcelain stoneware for plain areas and metal for fins and surrounds.

What to consider when choosing:

  • Fire safety. For multi-storey buildings the cladding and insulation must comply with applicable codes. According to TECHNOFORMA, its GFRC is combustibility class NG.
  • Durability of colour and surface. Integrally coloured and natural materials age more evenly than surface-painted ones.
  • Repairability. In a rainscreen system a damaged panel can be replaced.
  • Compatibility. Different materials have different thermal movement; this is allowed for in the joints.

The design freedom of GFRC lets both flat cladding and three-dimensional elements be made in one material, which simplifies junctions and colour consistency.

Can an old building be given a contemporary facade? Yes: a rainscreen system changes the look without touching the structural walls — substructure, insulation and new cladding are mounted over the old masonry. First the wall is surveyed and its capacity for anchors checked; then mismatched openings and balconies are unified with fins, window frames and large panels. Constraints are approval of the change in appearance (for apartment buildings also a decision of the owners) and junctions with existing windows and the roof.

How not to date

Fashionable facade solutions quickly become a mark of their decade. To keep a contemporary facade from looking dated in a few years, architects follow several principles:

  1. Proportions over devices. A well-found window rhythm and scale of divisions do not age, unlike random "trendy" inserts.
  2. A limited palette. Two or three materials and shades last longer than a patchwork of cladding.
  3. Natural textures. Stone, concrete and materials with natural texture age more gracefully than gloss and bright colours.
  4. Quality of details. Neat joints, junctions and drips keep the facade clean; streaks and stains age a building faster than any fashion.
  5. Scope for renewal. A rainscreen system allows part of the cladding to be replaced later without touching the walls.

Facade minimalism is especially demanding in execution: without ornament every defect shows.

Common mistakes when designing a contemporary facade:

  • too many materials and colours on one plane — the result is busy rather than concise;
  • cladding layout unrelated to windows: joints cut openings at random and narrow slivers appear;
  • air conditioners, vent grilles and downpipes not planned in the design, appearing on the facade haphazardly;
  • fins not designed for wind — vibration and noise at height;
  • glossy and brightly coloured surfaces on the sunny side — rapid fading and visible unevenness;
  • saving on the substructure with large formats — steps between planes show on long facades in raking light.

How the joint layout defines the look of a contemporary facade

In a contemporary facade the joint between panels acts as the main graphic element: in the absence of cornices and window surrounds, it is the joint grid that sets scale and order. The cladding layout is therefore developed together with the architectural concept rather than adjusted to a ready-made subframe.

The main rules followed in the layout are:

  • joints are tied to the window axes, floor slab levels and corners of the building, so that the grid coincides with the logic of the volume;
  • the joint width is kept uniform across the whole facade; at the building's movement joints it is increased deliberately and worked into the overall pattern;
  • narrow make-up strips at corners and openings are avoided: the difference is distributed between neighbouring panels;
  • horizontal joints are brought to the level of the window head or sill rather than to a random point on the pier;
  • deviations of the load-bearing structure are taken up by adjustment of the subframe, not by varying the joint width.

The layout is checked on the elevations of all facades at once, including end walls and internal corners: a joint perfectly tied to the windows of the main facade may fall in the middle of an opening on the side one. In disputed places a rule of priority helps: the grid is first defined on the main surfaces, and the secondary ones are then subordinated to it by changing the panel format within the limits acceptable for production.

A joint may be open or closed. An open joint, with a gap through which a shaded background is visible, gives a crisp line and needs no sealant, but it requires a dark backing or membrane behind it and protection of the insulation from moisture. A closed joint is filled with sealant or a gasket; it looks calmer but depends on the quality and durability of the filling. The choice is made at the design stage, since the panel edge detail and the make-up of the system depend on it.

Concealed panel fixing: what lies behind a clean surface

A smooth surface without visible rivets and clips is achieved by concealed fixing: the panel is hung on the subframe through parts mounted on its rear side. For glass fibre reinforced concrete cladding these parts are embedded elements cast into the body of the unit at the factory.

Fixing methodHow the facade looksFeatures
Visible: rivets, screws, clipsFixing points can be read on the surface and are painted to match the claddingSimple installation and replacement, suitable for flat slabs of small format
Concealed: embedded parts and hanging bracketsClean surface, only the joint is visibleRequires an accurate subframe and adjustment in three directions; the procedure for replacing a panel is thought through in advance
CombinedMain surfaces without visible fixings, secondary zones with visible onesReduces the cost of the system without loss of appearance on the principal facades

The larger the panel, the higher the demands on the subframe and on the accuracy of the embedded parts. A large-format unit must be free to move as the temperature changes, so some supports are made load-bearing and others sliding. The support scheme is calculated for each panel size, taking account of weight, wind load and installation height.

Repairability is a separate question. With concealed fixing it must be possible to remove a damaged panel without dismantling the neighbouring rows; an installation gap and a defined hanging sequence are provided for this. Spare panels of the main sizes are usually ordered together with the main batch: this guarantees a match of colour and texture, which is difficult to reproduce several years later.

The production and installation of GFRC facade panels is conveniently run from a shared model: the position of the embedded parts in the unit and of the brackets on the wall is taken from one drawing, and the panel fits into place without adjustment.

Corners, reveals and parapets: the details by which quality is judged

A minimalist facade is judged by its details: it is at the corners, reveals and junctions that the precision of design and assembly becomes visible. The plane of a wall will forgive a small inaccuracy; a corner or a reveal will not.

  • External corner. A joint of two slabs at forty-five degrees requires precise cutting and is vulnerable to chipping; a one-piece GFRC corner element removes the joint from the edge of the building.
  • Window reveal. A deep reveal in the same material as the wall creates a sense of mass; it is cast together with the face panel or made as a separate element with a concealed joint.
  • Parapet. The coping must fall towards the roof and have a drip, otherwise streaks will soon appear on the clean surface.
  • Plinth. The bottom row of cladding is separated from the paving and protected from splashing; a more resistant material, such as granite, is appropriate here.
  • Junction with glazing. The lines of the curtain wall mullions and the cladding joints are coordinated with each other.

These solutions are built into the working drawings; typical options are examined in the article on ventilated facade junction details. At the concept stage it is enough to define the principle: where the corner is in one piece, where the reveal is part of the panel, how the top of the wall is finished.

Parametric relief and 3D panels: when relief replaces ornament

Relief and parametric panels give a contemporary facade depth without historical details: the pattern is formed by a wave, a fold, facets or perforation that change from panel to panel. This technique works well on large blank surfaces such as end walls, podiums, car parks and stair and lift cores.

From the production point of view, the balance between variety and repetition is important. Every unique panel requires its own mould, so the pattern is usually built from a limited set of modules that are rotated and combined. On the facade this reads as a continuous, changing relief, while the schedule contains only a few types of unit. More on the technology can be found in the article on 3D panels and parametric facades.

Perforated panels solve several tasks at once: they screen technical zones, let air and light through, and in the evening, with internal lighting, act as a luminous screen. The proportion of open area and the minimum width of the bridges are determined by calculation and by the casting technology, so the perforation pattern is agreed with the manufacturer before the concept is approved. For car parks the required ventilation area is checked in addition.

The depth of relief is chosen with regard to the orientation of the wall. On south and west facades even a shallow relief gives expressive shadows, while on north facades a more active relief or lighting is needed. Horizontal areas of the relief are designed with a fall so that water and dust do not collect on them.

Which techniques suit different building types?

The set of techniques for a contemporary facade depends on the function of the building: a residential block, an office and a hotel place different demands on glazing, privacy, maintenance and budget.

Type of buildingTypical solutionsPoints to watch
Residential complexLarge panels, deep loggias, louvres over air-conditioning baskets, accentuated entrance groupsRepetition of panel sizes, places for air-conditioning units, resistance of the lower floors to damage
Business centreRegular grid, combination of glazing with solid panels, vertical finsInterface with curtain wall systems, solar shading, a representative entrance zone
HotelExpressive modelling, warm textures, evening lightingPrivacy of rooms, ease of cleaning, resistance to marine or urban air
Public buildingLarge scale, bespoke relief panels, extended blank surfacesDurability and repairability, fire safety requirements
Private houseThe same techniques in a smaller format, combination of panels with timber and stoneScale of panels relative to a small volume, a light subframe

Office architecture is closest to canonical minimalism; the combination of image and practical requirements in this typology is described in the article on the business centre facade. In housing the contemporary style is usually softer: more warm shades and textures, less continuous glass.

Lighting: how a contemporary facade looks in the evening

The evening appearance is designed at the same time as the daytime one: concealed luminaires need recesses, embedded parts and cable routes that cannot be added neatly after the cladding is installed. For restrained architecture, light is one of the few ways to show texture and depth after dark.

  • Flood lighting emphasises the texture and relief of the panels; grazing light reveals every unevenness, so it is used only on accurately installed surfaces.
  • Linear lighting in joints, under louvres and in reveals supports the graphics of the facade.
  • Contour lighting of fins and louvres turns them into the evening rhythm of the building.

The colour temperature of the light is matched to the material: warm light supports beige and sand textures, neutral light supports grey and white surfaces. Brightness is limited so that the light does not disturb residents and guests: on residential and hotel facades the luminaires are directed at blank areas of the wall and light spill into windows is excluded. Control scenarios, namely everyday, festive and night-time with reduced brightness, are built into the electrical design.

Access to luminaires and power supply units is thought through in advance: a removable panel or hatch must be provided in the design. The principles of selecting light are described in the article on architectural facade lighting.

What to prepare for an estimate of a contemporary facade

An architectural concept with elevations and an understanding of the wall construction are enough for a preliminary estimate. The earlier the cladding manufacturer joins the project, the easier it is to optimise the layout and the number of panel sizes without changing the appearance.

  1. Facade elevations and floor plans, and a 3D model where possible.
  2. The desired panel layout or an acceptable range of formats.
  3. Information on the load-bearing structure: wall material, floor-to-floor spacing, presence of insulation.
  4. Requirements for colour and texture, reference samples.
  5. Building height and construction region, which determine the loads and the requirements for the system.
  6. Construction programme and installation conditions.

The cost is influenced most by the format and complexity of the panels, the number of sizes, the fixing method and the height of the building. A flat panel of standard size and a three-dimensional corner element with a reveal differ noticeably in labour input, so at the concept stage it is useful to obtain an assessment of several layout options. Quite often a small change in the joint spacing reduces the number of moulds without a noticeable effect on the appearance.

From these data the panel types, fixing scheme and make-up of the subframe are determined, and then the schedule and cost estimate are prepared. A full cycle with one contractor, from design to installation of the ventilated facade, removes interface issues between the manufacturer of the units and the installation company.

FAQ

What materials are used for contemporary house facades?

GFRC, porcelain stoneware, fibre cement, metal cassettes and natural stone — most often as a ventilated rainscreen facade.

What is facade minimalism?

Giving up applied ornament in favour of clean volumes, large-format cladding, rhythm and texture.

What are facade fins made of?

Aluminium, steel, timber and composites, fibre cement and GFRC. Concrete fins are more solid-looking and non-combustible but need fixing calculations.

Can large panels of complex shape be made?

Yes, in GFRC: panels with a corner, reveal, relief or curve are made in bespoke moulds.

How can a contemporary facade be kept from dating quickly?

Limit the palette, choose natural textures, and pay attention to proportions and the quality of details.

Can an old house be made contemporary on the outside?

Yes, most often with a ventilated rainscreen facade: substructure, insulation and new cladding are installed over the existing wall. Before that the wall is surveyed for anchors and the change of appearance is approved.

Which joint is better for a contemporary facade, open or closed?

An open joint gives crisp graphics and does not depend on ageing of the sealant, but it requires well-considered protection of the insulation and a dark background behind the gap. A closed joint looks calmer and protects better against dust and precipitation, although the filling has to be renewed over time. The decision is made in the design together with the choice of system.

How many panel sizes are acceptable on one facade?

There is no strict limit, but every new GFRC panel size means a separate mould and additional time. The usual aim is to reduce the main surface to a few basic formats and to keep unique panels for corners, entrance groups and accent zones.

Can GFRC panels and curtain wall glazing be combined in one facade?

Yes, this is a common combination. It is important to coordinate the cladding joint lines with the curtain wall mullions in advance, to resolve the junction detail and to allow for the different thermal movement of the materials. These matters are settled at the working documentation stage.
