Parapets and Parapet Cladding

Parapet cladding is the finish of the outer, inner and top surfaces of the wall along the edge of a flat roof, balcony or terrace. A parapet is in the most exposed position on a building: rain washes it on three sides, snow settles on it, and its junction with the roof is one of the main sources of leaks. Parapet cladding is therefore not only about appearance but also about protecting the structure from water.

The main selection criterion is the reliability of the top protection (the parapet coping) and of the roof junction; the outer cladding material is chosen within the system of the whole facade, so that the parapet continues it rather than looking like a separate strip.

This article is for architects, general contractors and building operators. Other elements that crown a facade are in the section Architectural Facade Details.

What a Parapet Does

A parapet on a facade performs several functions:

  • Roof guarding. On accessible roofs and during equipment maintenance, the parapet protects against falls; its height is then set by the applicable codes.
  • Drainage. The parapet retains water on the roof and directs it to internal outlets or spouts, preventing run-off down the facade.
  • Roof protection. It shields the edge of the roofing membrane from wind uplift and conceals rooftop equipment.
  • Architectural finish. The parapet forms the building's upper silhouette; in classical architecture an attic or balustrade takes its place.

The parapet coping is the key element protecting the top of the wall. It can be a metal flashing, stone or concrete slabs, or profiled GFRC units. The coping is laid with a fall towards the roof, projects beyond both faces of the wall and has drips so that water does not run down the facade. If the coping leaks, water gets into the masonry and insulation, and damage appears on the top floor.

Cladding

The types of facade cladding for a parapet are the same as for the main wall surface, but allowing for harsher conditions. The main rule is to clad the parapet in the same system as the facade, so that there is no visual or technical break.

Cladding typeFeatures on a parapet
Ventilated rainscreen facade (porcelain stoneware, fibre cement, GFRC panels, stone)the substructure is taken up to the top of the parapet; the ventilation cavity is closed at the top with a perforated element beneath the coping
Rendered facadeinsulation and render rise to the coping; a reliable coping with a large overhang is needed
GFRC claddingpanels and profiles shaped to follow the cornice or attic; the coping can be of the same material

The inner face of the parapet is often clad more simply — with roof waterproofing turned up the wall, or with metal, since it is visible only from the roof. But if the roof is accessible, the inner face is finished like the outer one.

Protection from Water

Protecting a parapet from water comes down to three details.

  1. Coping. A fall towards the roof, overhangs beyond the outer and inner cladding, drips along the lower edges and sealed joints between elements. On metal copings, joints are seamed or lapped with cover strips; on stone and GFRC copings, they are sealed with sealant over an underlay membrane.
  2. Roof junction. The roofing membrane is turned up the inner face of the parapet to the height set by roof design codes and secured with a termination bar and sealant; the detail is then covered by a flashing or the coping.
  3. Cladding joints. Vertical joints of the outer cladding are sealed or left open with the substrate protected, depending on the system.

Errors in these details show up the same way: damp patches on top-floor ceilings, efflorescence and streaks on the facade, cladding damaged by water freezing in the joints. Parapet details are therefore worked out in the design as separate drawings rather than left to the installers' discretion.

On classical facades, parapets are often clad with GFRC elements: the same material as the cornices and string courses allows the profile to continue to the top of the building. Details

The main parapet details that must be in the design:

  • Coping detail — a section with fall, overhangs, drips, fixing to the wall and underlay waterproofing.
  • Roof junction detail — upstand height of the membrane, termination bar, flashing.
  • Top of the rainscreen facade — ventilation cavity outlet, protection of insulation from precipitation, a mesh against birds and debris.
  • Corner details — external and internal corners of the coping and cladding, where gaps most often remain.
  • Spouts and outlets passing through the parapet — with sleeves and sealing.
  • Fixing of guardrails, lightning conductors and facade lighting — so that anchors do not pierce the coping without sealing.
  • Movement joints — on long parapets, at the building's movement joints.

A parapet is subject to increased wind load at the building corners, so the cladding fixings there are designed separately. Dimensions of a parapet coping: fall, overhang and drip

A coping works if water runs off it onto the roof and the remainder breaks away from the edge without touching the wall. Three parameters are set for this: the fall, the overhang beyond the plane of the cladding and the drip. The values below are common guidelines; the actual dimensions are set by the design, taking account of the material and the wind zone.

ParameterGuidelinePurpose
Fall towards the roofgenerally no less than 3–5 %water and meltwater do not stand and do not run onto the facade
Overhang beyond the cladding planeusually 30–60 mm on each sidethe stream does not reach the top of the cladding or the ventilation gap
Dripa groove or downturn 15–25 mm from the edgewater breaks away rather than tracking along the underside to the wall
Length of unitabout 1–2 m for stone and GFRC, up to 2–3 m for metallimiting thermal movement, ease of installation
Joint between unitsaround 8–12 mm for sealant with stone and GFRCaccommodating movement and ensuring watertightness

The width of the coping is calculated from the full thickness of the parapet including cladding, insulation and ventilation gap, with two overhangs added. A frequent mistake is to order the coping to the width of the brick or concrete upstand, ignoring the facade layers; as a result the overhang disappears exactly where it is needed.

How are the coping and parapet cladding fixed at height?

The parapet is the zone of greatest wind suction on a building, so the coping and the top course of cladding are fixed mechanically, with a margin, and adhesive or mortar alone is not relied upon.

  • Metal coping. It is fitted on cleats or a continuous support strip at a spacing determined by calculation; the sheet clips onto the strip so that no fixings pass through the top surface.
  • Stone coping. The slabs are laid on mortar or adhesive over waterproofing and additionally secured with dowels or concealed anchors; slab thickness is set with the overhang in mind.
  • GFRC coping. A channel-section profiled unit is fitted over the upstand and fixed through cast-in inserts to anchors; a continuous waterproofing layer is maintained beneath it.
  • Cladding on a subframe. The top brackets are set at a reduced spacing, and at the corners of the building the spacing is reduced further by calculation.

Any fixing that passes through the waterproofing is sealed. In the parapet zone subframe components are made of corrosion-resistant steel or given an appropriate coating: here the metal is wet more often than on the rest of the wall. The general logic of the construction is described on the page about installing ventilated facades.

Separate thought is given to everything fixed to the parapet after the facade is handed over: railing posts, the lightning-protection mesh, cable trays, anchor points for maintenance. Each such fixing penetrates the coping or the cladding, so it is better to include the positions and the sealing method in the design in advance than to end up with dozens of unplanned holes in a finished detail.

The parapet in a classical facade: attic, balustrade, cornice

In classical and neoclassical architecture the parapet is part of the crown of the building, and its cladding continues the profile of the cornice. Three main solutions are used.

  • A solid attic: a wall above the cornice with panels, its own plinth and a small cornice serving as the coping.
  • A balustrade between pedestals. Balustrades on the facade are used on accessible roofs and terraces; the pedestals are capped with copings of the same profile as the handrail.
  • A parapet concealed behind the cornice. The crowning GFRC cornice projects so far that the upstand cannot be seen from the street, and the coping is made of metal.

In every case a decorative coping does not replace the waterproofing beneath it: the profiled unit is treated as a protective and decorative layer, while watertightness is provided by the membrane or metal underneath. The weight of the crowning elements is taken into account in the design of the parapet wall, especially if it is of thin brickwork.

Who builds the parapet detail and when: sequence of work

The parapet is the meeting point of two contractors, roofing and facade, and a significant share of leaks arise from an uncoordinated sequence. A reliable order is as follows.

  1. Acceptance of the parapet upstand: geometry, height, strength of the substrate for anchors.
  2. The roofer turns the membrane up the inner face and secures its edge.
  3. The facade contractor installs the subframe and insulation up to the top of the upstand.
  4. Waterproofing is laid across the top of the parapet, lapping onto the roof membrane and onto the wind barrier of the facade.
  5. The cladding of the outer face is installed up to the design level.
  6. The coping is fitted to a fall, and the joints and corners are sealed.
  7. Joint acceptance of the detail and, where possible, a water test.

Contracts should state explicitly who is responsible for the top waterproofing and the coping: when this layer has no owner, it is carried out as an afterthought. Junctions between the facade, the roof and openings are covered in more detail in the article on junction details.

Balconies and terraces: how their parapet differs from a roof parapet

A balcony or terrace parapet acts as a guard, so it is subject to requirements for height and for horizontal loading from people, and its inner face is constantly in view. Hence the differences in cladding.

Both faces and the top are finished as visible surfaces. The coping also serves as a handrail or as a base for one, so it is made without sharp edges, and the fall is directed inwards onto the terrace surface, from which water drains to an outlet or spout. Posts of glazing or metal railings passing through the coping are a weak point: every penetration is sealed, otherwise water gets inside the upstand. The foot of the parapet is tied into the waterproofing of the terrace floor in the same way as with a roof, by turning it up the vertical surface.

How defects in parapet cladding are identified and remedied

Parapet defects are usually visible from outside before leaks appear inside. During an inspection attention is paid to the following signs.

  • vertical streaks and dark stripes beneath the coping joints;
  • efflorescence on stone and render in the top metre of the wall;
  • tiles coming away, cracks along joints, grout falling out;
  • open or dried-out sealant joints, lifted metal components;
  • signs of wet insulation and bracket corrosion beneath the coping.

Repair begins not with the cladding but with the source of water: the fall, overhang and watertightness of the coping are restored and the roof junction is checked. Only once the construction has dried out are the damaged cladding units replaced. If the cause is not obvious, a facade survey is carried out with the detail opened up.

What to prepare for an estimate of parapet cladding?

The parapet is priced as a separate item rather than added to the facade area: it has different details, more fixings and its own waterproofing. The estimate requires information on both the facade and the roof.

  • sections through the parapet showing the height and thickness of the upstand and the substrate material;
  • a roof plan with the length of the parapet and the number of external and internal corners and movement joints;
  • the type of roof and the drainage method: internal outlets, spouts, external gutters;
  • the facade system and the wall build-up below the parapet;
  • the use of the roof: accessible or not, presence of railings, plant, lightning protection;
  • requirements for the appearance of the coping: material, colour, profile;
  • the construction region, to allow for wind and snow loads.

In the estimate the coping and trim pieces are usually measured in linear metres, the cladding of the faces in square metres, and the corners, ends and post penetrations by number. It is the individually counted items that most often go missing in preliminary calculations, although on a roof with a complex plan there may be several dozen of them.

Climate affects the solution directly. In snowy regions drifts form against the parapet, so the upturn height of the waterproofing and the reliability of the inner face are checked with particular care. In windy and coastal zones the fixing of the coping is strengthened and corrosion-resistant materials and fixings are chosen; in regions with frequent temperature swings around zero, higher demands are placed on the frost resistance of the cladding and the quality of the joints. In seismic regions heavy copings and crowning elements are additionally verified by calculation and, where possible, replaced with lightweight ones.

FAQ

What is a parapet coping?

The element capping the top of a parapet wall: a metal flashing, stone or a GFRC unit with a fall towards the roof and drips.

What should a parapet be clad with?

The same material as the facade: porcelain stoneware or panels on a substructure, GFRC, stone or render. The key is a reliable coping.

Why does a parapet leak?

Most often because of a coping without a fall or drips, unsealed joints or an incorrect roof membrane junction.

Which way should a parapet coping slope?

Towards the roof, so that water does not run down the facade.

Does the inner face of a parapet need cladding?

On a non-accessible roof it is usually covered with waterproofing or metal; on an accessible roof it is finished like the facade.

How is the width of a parapet coping calculated?

Two overhangs, usually 30–60 mm each, are added to the full thickness of the parapet including insulation, ventilation gap and cladding on both sides. The dimension is confirmed from the design detail, and on an existing building from measurements at several points.

Can a parapet coping be replaced without removing the cladding?

In many cases, yes: the coping is removed, the waterproofing across the top of the upstand is restored and a new unit with the correct overhang is fitted. The top course of cladding has to be taken off if the insulation beneath it is wet or the brackets are damaged.

How often should a parapet be inspected?

As a rule, twice a year: in spring after the snow has melted and in autumn before the frosts, and also after strong winds. The fixing and joints of the coping, the condition of the sealant and the junction of the roof membrane are checked.
