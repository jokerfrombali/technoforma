Balusters: Shapes, Sizes and Materials for Outdoor Use

Facade balusters are the shaped posts that make up a balustrade bay between the plinth and the handrail. Their profile sets the style of the guardrail, and their material determines how it will survive years of rain, frost and sun outdoors.

The main criterion for an outdoor baluster is the material's resistance to freezing water and temperature swings, and only then the shape. A baluster sits in the most vulnerable part of the facade: it is exposed to precipitation, snow lingers on the plinth, and near a porch there are also de-icing salts and knocks.

This article is useful for architects, contractors and private home owners choosing a guardrail for a balcony, terrace or staircase. Other elements of classical decor are in the section Architectural Facade Details.

Baluster Profiles

A baluster profile is formed by alternating swellings (vase, body), narrowings (neck) and fillets. The main types:

  • Vase (single) — swelling in the lower third, a slim neck at the top; a classic of the Italian Renaissance and neoclassicism.
  • Double (symmetrical) — two swellings mirrored about the middle; used in long bays and looks calm.
  • Pear-shaped — an elongated swelling, more slender, suits tall guardrails.
  • Cylindrical with rings — a simple profile for restrained facades.
  • Square in section — with arrises and panels, typical of Art Deco and modern classicism.

Concrete balusters are traditionally cast in split moulds, so their profile is limited to what can be demoulded. Outdoor GFRC balusters are cast hollow, with a through-hole for a steel rod, which lightens the unit and simplifies fixing. Stone profiles are turned on a lathe, so they can be more complex, but each baluster costs more.

When choosing a profile, look at the balustrade as a whole rather than a single baluster. Vase and pear shapes suit classical and neoclassical facades with cornices, window surrounds and rustication. On a laconic facade, cylindrical or square balusters without small rings work better. Above the second or third floor, fine profile details are not legible, so a bolder, simpler design is chosen for upper balconies.

Dimensions

Baluster dimensions are not standardised and are chosen to suit the guardrail height and chosen profile. Guideline values used in design:

ParameterGuidelineDepends on
Baluster heightapproximately 0.5–0.9 mrequired guardrail height minus plinth and handrail
Maximum width (body diameter)usually 1/4–1/5 of the heightprofile proportions
Spacingby the permissible gapfor housing with children, the gap is about 100 mm
Square block at top and bottomequal to or slightly larger than the maximum widthwidth of plinth and handrail

If the guardrail must be taller than the chosen baluster allows, the plinth or handrail is increased in height rather than the baluster stretched: an elongated vase loses its proportions. For stairs, either standard balusters with a raked plinth or special balusters with angled ends are used.

The spacing is checked at the narrowest part of the profile (the neck), where the gap is largest. Gap requirements for a specific building are set by the applicable building codes.

Materials for Outdoor Use

An outdoor baluster must withstand freeze-thaw cycles, UV, rain and impact.

MaterialOutdoor useFeatures
GFRCsuitablehollow lightweight units; according to the manufacturer, non-combustible (NG class) with high frost resistance
Natural stone (granite, marble, limestone, sandstone)suitable, depending on the stonegranite is the most durable; soft porous stones need water-repellent treatment
Cast concretesuitable with a quality mixheavy; with the wrong mix the surface scales
Gypsumnot suitabledeteriorates with moisture
Polyurethanelimitedcombustibility, brittleness in frost, fading

For multi-storey buildings and hotels GFRC is chosen more often: the units are lighter than concrete and stone, do not overload cantilevered balcony slabs and can be coloured to match any facade. Stone is justified for porches, garden stairs and terraces where the guardrail is seen close up. Frost resistance and water absorption of specific products are stated in the data sheet or test report; for stone they depend on the quarry.

Fixing

A baluster must not rely on mortar alone. A reliable arrangement is a steel rod passing through the baluster and tying the plinth to the handrail.

  1. Steel studs (stainless or galvanised) are installed in the plinth or slab at baluster spacing.
  2. Hollow balusters are slid onto the studs; the inner cavity is filled with grout if required.
  3. At the top, the rods enter the handrail or a steel section inside it.
  4. Joints between baluster, plinth and handrail are filled with a compound compatible with the unit material.

Horizontal loads from people are taken by the handrail and the piers with their frame, not by individual balusters. The bay length between piers is therefore limited by calculation. Stone balusters are set on mortar with steel dowels in their ends; on cantilevered balconies the slab is checked for the weight of the guardrail.

Studs are installed so as not to puncture the balcony or terrace waterproofing: either into embedded fixings cast in advance, into a raised plinth, or with each hole sealed. Leakage through an anchor under a balustrade is one of the typical causes of slab saturation and failure of the lower balusters.

Maintenance

Care of outdoor balusters comes down to protection from water and prompt repair of minor damage.

  • Drainage. Check that water does not stand on the plinth: blocked drainage gaps lead to constant wetting of the lower part of the balusters.
  • Water-repellent treatment. The impregnation is renewed at intervals specified by the product manufacturer; it reduces water absorption and soiling.
  • Painting. Painted units are periodically repainted with vapour-permeable facade paint. Coloured GFRC needs no painting.
  • Joints. Cracks in joints and chips are repaired with repair compound before water starts freezing inside.
  • De-icing salts. Near porches and stairs, keep salt-based de-icers off stone and concrete.

An annual inspection after winter allows problems to be caught early.

How to choose baluster sizes and shapes for a railing

The size and shape of a baluster are derived from the finished railing, not the other way round: first the required height and the span lengths are fixed, then the height is divided into plinth, baluster and handrail, and only after that is the profile chosen. This order avoids the situation in which a preferred element turns out to be too low or too massive.

  1. Establish the required railing height above finished floor level for the type of building in question.
  2. Set the plinth height and the handrail section; the remainder is the baluster height.
  3. Divide the length of the balustrade into spans between pedestals, allowing for corners, columns and movement joints.
  4. Choose the profile and the maximum body diameter so that the baluster looks neither elongated nor squat.
  5. Calculate the spacing and the number of elements per span and check the clear gap at the narrowest point of the profile.
  6. Approve a sample: profile, texture and colour.

Stair flights are a separate case. On an inclined run the baluster either remains straight and stands on a stepped plinth, or is made with raked ends to suit the pitch of the flight. The second option requires a separate mould and an exact pitch value, so it is entered in the order as a separate item; straight balusters from the landings cannot be transferred to the flight without modification.

How the railing as a whole is built and what requirements apply to it is covered in the article on balustrades on facades and terraces.

How do you calculate the number of balusters per span?

The number of balusters is calculated from the clear span between pedestals and the chosen centre-to-centre spacing. The spacing is the sum of the maximum width of the baluster and the gap between adjacent elements.

The procedure is simple: the span length is divided by a preliminary spacing, the result is rounded to a whole number, and the spacing is recalculated so that the balusters are evenly distributed and the end gaps at the pedestals equal half or one regular gap. A notional example: with a span of 2.4 m and a preliminary spacing of 0.2 m there are 12 intervals; if the end balusters are set half a spacing from the pedestals, the span takes 12 pieces. The figures are illustrative — the actual spacing is determined by the profile of the element and the gap requirements for the particular building.

  • Spans of different lengths are evened out by the number of balusters, not by changing the spacing arbitrarily: a difference in spacing between adjacent spans is noticeable.
  • Half-balusters are often placed at the pedestals — halves of the profile that abut the face of the pedestal; they finish the row and avoid an enlarged end gap.
  • A small allowance is added to the order for breakage in transit and for trimming on site.

Cross-section shapes: round, square and flat balusters

Besides the profile over their height, balusters differ in plan shape. Both the appearance of the railing and the manufacturing method depend on it.

SectionAppearanceWhere usedNotes
Rounda body of revolution, the same from all sidesclassical and neoclassical balustradesthe most common shape, simple to mould and demould
Square, facetedfaces and arrises, crisp light and shadeArt Deco, Art Nouveau, contemporary architectureeach baluster must be accurately oriented during installation
Flata slab with a profiled outline or pierced patternoriental motifs, stylisations, screensshallower railing depth; the pattern reads only from the front
Half-balusterhalf of the profile with a flat backabutments to pedestals and walls, solid parapetsworks as relief rather than as an independent post

Round and square balusters can be combined in one building if they are separated by zone: for example, round ones on the main terraces and faceted ones on the balconies of the typical floors.

The section shape also affects the spacing calculation. With a round baluster the gap is the same from any viewpoint, whereas with a square one it depends on how the element is turned: set with a face to the facade it gives a smaller gap than when turned arris-on. Flat balusters are placed closer together, and with a pierced pattern the size of the openings in the pattern itself is checked, not only the distance between the slabs.

How GFRC balusters are made and what is checked on acceptance

GFRC balusters are cast in split moulds from a master model, so every element in a batch repeats the profile with an accuracy that hand modelling cannot achieve. For complex profiles with undercuts, flexible moulds in a rigid casing are used; the types of tooling are described in more detail in the article on moulds for GFRC.

A standard profile can be chosen from the manufacturer's existing tooling, which is quicker and involves no cost for a model. If a bespoke shape is needed — from measurements of a historic baluster or from an architect's drawing — a model and a control sample are produced first; how this work is organised is described in the article on bespoke decoration from a sketch.

When a batch is accepted, the checks cover the height of the elements, the alignment of the top and bottom ends, the absence of voids and chipped arrises, and the uniformity of the shade. Deviations in height matter most: the handrail rests on the whole row, and balusters of unequal height have to be compensated with mortar, which shows at the joint.

Colour, texture and surface protection

Texture and shade are chosen to suit the rest of the facade decoration: a baluster should not differ from the cornices, architraves and pedestals of the same balustrade. A smooth surface for painting or a natural stone texture — limestone or sandstone — is used most often.

There are two ways of obtaining colour: integral pigmentation and a facade coating on the finished element; each has its own renewal requirements, which are compared in the article on colouring GFRC. Water lingers longer on the horizontal fillets of the profile and at the base, so the resistance of the material to repeated freezing is decisive here; what lies behind the grade is explained in the article on the frost resistance of facade materials.

Before dispatch the elements are usually cured until they gain strength and are packed so that the necks and fillets do not touch one another: the thin parts of the profile are vulnerable in transit. On site the balusters are stored upright or on soft spacers, under cover, and painted surfaces are kept away from cement dust and mortar — such soiling is difficult to remove without a trace.

Common mistakes in choosing and ordering balusters

Mistakes are more often linked not to the quality of the elements but to their being ordered in isolation from the railing design.

  • Choosing from a photograph without reference to the railing height: the baluster turns out lower than required and the difference is made up with a disproportionately high plinth.
  • A profile that is too massive for a narrow balcony: the railing takes up usable width and makes the facade look heavier.
  • Calculating the quantity from the overall length without dividing it into spans — pedestals, corners and half-balusters are not taken into account.
  • Ordering without specifying the fixing method: the element has no channel for a rod of the required diameter.
  • A repeat order from a different batch without a control sample — a noticeable difference in shade.
  • Cast concrete balusters on a cantilever slab without a load check.

Almost all of these mistakes are eliminated at the stage of a balustrade drawing with a span-by-span layout.

What to prepare for a baluster quotation

The cost depends on the profile, the quantity and the site conditions, so the quotation is based on the project input data. The more complete the set, the more accurate the result at the first attempt.

  • A plan of the terrace, balcony or staircase with the lengths of the runs and the positions of the corners.
  • The required railing height and the finished floor level.
  • The desired profile: a catalogue item, a sketch, a photograph of a reference or a measured drawing.
  • Texture and colour, and whether there is an approved sample of other facade elements.
  • The type of substrate: reinforced concrete slab, masonry, steelwork.
  • The site address and the desired delivery dates.
  • Whether installation and the design of fixing details are required.

From these data a schedule is drawn up: the number of regular balusters, half-balusters and pedestals, the linear metres of plinth and handrail, and the fixings. It is worth checking the schedule against the span-by-span layout before production starts — correcting the quantity at this stage is easier than ordering additional elements later.

FAQ

Which balusters are best for outdoor use?

GFRC, dense natural stone or quality concrete. Gypsum is not suitable outdoors; polymers only to a limited extent.

Concrete or GFRC balusters — which to choose?

Cast concrete balusters are heavier. GFRC units are hollow, lighter and do not overload balcony slabs; a frame inside provides strength.

How tall are balusters?

Usually about 0.5–0.9 m. The height is chosen so that, together with the plinth and handrail, the required guardrail height is achieved.

How are outdoor balusters fixed?

On steel studs running through the baluster from plinth to handrail. Mortar serves for filling and bedding, not as the only fixing.

Do balusters need treatment after installation?

Water-repellent treatment is recommended, and for painted units periodic renewal of the coating.

How do I work out how many balusters a balcony needs?

The length of each span between pedestals is divided by the centre-to-centre spacing and rounded to a whole number, then the spacing is adjusted so that the elements are evenly distributed. Pedestals, corners and half-balusters are counted separately.

Can a baluster be made to match one from an old building?

Yes. Measurements or a mould are taken from a surviving baluster, and a master model and a control sample are produced. After approval the profile is reproduced in the required quantity.

What is a half-baluster and why is it needed?

It is half of the profile with a flat back. It is placed tight against a pedestal or a wall so that the row finishes neatly and the end gap is no larger than the others.
