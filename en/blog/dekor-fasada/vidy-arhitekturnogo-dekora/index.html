Types of Architectural Facade Ornament: A Complete Guide

Architectural facade ornament comprises the elements that divide the wall surface, emphasise floors, openings and corners, protect the structure from water and shape the building's style: cornices, string courses and mouldings, pilasters and columns, window and door surrounds, balustrades, rustication, brackets and sculptural inserts.

The main criterion for choosing décor is not an individual element but the system: proportions, rhythm and profiles must be coordinated across the whole facade, otherwise even expensive details look random. The second criterion is material: for multi-storey buildings, weight, durability and non-combustibility matter.

This guide is for architects, developers and homeowners. An overview of the topic is in Architectural facade décor.

Main groups of elements

Decorative facade elements are conveniently grouped by function and position. The names come from the classical architectural tradition but are also used in contemporary design:

Decorative facade elements are made of natural stone, GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete), polymer concrete, polyurethane and coated expanded polystyrene, and for private houses sometimes timber. Timber elements need regular protection and are not used on multi-storey buildings because they are combustible. A comparison of materials is given in a separate article linked below.

Horizontal divisions

Horizontal elements determine how a facade "reads" vertically. The classical scheme is tripartite: the plinth (base), the main wall field and the crowning element (a cornice with frieze and architrave, or a simplified profile).

  • Plinth — the lower part, often clad in stone or finished with rustication; protects the wall from splashes and mechanical damage.
  • String courses and mouldings — horizontal profiles separating floors or groups of floors; on multi-storey buildings they help to visually "compress" the height.
  • Crowning cornice — the most expressive and largest element, shedding water away from the wall.
  • Parapet — a continuation of the wall above the roof, sometimes with a balustrade.

Every horizontal element has a practical function — shedding water from the facade plane. That is why the top of the profile is given a fall and drip edge, and joints between elements are sealed or have flashings beneath them.

Rustication is another horizontal division technique: the wall is divided by horizontal, or horizontal and vertical, joints imitating masonry of large blocks. It is usually applied on the plinth and ground floors to visually "weight" the base of the building. On rainscreen facades, rustication is made with GFRC panels with profiled edges.

Vertical elements

Vertical elements set the rhythm of the facade and emphasise the axes of symmetry and the corners of the building.

  • Columns — free-standing or attached supports with a base, shaft and capital. Proportions and details are determined by the order: Tuscan, Doric, Ionic, Corinthian or Composite.
  • Engaged columns and pilasters — a column half-embedded in the wall, and a flat projection of the wall with a base and capital. On contemporary facades, GFRC pilasters often mask cladding joints or emphasise vertical axes.
  • Lesenes — simplified pilasters without bases or capitals.
  • Quoins — alternating projecting blocks at the corners of a building that visually "strengthen" the corner.

When designing columns and pilasters for a multi-storey building, it is important to match their scale to the number of storeys and the window spacing: classical proportions transferred unadapted to a 15-storey building produce elements that are either too slender or disproportionately massive.

Window and door surrounds

Opening surrounds are the most numerous part of the décor: on a multi-storey building there are hundreds of identical elements, and they have the greatest effect on budget and schedule.

  • Window surround (architrave) — a profiled frame around the window perimeter.
  • Window pediment (hood moulding) — a small cornice above a window: straight, segmental (arched) or triangular. Sheds water from the head of the opening.
  • Keystone — an emphasised central element above an arched or straight opening.
  • Sill, apron, sill brackets — finishing and protection of the bottom of the opening.
  • Portal — an entrance surround, sometimes with columns and a pediment.

The layout takes into account the tolerances of window openings on the actual building: surrounds are made with adjustable joints or to site measurements. To save cost, a modular approach is used — the vertical and horizontal parts of a surround are made separately and assembled to fit different window sizes.

Balustrades on balconies, terraces and roofs combine decorative and protective functions. GFRC balusters and handrails are mounted on a metal frame or cast-in inserts designed for horizontal load — height and strength requirements for railings are set by Russian building safety codes.

Sculptural décor

Sculptural décor — capitals, rosettes, mascarons, cartouches, bas-reliefs, coats of arms, vases on parapets — makes a facade recognisable and requires the most work from modellers. The production process: architect's sketch → 3D model or sculpted plasticine original → master model → flexible mould → casting.

Architectural facade décor of this level in GFRC achieves detail close to stone at a weight acceptable for a rainscreen facade. Facade décor from different manufacturers, including well-known polymer brands, differs above all in material: for sculptural details at height, frost resistance, UV resistance and non-combustibility matter. At TECHNOFORMA, models and moulds are made in the company's own mould shops.

Viewing distance is especially important for sculptural décor: a fine detail that looks good in a sketch cannot be read from the ground at tenth-floor height. So relief depth and scale are matched to the installation height.

How to choose decoration to suit the style of the building?

The set of architectural elements is determined by style: the same cornices and window surrounds have a different profile, richness and rhythm in classical, Art Deco and contemporary architecture. Before selecting the units, the style and the level of facade detailing are fixed.

StyleTypical elementsRichness of decoration
Classical and neoclassicalAn order with columns and pilasters, a developed cornice, window pediments, balustrades, rusticated plinthHigh, with a clear hierarchy by floor
Art DecoVertical lesenes and pylons, stepped terminations, geometric relief, decorative panelsMedium, with accents at entrances and at the top of the building
Art NouveauCurvilinear surrounds, floral relief, mascarons, asymmetryMedium or high, many bespoke forms
Contemporary architectureLarge panels, louvres, deep reveals, parametric reliefLow, expressiveness through modelling and joints
Oriental stylesArches, portals, openwork screens, ornamental panelsHigh, concentrated on portals and openings

Residential complexes most often use a simplified classical language, described in detail in the article on neoclassicism in residential facades. For commercial and public buildings the geometry described in the article on Art Deco facades is popular. Mixing styles is acceptable but must be deliberate: one system of profiles per building rather than a collection of unrelated details.

Scale of decoration: how element size depends on the number of storeys

Element sizes are set from the height of the building and the distance from which it is viewed; they are not transferred from a low-rise prototype. A cornice that suits a three-storey mansion turns into a thin line on a twenty-storey block.

  • The crowning cornice is related to the full height of the facade: the taller the building, the greater the height and projection of the termination.
  • On a multi-storey building the order is often made giant, with columns or pilasters uniting two or three floors.
  • Fine detailing is concentrated in the lower tier, where it is seen at close range; higher up the profiles are enlarged and simplified.
  • Elements on the upper floors are checked in a 3D model from pedestrian eye level: foreshortening reduces the visible height of the details.

Scale is also linked to window spacing. On a facade with frequent narrow openings, wide surrounds merge into a continuous band, while on sparse large windows a thin frame is lost. The width of surrounds, the height of string courses and the projection of cornices are therefore chosen within one system of proportions: usually a module derived from the storey height or the opening width is taken as the basis, and all dimensions are expressed through it.

A mistake in scale is costly because it is discovered after installation. For this reason key fragments, such as the cornice, the window surround and the capital, are often checked with a full-size mock-up or a sample lifted to the design level.

From sketch to installation: the sequence of work on facade decoration

Work on the architectural decoration of a facade follows a clear sequence: first the system, then the units, then the details. Skipping any step usually comes back as rework on site.

  1. Architectural concept: style, divisions, list of elements and their position on the elevations.
  2. Development of profiles and a 3D model: sections, scale and the junctions between elements are refined.
  3. Working documentation and shop drawings: drawings of every unit, embedded parts, fixing details, a marked layout.
  4. Production of models and moulds, control samples for approval of texture and colour.
  5. Series production with deliveries sorted by work section and floor.
  6. Installation: setting-out, fitting of brackets, hanging of units, sealing of joints.
  7. Handover and transfer of as-built documentation.

When facade design, production and installation are handled by one organisation, the unit drawings take the casting technology and the fixing method into account from the start. At TECHNOFORMA Group this work is done by 12 design engineers and 2 architects, and installation is carried out by an in-house team of more than 100 specialists.

How decorative elements are fixed to the wall

The fixing method depends on the mass of the element, its projection and the wall construction. The general principle is the same: the load is transferred to the load-bearing substrate, whether in-situ concrete, masonry or frame, and not to the insulation or the finishing layer.

  • Light linear units, such as mouldings, string courses and bands, are fixed with anchors and adhesive or on brackets, depending on the section.
  • Cornices and pediments with a large projection are fixed on steel brackets designed for weight, wind and snow.
  • Columns and pilasters are assembled from sections around a load-bearing core or hung on the wall through embedded parts.
  • On a ventilated facade the decoration has its own brackets passing through the insulation, with the air gap maintained.

The substrate is surveyed before installation: the strength of the wall at the anchor positions, deviations from the vertical and the actual levels are checked. On old and non-uniform walls the load capacity of the anchors is confirmed by tests, and the spacing and type of fixings are refined from the results.

Fixings are made of stainless or galvanised steel. In thin-walled glass fibre reinforced concrete units the embedded parts are cast into the body of the piece at the factory, which allows installation without drilling the face. Long elements are divided by movement joints filled with sealant.

Colour and texture: how the elements are matched to the wall

Decoration is perceived together with the plane of the wall, so its colour and texture are chosen at the same time as the main cladding. Two approaches are possible: contrast, where light elements stand out against a darker background, and monochrome, where the details and the wall share one tone and work by shadow alone.

  • A contrasting scheme emphasises the drawing of the facade but requires careful proportions: any inaccuracy is visible from a distance.
  • A monochrome scheme is calmer and more contemporary; profile depth and the orientation of the facade are important for it.
  • A stone-like texture is appropriate next to the natural stone of the plinth, a smooth one next to render and large panels.

The way the colour is obtained also matters. Integral colouring gives an even tone that is not exposed by small chips, while surface painting makes it possible to hit a specified shade exactly and to renew it during repairs. For elements on which water lingers, such as cornices, string courses and sill mouldings, a water-repellent treatment is applied in addition: it reduces soiling and makes cleaning easier.

Colour is agreed from samples in daylight and preferably on site: the same shade looks different on the sunny and the shaded side. The approved sample is kept as a reference until the delivery is complete.

Where decoration is justified and where the budget is better reduced

Decoration is distributed unevenly over the facade: the maximum where it is seen at close range and shapes the image, the minimum on secondary surfaces. This approach preserves the appearance within a reasonable budget.

  • Main facade, entrance groups and lower floors: the full set of elements and finer detailing.
  • Typical floors: repeated surrounds in one or two sizes.
  • Courtyard facades and end walls: string courses and the cornice are kept, surrounds are simplified.
  • Top of the building: a large cornice or attic that is visible from afar and completes the silhouette.

A further reserve lies in the right choice of unit length. Linear elements of standard length with a minimum of make-up pieces are produced and installed faster, and the number of joints on the facade is reduced. It is not worth economising on fixings, sealants and protection of top surfaces: these items account for a small share of the budget but determine the service life of the decoration.

Cost is influenced most by the number of unique moulds and the complexity of the relief rather than by the number of units. Standardising profiles, grouping openings by size and avoiding one-off elements give a noticeable saving; the techniques are examined in detail in the article on how to reduce the facade budget.

Typical mistakes in choosing architectural elements

Most mistakes are related not to the quality of individual units but to the absence of a system. The list below is convenient as a checklist before the design is approved.

  • Elements from different catalogues with mismatched profiles and proportions.
  • Scale taken from a low-rise example without checking at the actual height.
  • Horizontal details without a fall and a drip, which become a source of streaks.
  • Decoration designed without fixing details and without regard to the wall construction.
  • Combustible materials where the regulations require non-combustible ones.
  • Too many sizes: production time and the number of moulds increase.
  • No movement joints in long string courses and cornices.

If decoration is selected from a ready-made range, it is useful to consult in advance the recommendations in the article on how to choose decoration from a catalogue: it helps to relate unit sizes to the scale of a particular facade.

FAQ

What types of decorative facade elements are there?

Cornices, string courses and mouldings, columns and pilasters, window surrounds and pediments, balustrades, rustication, brackets, capitals, rosettes and other sculptural details.

What are the elements above windows on a facade called?

A window pediment (hood moulding) is the small cornice above the window, the keystone is the central element, and the surround (architrave) frames the perimeter.

What are decorative facade elements made of?

Natural stone, GFRC, polymer concrete, polyurethane, coated expanded polystyrene; for multi-storey buildings non-combustibility matters.

How does a pilaster differ from an engaged column?

A pilaster is a flat projection of the wall; an engaged column is round in section and half-embedded in the wall.

Why does a facade need cornices and string courses?

They divide the facade by height and shed water away from the wall surface.

Can decoration in different materials be combined on one facade?

Yes, this is common practice: the plinth and entrance groups are clad in natural stone, while the cornices, surrounds and columns above are made of glass fibre reinforced concrete. It is important that the profiles belong to one system and that colour and texture are matched from samples.

Where to start if there is only a sketch of the facade?

Elevations with dimensions and the positions of decoration marked are sufficient. From these the design engineers select or develop profiles, determine the number of sizes and prepare a preliminary estimate. Fixing details and working drawings of the units are produced at the next stage.

When should decoration be ordered so as not to delay the facade works?

Design and mould-making are best started before the installers move on to the facade: new profiles need time for models, tooling and approval of samples. Specific lead times depend on the volume, the number of sizes and the complexity of the relief, and are determined in the estimate.
