How to Choose Decor from a Catalogue and Get the Scale Right

A facade decor catalogue is a list of standard elements (cornices, mouldings, window surrounds, columns, balusters, brackets, rustication) with profile drawings and dimensions, from which an architect or client selects decor without developing custom moulds. Standard products are cheaper and quicker to produce, but they must be correctly fitted to the scale of the particular facade.

The main selection criterion is how the element's size matches the building's size and the distance from which it is seen: a cornice proportioned for a two-storey house will get lost on a twelve-storey one. Catalogues are used by architects, developers and private homeowners; for large and unique projects they are supplemented with custom elements. An overview of decor types is in the section Architectural facade decor.

Working with a catalogue

A catalogue is useful if you read it as a set of drawings rather than pictures. How to work with it:

  1. Define the scope. From the facade, list the element types you need: crowning cornice, floor band, window surrounds, sill slabs, corners, plinth.
  2. Look at the profile, not the photo. Each element has its height, projection and product length. A catalogue photo may have been taken on a building of a completely different scale.
  3. Check compatibility. The profiles of cornices, bands and surrounds should come from one stylistic 'family'; it helps when the catalogue groups them by style or series.
  4. Clarify the technical data. Weight per linear metre or per piece, fixing method, drip and slope, material and its properties from the product data sheet.
  5. Calculate quantities. Linear metres of linear elements, number of individual pieces, corners (external and internal), end pieces.

The result should be not a list of item codes but a layout of elements on the facade — that is the only way to see how they work together.

Scale versus building height

Scale is the main cause of poor choices. An element that looks impressive in a photo becomes a thin line on a tall building. Guidelines for selection:

BuildingCrowning corniceFloor bandsWindow surrounds
Private house, 1–2 storeysModerate projection, proportioned to wall heightThin string courses or noneNarrow, with a simple profile
Low-rise building, 3–5 storeysPronounced, with a friezeAbove the plinth and below the corniceMedium width, with pediments on the principal floor
Multi-storey building, 9+ storeysLarge, often with an attic; designed to be read from the streetEvery few floors, at the boundaries of the partsEnlarged frames uniting several floors

There are no exact universal ratios — they depend on the style, street width and viewpoint. Classical practice relates the height of the crowning cornice to the height of the whole building or order, not of a single floor. That is why for a multi-storey building a standard element often has to be enlarged or built up from several profiles.

A reliable check is a 3D facade visualisation with the real element sizes and views from ground level.

Standard and custom elements

Standard catalogue elements and custom products complement each other.

  • Standard — standard profiles of cornices, mouldings, balusters, columns and capitals. The mould already exists, so there is no master model cost, and lead time depends only on the workshop's workload. Suitable for private houses and projects where uniqueness is not required.
  • Custom — elements to the architect's sketch: a non-standard profile, a special size, a signature ornament. They need a model and mould, but in a large series the mould cost is spread over all products.
  • Adapted — a standard profile modified in length or projection, or with an added reveal and drip. An intermediate option that often turns out to be optimal.

Large projects usually combine all three approaches: standard elements are catalogue or adapted, accent elements (portal, crown, corner towers) are custom. TECHNOFORMA makes models and moulds in its own mould shops, so custom elements are produced on the same site as serial ones.

Samples

A catalogue and a visualisation do not convey texture and colour as they will look on the facade. So before ordering the series it is worth looking at samples:

  • Material sample — a piece with the required texture (smooth, honed, stone-look) and colour. Checked in daylight, next to the wall material.
  • Element sample — a real product or full-size profile fragment. Lets you judge the depth of shadow and crispness of arrises.
  • Trial installation — fitting several elements on the facade or a mock-up to see the scale from a distance.

It is important to record the approved sample: it becomes the benchmark for accepting the batch. The colour of concrete and stone products can vary slightly from batch to batch, so acceptable tolerances are agreed in advance. For natural stone a sample is especially important — pattern and shade vary even within one quarry.

It is convenient to keep the samples on site until installation is complete — every delivery is checked against them.

Approval with the architect

Choosing decor from a catalogue without an architect is a frequent cause of mistakes: elements are picked by taste rather than by the building's proportions. Even for a private house it is worth having a specialist check the selection.

What is agreed with the architect:

  • the layout of elements on all facades, including corners and junctions;
  • sizes and projection relative to building height and street width;
  • junctions with windows, roof, balconies and drainpipes;
  • colour and texture in relation to the other finishes;
  • compliance with the design and, where required, urban planning requirements.

For apartment and public buildings, changing the facade's appearance requires approval of the architectural and urban planning solution in the procedure established in the particular city. Which parameters should be checked in an element's data sheet?

Height and projection are not the only things that matter in a catalogue entry. To decide whether an element is applicable, one needs to understand how it is made, how much it weighs and how it is fixed, since both the design and the estimate depend on this.

ParameterWhat it showsWhat it affects
Profile height and projectionOverall dimensions of the sectionScale, depth of shadow, lever arm of the load
Unit lengthSize of one sectionNumber of joints, ease of transport and lifting
Mass per linear metre or per unitLoad on the fixingType of anchors, bracket spacing, suitability of the substrate
Fixing methodCast-in inserts, adhesive with dowels, subframeApplicability on a ventilated or rendered facade
AccessoriesAvailability of corner, end and radius elementsWhether corners and bay windows can be handled without bespoke moulds
Material and textureComposition, appearance of the faceDurability, fire requirements, match with the wall

If any information is missing from the catalogue, it is requested from the manufacturer before the element enters the design. This applies above all to the mass and the fixing detail: the structural engineer uses them to determine whether the wall or subframe will carry the chosen element. Differences between materials in weight, durability and combustibility are examined in detail in the article on what facade decoration is made of.

How can a stylistically consistent set be assembled from a catalogue?

Elements are selected not one by one but as a family: the cornice, band, window surround and plinth should have related profile designs. A facade made up of details from different series looks accidental, even if each detail is good in itself.

  • One character of mouldings. If the cornice is built on a cyma and fillets, the same motifs are repeated in the bands and window surrounds.
  • Hierarchy of sizes. The largest element is the crowning cornice, followed by the string courses, then the surrounds and minor mouldings. Adjacent steps should differ noticeably in profile height.
  • Coordinated projections. A surround should not project further than the band it abuts, nor a pilaster further than the cornice above it.
  • Uniform texture and colour. All decorative elements are ordered in one finish, preferably from one manufacturer, to avoid variation in tone.

The set of items required depends on the style. A list of elements and their roles on the facade is given in the guide to types of architectural decoration, and the make-up of a typical set for classical architecture in the article on which elements a facade in the classical style requires.

From facade to schedule: the selection procedure step by step

Selecting decorative elements from a catalogue ends not with a choice of item codes but with a schedule giving quantities and layout. A sequence that avoids returning to stages already completed is as follows.

  1. Prepare elevations of all facades with opening dimensions, storey levels and corner positions.
  2. Mark out the horizontals and verticals: where cornices and bands run, which windows receive surrounds, whether there are pilasters and rustication.
  3. Select the crowning cornice from the catalogue, as it sets the scale for the other elements.
  4. Select bands, surrounds, sill elements and corner details of the same series to match it.
  5. Draw the chosen profiles on the elevation at their actual dimensions and check joints, corners and junctions.
  6. Check the ground-level view on the model and replace individual items where necessary.
  7. Compile the schedule and submit it for costing together with data on the substrate.

The fourth and fifth steps often have to be repeated: on the elevation it becomes apparent that a surround runs into a band or that the cornice does not clear a downpipe. If the facade is complex, it is sensible to include this work in the facade design, where the layout of decorative elements is coordinated with the subframe and fixing details.

How is the quantity of decorative elements calculated from a catalogue?

Linear elements are counted by length with allowance for corners and joints, individual pieces by the number of installation points. Counting errors are usually caused not by arithmetic but by forgotten items.

  • Cornices, bands, string courses. The length is taken from the elevation separately for each profile and divided by the section length; corner and end elements are counted separately, by the piece.
  • Window surrounds. These are counted by window type: perimeter of the surround, number of corner joints, presence of a pediment, keystone and sill part. Configuration options are shown on the page for window surrounds and architraves.
  • Pilasters, columns, brackets. These are counted by the piece, broken down into base, shaft and capital.
  • Rustication. This is counted by area, with corner stones identified separately.
  • Reserve. An allowance is made for cutting and possible damage in transit; its size is agreed with the manufacturer.

Make-up elements are entered as a separate line: short sections that close the remaining length between full units. For profiles of small section, such as mouldings, bands and string courses, make-up pieces are usually obtained by cutting on site; for large cornices they are made to size.

What does a catalogue not show?

A catalogue describes the product, not the building on which it will be installed. Some questions can therefore be resolved only in the design, and they must not be left until installation.

  • Substrate. One and the same element is fixed differently to cast in-situ concrete, brick, aerated concrete and a ventilated subframe.
  • Loads. A catalogue does not take account of wind pressure at height and in corner zones, or of snow on projecting profiles.
  • Water run-off. Falls, flashings and drips are specified on the spot with regard to adjacent structures.
  • Tolerances. Real walls have deviations, and the layout is adjusted following a survey.
  • Fire requirements. Whether a material is permissible depends on the height and use of the building.
  • Logistics. Packing dimensions and the method of unloading and lifting to level influence the choice of section length.

For this reason a standard element on a multi-storey building is almost always accompanied by a bespoke fixing detail. The profile itself remains a catalogue item, while the cast-in inserts, brackets and flashings are developed for the specific project.

How does selection differ for a private house, a residential complex and a public building?

The catalogue is the same, but the approach to it differs: on a private house proportion and ease of installation matter most, on a large project it is series production, fire requirements and coordination with the ventilated system.

  • Private house. Elements are viewed at close range, so crisp arrises and surface quality are important. The set is small, standard items are usually sufficient, and fixing directly to the wall is possible.
  • Residential complex. The volume is measured in kilometres of linear units and thousands of individual pieces. A standard profile is taken as the basis, enlarged to suit the number of storeys and standardised across blocks; the key issues are fixing to the subframe and non-combustibility of the material.
  • Hotel, business centre. Ordinary elements are supplemented with accent pieces: a portal, the crown of the building, corner compositions. The share of bespoke moulds is higher and the requirements for colour consistency are stricter.
  • Public or educational building. The decoration is restrained and large in scale; durability and resistance of the lower tier to mechanical impact come to the fore.

The larger the project, the less the catalogue works as a ready-made solution and the more it serves as a library of profiles from which the designer starts. The saving is nevertheless retained: adapting an existing model is, as a rule, quicker and cheaper than developing a profile from scratch.

Which mistakes are most often made when selecting decorative elements from a catalogue?

Most mistakes arise because the decision is taken from an image rather than from a layout on the facade. Before placing an order it is useful to go through a short list of checks.

  • Whether all elements are shown on the elevation at their actual dimensions rather than as schematic lines.
  • Whether corners, ends, breaks and curved runs have been taken into account.
  • Whether surrounds and bands clash with downpipes, ventilation grilles or air-conditioner brackets.
  • Whether the projections of adjacent elements are coordinated with one another.
  • Whether the mass and fixing method of each item are known and the substrate is suitable for them.
  • Whether colour and texture samples have been approved.
  • Whether delivery dates match the facade finishing programme.

A separate mistake is excess. A catalogue tempts one to add elements, and the facade ends up overloaded. It is safer to begin with a minimum set of cornice, band and window surrounds, and to introduce further details only where they solve a specific compositional task: emphasising the entrance, the axis of symmetry or the corner of the building.

FAQ

What is a facade decor catalogue?

A list of standard elements with profile drawings and dimensions, from which decor is selected without making custom moulds.

How do I know if an element fits the scale?

Compare its dimensions with the building height and viewing distance, and check it on a 3D facade visualisation with a view from ground level.

Which is cheaper: standard or custom decor?

Standard — no model or mould is needed. Custom pays off in a large series, when the mould cost is spread over many products.

Can the size of a catalogue element be changed?

Often yes: a standard profile is adapted in length or projection, or a reveal or drip is added.

Do I need a sample before ordering?

It is advisable: a sample shows the real colour, texture and profile depth and serves as the benchmark for batch acceptance.

Can elements from different catalogues be combined on one facade?

Technically yes, but there is a risk of discrepancies in profile design, texture and shade. If this cannot be avoided, elements from different manufacturers are assigned to different tiers of the facade, and samples must be compared side by side in daylight.

What should be done if the catalogue has no corner element for the chosen profile?

The corner is either made separately in a bespoke mould or assembled from two sections cut at an angle, with the joint subsequently made good. The first option is more reliable for large profiles and for ventilated facades; the solution is agreed with the manufacturer.

Which element should the selection begin with?

With the crowning cornice: it is the largest and sets the scale and profile character for the remaining details. After it the string courses are selected, then the window surrounds and minor elements.
