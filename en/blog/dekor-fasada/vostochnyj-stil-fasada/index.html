Oriental-Style Facades: Arches, Ornaments, Stone

An oriental-style facade is architecture rooted in the traditions of Islamic, Persian, Arab and Central Asian building: pointed and horseshoe arches, deep galleries and loggias, carved screens, geometric and floral ornament, light stone. Such a facade is both decorative and functional — it protects against sun and overheating.

The main selection criterion is accuracy of ornament and suitability for the climate: an intricate pattern must be reproduced well, and materials must withstand local conditions, whether southern heat or continental winters. The style is chosen for hotels, residential complexes, public and religious buildings, and private residences. An overview of all elements is in the section Architectural facade decor.

Elements of the style

'Oriental style' is an umbrella term. The architecture of the Maghreb, Persia, Central Asia, the Ottoman Empire and the Caucasus differs noticeably, but the facades share common features:

  • Arched openings — pointed, horseshoe, multifoil and ogee arches for windows, entrances and galleries.
  • The portal (pishtaq) — a tall arched entrance niche in a rectangular frame, often richly ornamented.
  • Carved screens (mashrabiya, panjara) — openwork screens on windows and balconies.
  • Ornament — geometric (girih), floral (islimi), calligraphic.
  • Domes and minaret-like towers — on public and religious buildings, sometimes as accents on hotels.
  • Courtyards and galleries — the facade faces not only outward but also onto the courtyard.
  • Light stone and render with coloured accents — tiles, mosaic, painting.

Arab-style facades usually have flatter screens and blank walls, Persian ones rich portals and tilework, Central Asian ones monumental portals and domes.

Arches and screens

The arch is the main structural motif of an oriental facade. It defines the shape of windows, entrances, galleries and niches. Design takes into account:

  • The arch shape — it determines the style: pointed is closer to the Persian and Ottoman tradition, horseshoe to the Maghrebi, multifoil to the Andalusian.
  • The frame — archivolt, portal frame, ornamental border.
  • Depth — gallery and loggia arches create shade and protect from the sun.

Screens provide solar shading, ventilation and privacy. In modern buildings they are made of:

GFRC arch elements — archivolts, surrounds, gallery columns — are cast in moulds; when repeated across a facade, one mould serves the whole series.

Ornaments

Oriental ornament on a facade is executed in several ways: relief, carving, mosaic, tiles, openwork perforation. The main types:

  • Girih — a geometric pattern of stars and polygons built on strict rules of symmetry.
  • Islimi — a floral ornament of interlacing stems, leaves and spirals.
  • Calligraphy — inscriptions within friezes and portals.
  • Muqarnas — 'stalactite' honeycomb vaulting in niches and under cornices.

Precision of construction is critical for ornament: a badly executed joint in a repeating module is immediately visible. In GFRC production an ornamental panel is designed as a repeating module that joins its neighbours without breaking the pattern. The master model is made from precise digital geometry, and the mould allows identical products to be produced.

Ornament relief is calculated for the viewing distance: fine detailing suits entrance level, while upper floors need a bolder pattern and deeper shadows.

Materials

Traditionally, oriental facades were built of local stone, brick, clay and render with tile and mosaic inserts. Today the main materials are:

  • Natural stone — limestone, sandstone, shell limestone (coquina), travertine, marble, granite. TECHNOFORMA has extensive experience with Dagestan stones, imports stone from India, Turkey, China and Iran, and processes both soft rocks (sandstone, shell limestone, dolomite, limestone) and hard ones.
  • GFRC — for arches, columns, portals, openwork screens and ornamental panels; it reproduces complex geometry at a lower weight than stone. According to the company, GFRC is non-combustible (Russian class NG) and frost-resistant.
  • Ceramics and tiles — for coloured accents.
  • Metal — screens, railings, dome coverings.

Combining stone for plain surfaces with GFRC for complex decor gives an authentic look with reasonable lead times and structural loads.

Climate and choice

Oriental architecture was born in a hot climate, and many of its techniques — deep galleries, screens, light walls — aim to protect from the sun. When transferring the style to other regions, local conditions must be considered.

  • Southern Russia, the Caucasus. The style's techniques work as intended: shading, ventilation, light cladding. Resistance to UV and temperature swings matters, and in mountain areas resistance to frost.
  • Central Russia and Siberia. The key factors are frost resistance and water absorption. Deep-relief ornament must have slopes and drips so that water and snow do not linger on horizontal surfaces.
  • Coastal areas. Fixings need corrosion protection and materials must be salt-resistant.

Soft porous stones (shell limestone, some limestones and sandstones) are beautiful, but in regions with frequent freeze–thaw cycles they need water-repellent treatment and a frost-resistance check for the specific quarry. GFRC and dense stones are more reliable in such conditions. Exact properties are in the product data sheet or test report.

How do the regional schools of oriental architecture differ?

The general name covers several independent traditions, each with its own arch forms, proportions and finishes. For a project it is important to choose one reference school and keep to its logic: a mixture of motifs from different regions on one facade usually looks accidental.

The regional context also matters: a building in an oriental style is most convincing where it continues the local building culture. Materials and techniques typical of the south of Russia are described in the article on facades in the Caucasus.

Composition of an oriental facade: portal, gallery and calm wall

An oriental facade is built on contrast: a calm, almost blank wall plane and a few rich accents, namely the portal, the arcade and the screens. Decoration is not spread evenly, as in European classicism, but gathered in key places.

  • The portal is the main accent. It is made taller than the main volume or set off by a rectangular frame within which the entrance arch is placed.
  • A gallery or arcade forms the lower tier and provides shade; the column spacing sets the rhythm of the whole facade.
  • The wall plane is left calm: large stone slabs or panels with shallow relief.
  • The top of the wall is finished with a cornice, a shaped parapet or merlons.
  • Corners are emphasised with turrets, pylons or three-quarter colonnettes.

The proportions of the arches are checked on the elevation together with the glazing: the window unit behind the arch is, as a rule, rectangular, and the curved part is closed with a solid insert, a screen or shaped glazing. This decision is taken before the windows are ordered, otherwise the arch becomes an applied detail unrelated to the opening.

Gallery columns in oriental architecture are, as a rule, more slender than classical ones, and the capitals have a stalactite or carved form. Such elements, including shafts, bases, capitals and archivolts, are produced as GFRC columns and capitals from bespoke models. With a large number of identical bays the arcade is assembled from a few repeating parts, which is convenient for both production and installation.

How ornament becomes series-produced panels

An ornamental panel is designed from the technology: the pattern must not only look authentic but also release from the mould, withstand transport and keep its strength at thin points. For this reason the historical model is almost always adapted.

  • Draft angles: the side faces of the relief are given a slight slope, otherwise the unit is damaged on demoulding.
  • The minimum thickness of the bridges in openwork screens is limited; a pattern that is too fine is enlarged or converted into blind relief.
  • The panel size is chosen as a multiple of the ornament repeat, so that the joint runs along the boundary of the module.
  • Deep relief increases mass and complicates cleaning; drainage is provided on horizontal areas.
  • Corner and make-up elements are designed separately so that the pattern does not break off at the edge of the building.

Once the sample is approved, texture and colour are fixed as the reference for the whole batch. The surface may be smooth, rough or imitate dressed stone; the options are described in the overview of GFRC textures.

Which stone is chosen for a facade in the oriental style?

Light stones in warm shades with a matt surface suit the oriental style: they are close to the historical prototypes and take carving well. The choice of a particular stone depends on the climate and on where it works, whether on the wall plane, in the plinth or in carved details.

  • Travertine for the facade gives a characteristic banded texture and a beige-golden palette; the pores are usually filled, and the surface is ground or honed.
  • Dagestan stone, meaning local limestones, dolomites and sandstones, is the traditional material of Caucasian architecture and is easy to work.
  • Granite is used in the plinth and on steps, where abrasion resistance and low water absorption are important.
  • Marble is appropriate in portals and in the interiors of entrance zones.

TECHNOFORMA Group has worked with natural stone for more than 15 years and supplies it from India, Turkey, China and Iran, which makes it possible to select a stone to suit the image and the budget. Wall planes are usually clad in stone on a subframe, while complex carved details are more economical to reproduce in glass fibre reinforced concrete, with colour and texture matched to the chosen stone.

Colour, light and shadow in oriental architecture

The expressiveness of an oriental facade is created largely by light: deep niches, openwork screens and relief are designed for bright sun and sharp shadow. In regions with overcast weather the same relief looks softer, so it is made deeper or supported by lighting.

The basic palette is white, sand, ochre and terracotta. Saturated colour is introduced sparingly: turquoise and blue inserts, golden details on portals, dark timber or metal screens. In the evening, openwork panels with internal lighting turn into luminous screens, and grazing light along the wall brings out the ornament. Positions for luminaires and cable routes are provided at the design stage, before the panels are produced.

The oriental style for hotels, residential complexes and public buildings

The style is applied differently depending on the type of building: in some cases it defines the whole image, in others it is present as individual motifs.

  • Hotels and resort complexes. Full stylisation with galleries, portals and screens creates a recognisable image; the requirements for such buildings are covered in the article on the hotel facade.
  • Residential complexes. Individual motifs are used more often: arched heads to the windows of the upper floors, ornamental inserts, the treatment of entrance groups and the podium.
  • Public and religious buildings. Large portals, domed terminations and long ornamental friezes require bespoke design of every detail.
  • Private houses. An arched gallery, a carved portal and window screens are sufficient; an excess of decoration on a small volume looks heavy.

For buildings on the sea coast, salt air and strong wind are taken into account in addition: corrosion-resistant fixings are chosen, and openwork panels are designed for wind load with regard to their shape and area. In seismic regions the fixing details of the cladding and decoration are designed to allow movement.

On multi-storey buildings screens and ornamental panels are conveniently used to conceal technical zones such as air-conditioning baskets, ventilation openings and car parks. The decorative element thereby acquires a practical function.

What to prepare for an estimate and which mistakes to avoid

An estimate for a facade in the oriental style requires elevations with the decoration marked and sketches of the main motifs. The more precisely the arches and ornament are defined, the closer the preliminary assessment is to the final cost.

  1. Facade elevations with levels and opening dimensions.
  2. Sketches of arches, portals, screens and ornaments, or references from which they can be developed.
  3. Information on the walls and the facade system.
  4. Preferences for materials: stone, glass fibre reinforced concrete, their combination, colour.
  5. Construction region and building height.

Typical mistakes in such projects:

  • ornament too fine for the height at which it is installed;
  • arches of different types on one facade without a common logic;
  • porous stone in the plinth without protection against moisture;
  • screens designed without regard to cleaning and access to windows;
  • patterns copied without checking symmetry at the panel joints.

Most of these problems are resolved at the stage of the 3D model and the control sample, before the series is launched.

FAQ

What elements are typical of an oriental-style facade?

Arches of various shapes, portals, openwork screens, geometric and floral ornament, galleries, light stone.

How does an Arab-style facade differ from a Persian one?

Arab style is usually restrained outside, with flat screens and blank walls; Persian has rich portals, tilework and domes.

What is oriental facade ornament made of?

GFRC, natural stone, ceramics, metal. GFRC allows repeating ornamental modules with precise joints.

Is oriental style suitable for a cold climate?

Yes, with frost-resistant materials and properly designed slopes and drips on horizontal relief surfaces.

Can a mashrabiya screen be made of concrete?

Yes, openwork GFRC panels are cast in moulds; web thickness is set by the technology and calculation.

Can oriental motifs be combined with contemporary architecture?

Yes. Most often one or two techniques are taken, such as openwork panels with a geometric pattern, simplified arches or deep shaded loggias, and applied to a restrained volume without historical detailing. Such a facade retains its regional character and does not look like a pastiche.

How should openwork screens and relief ornament be maintained?

Relief collects more dust than a smooth surface, so it is periodically washed with water at moderate pressure and soft brushes, without abrasives. Water-repellent treatment of the surface reduces soiling and makes cleaning easier. Access for maintenance of screens in front of windows is built into the design.

Which is more economical for a large volume: carved stone or GFRC ornament?

With high repetition, glass fibre reinforced concrete is, as a rule, more economical: the mould is used many times, whereas in carved stone every piece is worked separately. Stone is justified on wall planes and in one-off accent elements. The exact ratio depends on the pattern, the stone and the run, and is determined by calculation.
