Cornice Projection: How to Calculate and Fix It

Facade cornice projection is the distance by which a cornice stands out from the wall plane (or the cladding). It is set on the basis of architectural proportions and the building's scale and then checked by calculation: the greater the projection, the higher the cantilever moment on the fixing points and the more substantial the inserts, brackets and anchors must be.

The main criterion is a balance of three requirements: the cornice must shed water and be legible from the ground, be securely fixed against wind and snow, and not overload the substructure or wall. On multi-storey buildings the architect sets the projection and the structural engineer designs the fixing.

This article is for designers and clients who want to understand the logic behind the solution. All décor is covered in Architectural facade décor.

What projection is

Projection is measured horizontally from the wall plane (for a rainscreen facade, from the face of the cladding) to the most projecting point of the profile. Together with the profile height, it determines the proportions of the cornice and how it looks from different viewpoints.

Projection serves several functions:

  • it sheds run-off beyond the wall plane;
  • it casts a shadow that emphasises the horizontal and makes the profile legible;
  • it visually completes the facade or part of it.

It is important to distinguish architectural projection (the visible profile) from structural projection: on a rainscreen facade the cornice is fixed through the substructure, and the actual lever arm from the wall to the cornice's centre of gravity is greater than the visible projection by the thickness of the insulation and air gap.

An example of the logic: if the visible projection of a cornice is several tens of centimetres and the insulation plus air gap adds as much again, the bracket works on a lever arm almost twice the visible projection. That is why on rainscreen facades even cornices that look small need the fixing detail to be calculated.

Standards and proportions

There is no single standard for the projection of a decorative cornice — it is an architectural decision. Guidelines are provided by:

  • Classical order proportions. Treatises relate cornice projection and entablature height to the column module; in the classical tradition, the projection of a crowning cornice is comparable to its height. For a neoclassical facade these ratios are a starting point that is adapted to the number of storeys.
  • Building scale and viewpoint. The higher a cornice is installed, the larger its profile and projection must be to be legible from the street.
  • Drainage. The projection must be sufficient for water to drop from the drip clear of the elements below.
  • Site constraints. Projections over pavements and site boundaries may be restricted by planning requirements.

Load requirements for facade elements are set by current Russian codes of practice (SP) on loads and actions, and fixing requirements for rainscreen systems by the documents for the specific facade system.

A typical mistake is transferring proportions from a low-rise building to a high-rise one without adaptation: a profile that works on a mansion becomes a thin line at 15th-floor level. The opposite mistake is excessive projection at lower levels, which looks oppressive and requires heavy fixings. The solution is found on a 3D model from real viewpoints, and the design department checks the chosen projection structurally before moulds are made.

Cantilever load

A cornice is a cantilever: it is supported by the wall on one side, and loads act on a lever arm. The main loads:

LoadHow it actsWhat is taken into account
Dead loadVertically, on the lever arm to the centre of gravityWeight of the element and fixings
Snow and iceVertically, on the top surfaceSnow region, fall, risk of ice build-up
WindPressure and suction, including upwardHeight, terrain type, corner zones
ImposedMaintenance loads, ladders leaning on itClient requirements

The moment at the fixing point grows in proportion to the lever arm, so doubling the projection roughly doubles the bending moment from the same weight. For lightweight GFRC this is an advantage over stone: the same profile produces a smaller moment. Even so, large projections require calculation to current codes and, as a rule, a metal frame or cantilever brackets.

Calculations cover load combinations with partial safety factors and check not only the cornice itself but also the brackets, anchors and substrate.

Anchors and cast-in inserts

A facade cornice fixing consists of three links: cast-in inserts in the element → bracket or cantilever → anchor in the substrate.

  • Cast-in inserts are placed in the GFRC during casting: plates, sockets, anchor loops of stainless or protected steel. Their position and number are determined by calculation; the element is strengthened around the inserts.
  • Brackets are metal cantilevers that transfer the load to the floor slab or wall. A bracket-supported cornice is the main solution for large projections: the bracket takes the moment and the skin forms the profile.
  • Anchors are selected according to the substrate material (concrete, brick, aerated concrete) and the results of on-site pull-out tests. In hollow brick and aerated concrete, anchor capacity is lower, so the aim is to transfer the load to the floor slabs.

The scheme must let the cornice move freely with temperature: some fixings carry load, others only restrain. Fixings in areas exposed to water and condensation are made corrosion-resistant.

Before installation starts, on-site pull-out tests of anchors in the substrate are carried out: their results confirm or adjust the design decisions. During installation each fixing is set to survey levels, and after installation the cornice line is checked along the full length of the facade.

Protecting the top

The top of a cornice is the surface most vulnerable to water. It receives rain, snow and meltwater from the roof. Solutions:

  1. An outward fall on the top surface — so that water does not stand.
  2. A metal flashing (polymer-coated galvanised steel, copper, aluminium) over the cornice, tucked into the wall under the cladding or into a joint.
  3. Sealing the joints between cornice elements, or cover strips over the joints.
  4. Water-repellent treatment of the GFRC surface.
  5. Bird protection — deterrent systems are fitted on wide ledges where necessary.

On crowning cornices, snow and ice are also taken into account: without snow guards on the roof, snow may slide onto the cornice, so the roof and cornice detail are designed together.

The most common mistake is a flashing without enough width and without a drip: water runs off its edge onto the face of the cornice and leaves streaks. The flashing edge must project beyond the profile and be turned down.

What input data is needed to calculate a cornice projection?

The calculation requires three groups of data: the profile geometry, information on the substrate and the site conditions. Without any one of them the projection remains an architectural wish that cannot be confirmed structurally.

  • Profile and layout. The cornice cross-section with dimensions, installation level, length of runs, positions of corners, breaks and joints.
  • Substrate. Wall material and thickness, position of slab edges, concrete class or type of masonry. In aerated concrete and hollow blocks the load-bearing capacity of an anchor is markedly lower than in cast in-situ concrete, so the load is, as a rule, transferred to the floor slab.
  • Facade build-up. Insulation thickness, air gap, type of subframe and cladding: these determine the structural lever arm.
  • Site. Wind and snow zone, terrain category, building height and, for certain regions, seismicity.
  • Adjacent details. Roof, parapet, rainwater drainage, lighting, fixings for facade maintenance.

This data is usually gathered at the facade design stage, while it is still possible to adjust the profile or the position of cast-in plates in the concrete. If the building frame has already been erected, the actual geometry is surveyed first: deviations of slab edges by a few centimetres change the length of the brackets and must be reflected in the drawings.

Calculation sequence: from the profile to the fixing detail

The calculation proceeds from the load to the substrate: first one determines what acts on the cornice, then what reaches each bracket and anchor. The sequence is generally as follows.

  1. The mass per linear metre and the position of the centre of gravity relative to the wall are determined from the cross-section.
  2. The loads are compiled: self-weight, snow and ice on the top face, wind with allowance for height and corner zones.
  3. The bracket spacing and the scheme are set: which fixings are load-bearing and which are restraining only.
  4. The forces at the fixing are found: the vertical reaction, pull-out on the upper anchor and compression on the lower one.
  5. The bracket is checked for strength and deflection, the cast-in insert for pull-out from the GFRC body, and the anchor for its capacity in the specific substrate.
  6. The design forces are compared with the results of on-site anchor tests and, where necessary, the spacing or the type of fixing is changed.

The fourth step is the most revealing. The overturning moment is resisted by a couple of forces, and the smaller the vertical distance between the upper and lower anchors, the greater the pull-out force. A cornice with a large projection and a shallow profile therefore loads its fixings more heavily than a tall profile with the same projection. It is sometimes simpler to increase the height of the bracket's bearing part than to install a more powerful anchor.

The deflection of the cantilever is checked separately. Even where strength is sufficient, noticeable deflection disturbs the line of the cornice and opens the joints, so bracket stiffness often proves to be the governing criterion. More on the types of supporting elements can be found in the article on facade brackets and consoles.

How does the cornice material affect the permissible projection?

The lighter the profile, the greater the projection achievable with the same fixing. The limit is set not by the material itself but by the moment that the fixing is able to transfer to the substrate.

SolutionWeight characteristicsTypical applicationWhat limits the projection
Hollow GFRC profileThin shell, low mass per linear metreString-course and crowning cornices of multi-storey buildingsCapacity of cast-in inserts and brackets
GFRC on a steel frameWeight of the shell and the frameLarge crowning cornices, large projectionsFrame stiffness and anchorage into the floor slab
Natural stoneMassive blocks or slabs, density usually 2.2–2.8 t/m³Plinth, lower tiers, historic facadesWeight of the unit, bedding into masonry or heavy consoles
Cast in-situ reinforced concreteGreatest weight, works as part of the structureCornices provided for in the frame designReinforcement and connection to the floor slab

On ventilated facades, heavy solutions increase not only the size of the brackets but also the number of thermal bridges: every console passes through the insulation. A lightweight GFRC cornice allows supports to be placed less frequently and with a smaller section. A stone profile nevertheless remains appropriate where the cornice is viewed at close range and a natural texture matters.

Section length, joints and thermal movement

The section length is chosen so that the element can be cast in a single mould, transported without damage and lifted to its level with the equipment available. In practice this is usually between one and three metres; the exact value depends on the cross-section and weight.

A cornice is exposed to the open air and heats up and cools down over a wide range during the year. A long run of profile changes its length accordingly, and if the movement has nowhere to go, cracks or chipped ends appear. A joint of calculated width, filled with elastic sealant, is therefore left between sections, and at the building's movement joints a cornice joint is mandatory.

  • Each section rests on at least two fixings: one fixes its position, the other allows movement along the facade.
  • Joints are not placed above the axis of an opening or at breaks in the profile, where they are most visible.
  • Corner elements are made as single pieces: a joint on the corner itself is difficult to seal and opens quickly.
  • The joint spacing is coordinated with the cladding pattern below so that the verticals coincide.

In the crowning position the cornice meets the parapet, and the joint layout is made common to both elements. The construction of the top of the wall is covered in the article on parapets and their cladding.

What projection is appropriate for different types of building?

There is no universal value, but the selection logic differs from one type of project to another. On a private house the cornice is viewed at close range, on a multi-storey building from below and from a distance, so both the profile and the approach to fixing change.

  • Private houses. The projection is usually small and the cornice often bears directly on the wall; most attention is paid to the junction with the roof eaves and the rainwater drainage.
  • Residential complexes. The facade carries hundreds of metres of identical profile, so standardisation of sections and a bracket spacing tied to the floor slabs are decisive.
  • Business centres and hotels. A large crowning cornice is often combined with lighting and facade maintenance systems, which adds loads and cast-in parts.
  • Public buildings. Above entrances and walkways, higher requirements are placed on the reliability of the fixing, and the anchor reserve is set with this in mind.

In every case the final value is confirmed by calculating the fixing and by a ground-level view on the facade model. If the calculation shows that the fixing becomes too heavy, the cross-section and material of the profile are reviewed first, and only then the projection itself.

Typical mistakes in choosing the projection and fixing

Most problems are built in before installation, at the stage when the profile has been drawn but the fixing detail has not yet been developed. The following mistakes occur most often.

  • The lever arm is measured from the face of the cladding rather than from the load-bearing wall, and the moment is underestimated.
  • Fixing is specified into lightweight block masonry without verifying the anchors by testing.
  • Wind suction at corners and at the parapet, where the load acts upwards and tends to tear the cornice off, is not taken into account.
  • Cast-in plates for brackets are not provided in the concrete, and drilling on site has to be done in zones of dense reinforcement.
  • All fixings are made rigid, leaving no allowance for thermal movement.
  • The cornice is designed separately from the subframe, so that brackets clash with rails and cladding fixings.

The last mistake is typical of projects where the decorative elements and the ventilated facade are carried out by different contractors. A combined drawing of the subframe and cornice fixings removes most clashes before work starts on site. It is also useful to agree the sequence in advance: cornice brackets are, as a rule, installed before the insulation, and the profile itself after the cladding beneath it has been fitted.

What is checked when an installed cornice is accepted?

Three things are checked at acceptance: conformity of the fixings to the design, the geometry of the line and water run-off. Some fixings are concealed after installation, so covered-up work is recorded in certificates and photographs as it proceeds.

  • type, number and spacing of anchors and brackets, against the as-built drawing;
  • records of anchor tests in the substrate;
  • straightness of the lower and upper edges, steps between adjacent sections;
  • width and filling of joints, absence of chipped ends;
  • fall of the top face, junction of the flashing with the wall, presence of a drip;
  • corrosion protection of metal at cut and welded points.

In service a cornice is inspected, as a rule, once a year, most conveniently in spring: the condition of the joint sealant, the flashing and the fixings in accessible places is examined. A joint renewed in time costs incomparably less than repairing cladding that has become wet beneath the cornice. FAQ

How is a facade cornice fixed?

Via cast-in inserts in the element to brackets or cantilevers anchored into the floor slab or wall; the scheme is determined by calculation.

When is a bracket-supported cornice needed?

For large projections and weights: the metal bracket takes the cantilever moment while the GFRC skin forms the profile.

What should the projection of a facade cornice be?

There is no single standard: projection is set by the building's proportions and scale and checked by a fixing calculation.

What loads are considered when designing a cornice?

Dead load, snow and ice, wind (pressure and suction), imposed loads.

How do you protect the top of a cornice from water?

With an outward fall, a metal flashing, sealed joints and water-repellent treatment.

Can the cornice projection be increased if the building frame is already built?

Yes, but within the load-bearing capacity of the substrate. Brackets are fixed to the slab edges with anchors, and the actual capacity is confirmed by testing. If the reserve is insufficient, the support spacing is reduced, the profile is made lighter or the projection is shortened.

Who is responsible for calculating the cornice fixing?

The structural engineer who prepares the working documentation for the facade. The architect sets the profile and projection, the manufacturer provides the mass of the unit and the positions of the cast-in inserts, while the calculation of the fixing and the selection of anchors form part of the facade design.

Should seismic action be considered when fixing a cornice?

In seismic regions, yes: a seismic load is added to the static loads, and the fixings are designed so that the cornice does not lose its support when the frame moves. The specific requirements are determined by the designer in accordance with the codes for the construction area.
