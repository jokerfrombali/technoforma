Custom Decor to an Architect's Sketch

Custom facade decor means elements made to an architect's sketch or drawings: non-standard cornices, window surrounds, columns, bas-reliefs, ornamental panels, signature details. Unlike catalogue products, each such element needs its own master model and mould, and a sample is approved before serial production.

The main criterion when choosing a contractor is a full cycle: design, model and mould making, production and installation in one pair of hands reduce the risk of discrepancies between the sketch and the result. Custom decor is ordered by developers, architectural practices, private home owners and operators of historic buildings. All element types are covered in the Architectural facade decor section.

What is needed for a quote

To estimate the cost and lead time of custom decor, the manufacturer needs input data. The more complete the package, the more accurate the estimate and the fewer iterations.

  • Facades: drawings, or at least photos with dimensions, showing where the elements go.
  • Sketches or drawings of elements: profiles, sections, ornament developments; 3D models if available.
  • Quantities: linear metres of linear elements, number of individual pieces, number of repeats of each type.
  • Substrate: wall material, presence of insulation or a rainscreen system.
  • Surface requirements: colour, texture, a sample if one has been approved.
  • Scope of work: decor only, or fixing design and installation as well.
  • Schedule: when the building will be ready for installation.

If the sketch is still rough, the manufacturer's design department can develop it into working drawings: split it into pieces taking joints, weight and transport into account, and produce a 3D visualisation. At TECHNOFORMA this work is done by 12 designers led by two architects, together with 3D visualisers.

Typical mistakes at the start that lengthen the estimate: a sketch without dimensions tied to the facade, no information on the number of repeats, a single general view instead of profile sections. Without a section, the manufacturer has to guess it, which means new rounds of approval.

Master model

A master model is a full-size reference piece from which the mould for serial production is taken. The quality of every piece in the series depends on its accuracy.

Ways of making a master model:

  • CNC milling from a 3D model: precise geometry, suitable for profiles and geometric ornament.
  • Hand modelling and carving: for sculptural elements, foliate ornament and figures.
  • Combined method: the base is milled and details are finished by hand.
  • Assembly from run profiles: for cornices and mouldings whose profile is run with a template.

The model is approved by the architect before the mould is made: correcting relief on a model is easier and cheaper than remaking a mould. Moulds are made from different materials (silicone compounds, polyurethane, fibreglass, metal) depending on the complexity of the relief and the number of pieces to be cast from it.

TECHNOFORMA makes models and moulds in its own mould shops rather than outsourcing this stage to third-party workshops.

For symmetrical elements, a model of half or a quarter of the piece is often made and then mirrored in the digital model. This reduces manual work and eliminates differences between the left and right parts of a window surround or portal. For long profiles, the piece length and joint positions must be decided in advance, as they determine the size of the mould.

Before the mould is made, the model should be checked for undercuts: areas of relief that prevent the piece from being released are either simplified or handled with a split mould. This affects both price and lead time.

Lead time

The lead time for custom decor is made up of several stages:

  1. developing the sketch, working drawings and 3D model;
  2. making and approving the master model;
  3. making the mould;
  4. producing and approving the first sample;
  5. serial production, including curing of the pieces;
  6. delivery and installation.

The duration of each stage depends on the complexity of the elements, the number of different types, the size of the series and the production workload, so there is no single lead time: it is determined after reviewing the documents. What speeds things up:

  • ready 3D models or accurate drawings from the architect;
  • fast approval of the model and sample;
  • standardisation: fewer element types, more repeats;
  • parallel work: while moulds for some elements are being made, others are already in production.

For large projects, production capacity matters. According to the company, TECHNOFORMA's production site covers more than 2 hectares and its GFRC capacity is more than 20,000 m² per month.

Delays most often arise not in production but in approvals: the model and sample wait for the architect's and client's decision. It helps to appoint one person responsible for sign-off and agree a response time in advance.

Cost of moulds

The cost of moulds is the main item that distinguishes custom decor from catalogue products. It depends on:

FactorEffect
Element sizeThe larger the piece, the more material and work go into the model and mould
Relief complexitySculptural relief and undercuts require handwork and a more complex mould
Number of different typesEach type needs its own model and mould
Series sizeThe more pieces from one mould, the smaller its share in the price of each element
Mould materialChosen by the number of casts required

The practical conclusion: elements should be standardised at the design stage. Three types of window surrounds instead of ten barely change the look of the facade but noticeably reduce the cost. The exact price is calculated after reviewing the drawings; there are no general rates for custom moulds.

Sample approval

The sample is the first piece cast from the production mould. Its approval is the final check before serial production, and changes after that are expensive.

What is checked when approving the sample:

  • Geometry: dimensions, straightness, angles, match of the profile to the drawing;
  • Relief: crisp edges and details, no voids or chips;
  • Texture and colour: in daylight, compared with the approved reference and the wall material;
  • Embeds and fixings: position and match to the installation scheme;
  • Fit: how the piece joins its neighbours, with no break in the pattern.

The result of approval is documented: the approved sample becomes the reference for the whole batch. Tolerances for colour and geometry are best agreed in advance. The technical properties of the material are confirmed by the product data sheet and test reports. At TECHNOFORMA quality is controlled at every production stage, and installation is carried out by the company's own installation teams, so installers' comments can be taken into account straight away.

Checklist for the approval record: sample number and date, photos from several angles, agreed dimensional and colour tolerances, signatures of the architect and client, and where the reference is kept, at the factory or on site.

Which décor should be made to a bespoke sketch and which taken from the catalogue?

It makes sense to produce as bespoke items those that shape the image of the building and are seen at close range: portals, capitals, cartouches, ornamental inserts, a crowning cornice with a special profile. Ordinary elements — straight bands, simple architraves, sill profiles — are often selected from existing moulds or slightly adapted, and this has almost no effect on the appearance of the facade.

ElementUsual solutionReason
Main entrance portal, cartouche, coat of armsbespoke onlyunique composition, tied to the dimensions of the opening
Column capitals and basesbespoke or adaptation of an existing modeldepend on the shaft diameter and the order
Crowning cornicemore often bespokethe profile defines the silhouette of the building and the projection
Architraves, window headscatalogue with dimensions adjustedmany repeats, standard profile
String courses, bands, mouldingscataloguesimple section, long runs
Rusticated panelsstandard profile, bespoke layoutdepend on storey height and the axes of the openings

A mixed approach gives the best balance of budget and expressiveness: the spend on moulds is concentrated where it is noticed. Working with the standard range is described in the article on how to choose décor from a catalogue.

Another practical technique is to design unique details so that one profile is used in several places: the same moulding in the portal, in the shopfront surrounds and in the architraves of the upper floors. The facade then looks coherent, and the number of moulds is reduced.

The decision is conveniently made from a schedule of elements: for each type, the quantity, the position on the facade and the distance from which it is seen are stated. Details with a large number of repeats and far from the viewer are the first candidates for a standard profile; one-off elements at the entrance are candidates for bespoke development.

Which manufacturing constraints should the sketch take into account?

A sketch becomes a product without rework if wall thickness, the method of demoulding, transport dimensions and fixing positions are taken into account from the outset. The architect does not need to know the technology in detail, but a few rules save weeks of approvals.

  • Draft angles. The vertical walls of a relief are given a slight taper so that the unit leaves the mould undamaged; undercuts require a flexible or split mould.
  • Minimum detail sizes. Very thin projecting ribs and sharp arrises chip during demoulding and transport, so arrises are slightly rounded and thin details are thickened towards the base.
  • Depth of relief. This is matched to the installation height: what reads well at eye level merges into a flat plane on the fifth floor.
  • Dimensions and weight. The unit must fit the transport and be lifted by the plant available on site; large compositions are divided into parts in advance.
  • Joints. Their width and position are set deliberately in the sketch, because a joint becomes part of the facade pattern.
  • Cast-in fixings and stiffening ribs. Space is needed at the back for the frame and fixing parts; a thin-walled unit is not normally set tight against the wall without a gap.
  • Water run-off. Upper surfaces are given an outward fall, lower edges a drip.

Colour and texture deserve separate thought. A smooth surface emphasises any unevenness and shade variation, whereas a stone-effect texture or slight roughness conceals them and ages more gracefully. Colour is specified in the sketch from a sample of the material rather than from a screen palette: the shade of a mineral surface depends on the cement, sand and pigment and cannot be reproduced on a monitor.

These requirements are reflected in the working documentation. The types of tooling and what they can do are covered in the article on moulds for GFRC. In practice it is enough to show the manufacturer the sketch at an early stage: the production engineers’ comments on tapers, joints and subdivision usually fit on a single sheet and do not alter the architectural intent.

Who approves what: architect, manufacturer, main contractor

Bespoke décor made to an architect’s sketch passes through several approval points, and each should have one person responsible. When roles are not allocated, decisions are made after the event, and rework is paid for by whoever failed to object in time.

StageWhat is approvedWho decides
Sketch and 3D modelform, proportions, division into unitsproject architect
Working drawings of the unitsdimensions, joints, weight, cast-in fixingsmanufacturer’s designer and structural engineer
Master modelrelief, level of detailarchitect and, where necessary, the client
Samplecolour, texture, geometryarchitect and client
Fixing detailsloads, compatibility with the subframe and substratestructural engineer and main contractor
Delivery schedulesequence by installation zonesmain contractor and manufacturer

Decisions are recorded in writing — by a signature on the drawing, minutes or a sample acceptance certificate. The set of unit and detail drawings is usually called the facade shop drawings; both the workshop and the installers work from it. At the installation stage, compliance with the design intent is monitored by the author of the project as part of site supervision.

Changes after the master model has been approved are documented separately, with programme and cost recalculated. This procedure keeps both parties disciplined and rules out disputes over what exactly was agreed.

Marking, packing and delivery of non-standard units

Bespoke pieces cannot be replaced from stock, so logistics are planned more carefully than for standard products. Each unit is given a mark that matches the installation drawing, and the packaging states the elevation, the level and the lifting sequence.

General rules for transport and storage:

  • units are carried in rigid crates or on pallets with spacers, and the exposed face is protected from abrasion;
  • long profiles are laid on edge or in cradles so that they are not subjected to bending;
  • corners and thin arrises are protected separately, and sculptural elements are packed individually;
  • for long-distance transport, transhipment and storage at intermediate warehouses are taken into account;
  • on site the units are checked against the packing list and inspected before lifting, and any comments are recorded immediately with photographs;
  • they are stored on a level area under cover or sheeting, clear of the ground and stacked no higher than the manufacturer specifies.

The delivery is split into batches by installation zone, so that the site does not accumulate stock which there is nowhere to store and which is easily damaged.

The first batch requires particular attention. It is useful to send a small one and assemble it on the facade as a trial area: this tests the packing, the marking, the ease of slinging and the actual rate of installation. Comments arising from it are taken into account in subsequent shipments while the main run is still in production.

How is made-to-order décor installed?

Installation of non-standard elements begins with an as-built survey: the actual dimensions of openings and the levels almost always differ from the design, and this needs to be known before the production run, not after. Based on the survey, the lengths of the make-up pieces and the joint positions are adjusted.

Complex assemblies — a portal, an arched surround, the corner returns of a cornice — undergo a trial assembly at the factory. On the facade the elements are hung on brackets adjustable in three directions, aligned to the axes and only then finally fixed. Joints are filled with a colour-matched sealant or left open if the design intends this.

Typical mistakes at this stage are cutting units on site without the manufacturer’s agreement, drilling additional holes close to an edge, rigidly fixing all fixing points with no allowance for thermal movement, and filling joints with mortar. Each of these eventually shows up as cracks or chips, and the defect usually appears not immediately but after one or two seasons.

It is more convenient to entrust the work to the contractor that manufactured the units: it knows the positions of the cast-in fixings and the permissible slinging points. TECHNOFORMA Group carries out installation with its own team of more than 100 specialists; the scope of services is set out on the construction, installation and facade works page.

Spare units and mould storage: what to provide for in advance

A small stock of spare units and an agreement on mould storage avoid problems when pieces are damaged during installation and during future repairs. A single piece can be made again several years later only if the mould has survived or can be recreated from the master model.

The contract and the brief should define in advance:

  • the allowance for breakage and cutting — its size is set according to the complexity of the elements and the delivery conditions;
  • the period and conditions for storing moulds and models at the manufacturer’s premises;
  • who owns the models, moulds and 3D files once the contract is complete;
  • the colour and texture reference — a sample from which the shade can be reproduced;
  • the product certificates and as-built documentation handed over to the building’s maintenance team.

A digital model is more reliable than a physical mould: flexible moulds wear out over time, whereas the master model can be produced again from the file. This is especially important for developments built in phases: the décor of the second phase must match the first in profile, texture and shade, and more than a year may pass between them.

FAQ

Can facade decor be ordered to my own sketch?

Yes. Working drawings, a master model and a mould are made from the sketch, followed by a sample for approval and then the series.

What should I send for a custom decor quote?

Drawings or photos of the facade with dimensions, sketches of the elements, quantities, information about the substrate and colour and texture requirements.

Why is custom decor more expensive than catalogue decor?

Because of the cost of the master model and mould. In a large series their share in the price of each piece decreases.

How long does custom decor take to make?

It depends on complexity, the number of element types, volume and production workload; the lead time is set after reviewing the documents.

What is a master model?

A full-size reference piece of the element from which the mould for serial production is taken.

Can the sketch be changed after the master model has been made?

Yes, as long as the mould has not been made: the model is reworked and approved again. Once the mould exists, a change to the relief means reworking it or making a new one, so programme and cost are recalculated separately.

Does the architect have to supply a 3D model?

No. A sketch or drawing with dimensions and sections is sufficient — the manufacturer’s design department will build the 3D model from it. A ready-made model from the architect shortens the development stage, but it is still checked for manufacturability.

Can décor from an existing building be reproduced?

Yes. The surviving element is measured, scanned or has a mould taken from it, after which a model and the production run are made. On heritage buildings such work is carried out as part of the restoration design and in line with its requirements.
