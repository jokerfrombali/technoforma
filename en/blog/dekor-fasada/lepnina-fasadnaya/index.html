Exterior Facade Mouldings: Modern Technologies Instead of Plaster

Facade mouldings are relief architectural elements on exterior walls: cornices, string courses, mouldings, window surrounds, brackets, rosettes and bas-reliefs. Historically they were run and cast in plaster and lime mortars, but outdoors plaster absorbs water, breaks down when it freezes and needs constant repair. Today, facade mouldings almost always mean factory-made products of glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC), polymer concrete, expanded polystyrene with a protective coating, or natural stone.

The main selection criterion is service conditions: frost resistance, water absorption, combustibility, and how the element is fixed to the wall and carries loads. For multi-storey housing and public buildings, where fire rating and service life matter, GFRC or stone is usually chosen; for small private houses, lightweight polymer elements are sometimes acceptable. An overview of all element types is in the section Architectural facade decor.

What facade mouldings mean today

In classical architecture, mouldings meant sculpted decor made on site: a craftsman ran the cornice profile with a template through fresh mortar, or cast rosettes and capitals in moulds and set them on the wall. The materials were plaster, lime and later cement mortars.

Today the term is used more broadly. Facade mouldings are any three-dimensional decor that repeats classical forms, whatever the technology:

  • linear elements — cornices, bands, string courses, mouldings, archivolts, rusticated bands;
  • opening surrounds — window surrounds, pediments over windows, keystones, sill slabs;
  • individual elements — brackets, corbels, rosettes, cartouches, mascarons;
  • order elements — columns, pilasters, capitals, bases, balusters;
  • reliefs — bas-reliefs, panels, ornamental inserts.

Put briefly, facade mouldings today are not made of plaster: it is unsuitable for exterior work without constant protection. Modern elements are cast in moulds at the factory and delivered to site ready for installation.

Materials for exterior use

A material for exterior decor must withstand freeze–thaw cycles, precipitation, UV and temperature swings. Below is a comparison of the main options.

MaterialStrengthsLimitations
Glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC)Non-combustible, frost-resistant, thin-walled and relatively light, reproduces fine relief, allows large sizesRequires an engineered fixing scheme and cast-in anchors; quality depends on the mix design and production control
Natural stoneDurability, natural texture, graceful ageingHeavy, carving complex shapes is expensive, fixing must be calculated
Polymer concrete, polyurethaneLow weight, simple installationCombustibility and behaviour under heat depend on composition; restrictions on use on multi-storey buildings
Expanded polystyrene with reinforced coatingLow price, easy adhesive installationVulnerable to impact, combustible, reproduces fine relief poorly
PlasterPrecise reproduction of reliefUnsuitable outdoors: absorbs water and breaks down

GFRC is fine-aggregate concrete reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibre. According to TECHNOFORMA, it has four components: glass fibre, sand, high-grade cement and water; the material is rated non-combustible (Russian class NG) and has high frost resistance, which is further improved with admixtures. Specific strength, density and water absorption values depend on the mix design — exact figures are given in the product data sheet and test reports.

Relief and sculpture

A relief is an image projecting from a background plane. On facades there is bas-relief (low relief, projecting less than half its volume), high relief (figures almost detached from the background) and sunken relief (an incised image). Free-standing sculpture — vases, figures, finials placed on parapets, in niches and on pedestals — is a separate category.

Technologically, GFRC reliefs are made like other decor: a mould is taken from a master model and the GFRC mix is sprayed or poured into it. The thin wall of the product with stiffening ribs on the back allows large panels without critical weight, while the fine mix reproduces details — leaves, drapery folds, ornament.

When designing a relief, the viewing distance is taken into account. At ground-floor level fine detailing reads well; at the seventh to ninth floor deeper shadows and a bolder pattern are needed. Horizontal projections of the relief must have a slope or a drip, otherwise water and dirt will collect on them.

Free-standing sculpture on a facade needs a separate fixing calculation for wind load and overturning, plus reliable anchoring to the load-bearing structure — the element must not be held by adhesive or mortar alone.

Made to your sketch

Standard catalogue elements are not always suitable: the architect may have their own cornice profile, a non-standard bracket spacing or a unique ornament. In that case the decor is made to a sketch. The sequence is usually as follows:

  1. Input data. A sketch or facade drawing, element profiles, dimensions, required texture and colour.
  2. Design development. Dividing the decor into products, allowing for joints, weight, transport and fixing scheme. A 3D model and visualisation are usually produced at this stage.
  3. Master model and mould. A full-size model of the element is made, and from it a mould for serial production.
  4. Sample. The first piece is shown to the client and architect to approve relief, texture and colour.
  5. Series and installation. After approval, the batch is produced and installed according to the design scheme.

At TECHNOFORMA this cycle is closed in-house: a design department (12 designers led by two architects, plus 3D visualisers), our own mould shops where models and moulds are made, production with quality control at every stage, and our own installation team.

Restoration

Restoring historic mouldings is a separate task, where the decision is made after surveying the facade. If an element is partly lost, it may be repaired in place; if it is completely destroyed or unsafe, it is reproduced from surviving fragments, measurements and archive photographs.

Sequence of work when replacing lost decor:

  • measurements and casts taken from surviving fragments;
  • reconstruction of the profile and pattern in a model;
  • making the mould and a trial element;
  • matching texture and colour to the existing finish;
  • removing unsafe elements and installing new ones fixed to the load-bearing structure.

For cultural heritage sites, the material, method and scope of intervention are set by the restoration design and agreed with the heritage protection authority. For ordinary buildings with lost decor, replacing plaster and cement elements with GFRC preserves the appearance and reduces maintenance.

What set of facade mouldings is needed for different building types

The composition of moulded decor is determined by the purpose of the building, its height and the distance from which the facade is viewed. On a multi-storey building, large horizontals and surrounds do the work; on a private house, a few carefully chosen details at the entrance and windows. Guidance by building type is summarised in the table.

Building typeMain elementsWhat to look out for
Multi-storey residential complexCrowning cornice, string courses, architraves, rustication of the lower floorsNon-combustibility of the material, standardised profiles, fixing to the subframe
HotelPortal, columns and pilasters, balustrades, window surrounds on principal floorsDetailing of the lower tiers, lighting, resistance to soiling
Business centreRestrained string courses, pylons, entrance portalsLarge scale, precise geometry, combination with glazing
Public buildingElements of the orders, pediment, reliefs and panelsBespoke moulds, approval of samples
Private houseCornice, architraves, corner rustication, porch columnsProportion to the house, impact resistance near the ground

For residential and public buildings the core of the set usually consists of linear products; their range is given on the page GFRC cornices. Opening surrounds are selected separately, taking account of window sizes and reveal depth — see architraves and window surrounds.

How to match the scale of mouldings to the height of the building

The size of a profile is chosen not from the catalogue but from the facade: the element must be in proportion to the storey height, the width of the pier and the distance to the viewer. The same moulding looks heavy on a two-storey house and is lost on the twelfth floor.

Practical rules used by architects:

  • the crowning cornice is the largest horizontal element; string courses and sill mouldings are noticeably smaller;
  • the width of an architrave is related to the width of the window and the pier so that the surrounds of adjacent windows do not merge;
  • the higher an element is placed, the larger its divisions and the deeper its relief, while fine ornament is kept for the lower floors;
  • within one facade a limited number of profiles is used, repeated at different levels.

A scaled facade elevation and a 3D model viewed from pedestrian level help to check the solution. For important projects a full-size fragment is made and assessed on site. More on this in the article on how to choose decor from a catalogue and avoid mistakes with proportions.

The depth of relief is assessed separately. On south-facing facades sharp sunlight emphasises even a small projection, whereas on north-facing ones and in dense development, where diffuse light prevails, the same profile looks flat and is made more pronounced. Colour also affects perception: shadows read better on light products than on dark ones.

How GFRC mouldings are installed on ventilated, rendered and brick facades

The installation method depends on the facade system: GFRC products are always fixed mechanically, but the support points and the sequence of work differ. The arrangement must be decided before finishing starts, otherwise the completed facade will have to be opened up.

  • Ventilated facade. Decor is hung on the subframe through embedded parts and adjustable brackets; massive elements are given their own supports in the wall. It is important to maintain the ventilation gap and the function of the fire barriers. The specifics of the details are described in the article on the ventilated facade with GFRC cladding.
  • Rendered facade with insulation. Fixings pass through the insulation into the load-bearing wall; support elements are installed before the base coat is applied, and the junction of the render with the product is made with a sealing tape or sealant.
  • Brick or in-situ concrete wall without insulation. Products are anchored directly to the substrate; the back is grouted with mortar where necessary.

In all cases the joints between elements are left movable at set intervals, and the top faces are protected by a fall or a flashing. Linear products are set out to a string line or laser level: on a long facade a difference of a few millimetres shows up in the shadow.

What mistakes are made when ordering and installing facade mouldings?

Problems most often arise when decor is chosen at the end of the project as an afterthought and is not coordinated with the wall construction. A list of typical mistakes helps to check the design before ordering.

  • Choosing the material without regard to building height. Combustible polymer elements on a multi-storey building may not meet fire safety requirements.
  • Ordering without a layout. The linear metres have been counted, but corners, ends, breaks and make-up pieces have been forgotten.
  • No approved sample. Colour and texture chosen from a photograph look different on the facade.
  • Adhesive-only fixing. Projecting and heavy elements require anchors into the load-bearing substrate.
  • No drainage. Horizontal ledges without a fall and a drip produce streaks and wetting of the wall.
  • Rigid joints. Joints filled with mortar without movement breaks crack with temperature changes.
  • Installation before wet trades are finished. The decor is soiled with mortar and paint, and attempts are then made to clean it with aggressive compounds.

It is worth checking the design against this list before placing the order: correcting a layout or a detail on paper is incomparably easier than redoing an installed cornice. It is also useful to appoint one person responsible for coordinating the decor with windows, flashings and the subframe; otherwise adjacent contractors each resolve the junctions in their own way.

How to organise an order for mouldings: stages and what affects lead times

An order for facade mouldings is best planned as a separate workstream with its own programme: several consecutive stages lie between approval of the profiles and installation, and each depends on the client's decisions. The earlier decor is included in the overall construction programme, the lower the risk that scaffolding will have to stand waiting for products.

  1. Approval of the set and profiles. The architect and client fix the list of elements and their dimensions.
  2. Working documentation. Layout on the elevations, division into products, fixing details, schedule of items.
  3. Tooling and sample. Making of models and moulds, approval of the reference sample for colour and texture.
  4. Serial production. Products are made in an order that matches the installation sequence by work zone.
  5. Delivery and installation. Complete, labelled shipments, fixing, joint filling, final finishing.

Duration is affected most by the number of unique moulds, the time taken to approve the sample and the readiness of the substrate on site. Changing a profile after the tooling has been made means going back to the third stage, so architectural decisions are best closed before the moulds are started. On large projects production and installation run in parallel: the first batches go onto the facade while the next are being cast.

What makes up the estimate for facade mouldings

The cost of moulded decor is not only the price of the products per metre or per piece. The estimate includes production preparation, fixings, installation and associated works, and their share is sometimes comparable with the cost of the elements themselves.

  1. Design. Layout, drawings of products and details, calculation of fixings.
  2. Models and moulds. For standard profiles the tooling already exists; for bespoke ones it is made, and the smaller the run, the more noticeable this item is in the unit price.
  3. Manufacture. Depends on dimensions, complexity of relief, texture and colour.
  4. Packaging and delivery. Bulky products take up a great deal of space in the vehicle, so logistics are calculated by volume rather than weight.
  5. Fixings and subframe. Brackets, anchors, embedded parts, seals.
  6. Installation. Work at height, lifting equipment, joint filling, final painting.

The budget is reduced by standardising profiles and cutting the number of unique moulds, not by giving up mechanical fixing. A detailed breakdown of the factors is given in the article on how much GFRC decor costs. For a preliminary estimate, dimensioned elevations and a list of elements are sufficient.

How long do exterior mouldings last and how should they be maintained?

The service life of facade mouldings is determined by the material, the quality of the fixing and the way water is drained. GFRC and stone products are designed for decades of service; polymer elements and coatings over expanded polystyrene, as a rule, need repair and renewal sooner.

Maintenance comes down to a few regular actions:

  • inspection of the facade once or twice a year, first of all the joints, flashings and fixing points of projecting details;
  • washing with water and neutral agents as soiling occurs, without abrasives or acidic compounds;
  • renewal of the sealant in movement joints when it loses elasticity;
  • repainting of painted elements as the coating wears, and repeated water-repellent treatment if the maintenance schedule provides for it.

It is useful to record inspection results in the building's maintenance log, with photographs of problem areas. Such a record makes it possible to track whether a defect is developing and to schedule work for the warm season in good time. High-pressure washing and chemical cleaners are best tried first on an inconspicuous area to make sure the coating and texture will not suffer.

Local chips and cracks are repaired without removing the product. Recommendations on cleaning agents and the frequency of work are collected in the article on caring for GFRC products.

FAQ

What are exterior facade mouldings made of?

GFRC, natural stone, polymer concrete and polyurethane, or expanded polystyrene with a protective coating. Plaster is not suitable for exterior work: it absorbs water and breaks down when it freezes.

Can plaster mouldings be used on a facade?

Not recommended. Even painted plaster gradually takes on moisture through cracks and joints, and after a few winters delamination and chipping appear.

Why is GFRC better than expanded polystyrene?

GFRC is non-combustible (class NG according to the manufacturer), withstands impact and reproduces fine relief. Polystyrene is cheaper and lighter, but combustible and vulnerable to mechanical damage.

Can mouldings be made to my own sketch?

Yes. A master model and mould are made from the sketch, then a sample for approval and the series. Cost and lead time are determined after reviewing the drawings.

How are mouldings fixed to a facade?

Light elements with adhesive plus additional mechanical fixing; heavy and projecting ones with anchors and cast-in fixings to an engineered scheme with load calculation.

How long do GFRC facade mouldings last?

With correctly designed details, mechanical fixing and proper drainage, the products are designed for decades of service. What usually needs attention first is not the elements themselves but the sealant in the joints and the paint coating — these are renewed according to the maintenance schedule.

Can mouldings be installed on an insulated facade?

Yes, but the fixings must pass through the insulation into the load-bearing wall or bear on the facade subframe. Fixing points are included in the design before finishing works begin, so that the finished render or cladding does not have to be opened up.

What should be sent for an estimate of moulded decor?

Dimensioned facade drawings, a list or sketches of the elements and information on the wall construction and facade system are sufficient. If profiles have not yet been chosen, they are selected from the catalogue or developed from analogues, after which the scope and content of the work are refined.
