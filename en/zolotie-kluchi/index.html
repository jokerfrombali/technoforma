What a residential complex facade usually requires

A multi-storey residential facade has to combine a distinctive look with durability and fire safety: at this height the cladding is usually fixed to a ventilated rainscreen facade substructure, and decorative elements and panels are made from non-combustible materials. Repeating elements such as window surrounds, string courses and balcony balustrades are cost-effective to cast in series from a single mould, and installation is planned in sections so that it does not hold up finishing works and handover of the building.

The North Caucasus is a seismically active region, so low weight and reliable fixing are especially important for cladding and decorative elements here: lightweight thin-walled GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete) products reduce the load on the substructure and the supporting structure.

Why a multi-block residential complex facade is harder than a single building

The key feature of a multi-block scheme is a uniform appearance achieved while production is spread over time. Elements for different blocks are manufactured in batches months apart, so colour, texture and geometry are fixed by a reference sample approved before the series is launched.

The second task is standardisation. The fewer sizes there are among the GFRC facade panels and window surrounds, the fewer moulds are needed, the faster the output and the easier it is to replace a damaged element. The layout is usually tied to the window spacing and storey height as early as the facade design stage, so that make-up pieces remain only at corners and parapets.

The third task is coordination with the overall construction programme. Blocks are, as a rule, handed over in phases, and the facade of each must be completed before landscaping of the adjacent area begins, otherwise scaffolding and hoists obstruct the works at ground level.

How does Makhachkala's coastal climate affect the choice of cladding?

The city stands on the shore of the Caspian Sea, and a facade here is exposed to strong wind, humid salt-laden air and intense summer sun. These factors are taken into account when choosing the material, the fixings and the installation scheme.

FactorWhat it means for the facade
Wind loadOn upper storeys and at building corners the bracket spacing and the number of fixing points are, as a rule, increased by calculation
Humid salt-laden airThe subframe and fixings are selected for corrosion resistance — usually stainless steel or coated aluminium alloys
Sun and temperature swingsUV-resistant finishes and movement joints between elements are required
Freeze-thaw cycles in winterFrost resistance of the material and drainage of water from horizontal surfaces are important

Glass fibre reinforced concrete is non-combustible, and the thin wall of an element contains no steel reinforcement that could corrode and cause rust staining — a practical advantage on the coast.

How supply and installation are organised on a remote site

When the distance from the factory to the site exceeds a thousand kilometres, the dispatch schedule becomes part of the project. Elements are packed by work section and storey, marked according to the installation drawings and dispatched in the order in which they are needed on the facade.

  1. Survey of the actual geometry of the structural frame and adjustment of the layout.
  2. Installation of brackets and rails of the ventilated facade system, insulation.
  3. Hanging of panels and decorative elements tier by tier, with joint control.
  4. Sealing of junctions, installation of sills and parapet copings.

A reserve of elements for breakage and trimming is usually allowed for in advance: delivering a few pieces by a separate trip costs more and takes longer than producing them in the main batch. What determines the duration of the works is covered in the article on planning installation time.

What a client should prepare for an estimate of a similar facade

Architectural drawings are sufficient for a preliminary assessment; an accurate estimate requires information on the structures and site conditions.

  • Elevations, plans and sections with levels, in an editable format where possible.
  • The material of the external walls and frame, and data on the load-bearing capacity of the substrate.
  • The desired texture and colour, and the details the architect regards as essential.
  • The construction programme by block and information on lifting equipment on site.

The earlier the facade contractor receives this information, the more solutions can be standardised before the moulds are made. Changes introduced after series production has started usually mean new moulds and a shift in dispatches.

FAQ

Can the facades of a residential complex be installed block by block?

Yes, sequential installation is common practice for complexes of several blocks. It is important to approve a reference sample of colour and texture before the first batch is launched and to keep the moulds, so that elements for later blocks do not differ from those already installed.

Is GFRC suitable for buildings by the sea?

As a rule, yes. The material is non-combustible, frost-resistant and has no steel reinforcement within the body of the element. On the coast the main attention is paid to the subframe and fixings: they are selected for corrosion resistance and the design wind load.

Does the building need to be surveyed if design drawings are available?

Usually it does. The actual geometry of a cast-in-place frame and masonry differs from the design, and without an as-built survey the panel layout may not match the openings and corners. The survey is carried out before the elements go into production.
