Catalogue of elements and textures

The catalogue shows standard architectural mouldings and GFRC surface textures. It helps you choose a profile and finish but does not limit your options: the company makes models and moulds in its own mould shops, so elements are also produced to an architect's custom drawings.

How to use the catalogue

Mark the elements and textures that suit you and send them together with the facade drawings — our design engineers will check scale and fixing details and prepare a quote. More about this line of business on the GFRC: element manufacturing page.

Which architectural concrete products are presented in the catalogue

The catalogue brings together the standard profiles and shapes most often needed on a facade: from linear mouldings to three-dimensional panels and landscape elements. All of them are made of GFRC (glass fibre reinforced concrete), a thin-walled concrete which, with the same expressiveness, weighs considerably less than conventional cast concrete.

Catalogue products in architectural concrete are thin-walled shells with cast-in fixings, formed from ready-made patterns. The wall is, as a rule, 10–20 mm thick, so even a large cornice or capital can be fixed to an insulated wall without strengthening the load-bearing structure. The material is non-combustible and frost-resistant, which allows it to be used on buildings of different heights and purposes.

Surface textures: how they differ

Texture determines how a product looks close up and from a distance, how it ages and how visible dirt is on it. The same shape can be produced with different surfaces, so the texture is chosen separately from the profile.

The choice of texture is also influenced by where the element is located. For details at eye level — portals, the plinth, entrance surrounds — cleanliness of surface and accuracy of pattern matter. On the upper storeys a fine texture cannot be read; silhouette and depth of relief do the work there, so it is rational to reserve a complex surface for the lower tiers.

Samples should be viewed in daylight and from the distance at which the facade will actually be perceived. A detailed comparison is given in the article on GFRC textures.

Product colour: integral colouring or surface coating?

Colour is obtained in two ways: pigment is added to the mix, or the finished product is coated with facade paint. The first option gives a natural mineral tone and needs no repainting; the second allows a specified colour to be matched precisely and is easier to renew.

With integral colouring the shade depends on the cement, aggregate and pigment, so a slight tonal variation between batches is considered normal for a mineral material. For critical areas control samples are produced and approved by the architect; the products are then accepted against them. White cement widens the palette of light and clean shades, while grey cement gives a more muted range. The choice of pigments and colour stability are discussed in the article on GFRC colour.

How to match an element to the scale of the building?

A profile from the catalogue is chosen not by its picture but by its relationship to storey height, opening width and viewing distance. An element that looks good on a two-storey mansion will be lost on the twelfth floor of a residential block, and vice versa.

  1. Define the tiers of the facade: plinth, main wall field, crowning part.
  2. Assign the principal element of each tier — as a rule, a cornice, string course or entrance surround.
  3. Select the remaining details so that they are noticeably smaller than the principal one.
  4. Check the projection and height of the profile on the elevation and in the three-dimensional model.
  5. Reduce the number of different profiles: repetition simplifies moulds and installation.

A convenient reference for a first approximation is the storey height: the crowning cornice is usually made noticeably larger than the inter-storey string course, and the architrave width is related to the window width. Exact dimensions are set by the architect, while the designer checks how the profile fits with the cladding thickness and junction details.

Typical errors and ways to avoid them are examined in the article on choosing decoration from a catalogue.

Standard element or bespoke product to the architect's sketch

A standard profile is appropriate when it is close to the architect's intent in proportion and outline: a ready-made pattern shortens production preparation. A bespoke shape is justified if the building has a signature appearance, historic decoration must be replicated or the element has to fit a non-standard opening.

In practice both approaches are combined on one project. Ordinary bands and sills are taken from the catalogue with adjusted dimensions, while portals, capitals, bas-reliefs and three-dimensional panels are developed afresh. The cost of a new mould is spread over the production run, so the more repeating parts there are, the smaller its share in the price of the product. Changing the length, radius or thickness of a standard element usually does not require an entirely new pattern.

Lead times differ as well. For a standard item the pattern already exists, and production can begin once the drawings are approved. For a new shape, further stages are added — developing the three-dimensional model, making the master pattern and the mould, approving a control sample — and their duration depends on the complexity of the relief and the size of the part.

What to send together with the list of elements

The fuller the input data, the more accurate the first estimate and the fewer the rounds of approval. The following set is sufficient for selection:

  • elevation and section drawings with levels and opening dimensions;
  • a list of the selected catalogue items indicating where they are installed;
  • wall material and construction, presence of insulation and a ventilated cavity;
  • desired texture and colour, references or samples;
  • region of construction and number of storeys;
  • approximate start date of the facade works.

If there is no design yet, the estimate is made from a sketch or photographs, and the missing details and layout are developed by the design department. As a result the client receives a product schedule with quantities, fixing diagrams and a proposal for supply and installation.

Common mistakes when choosing products from the catalogue

Architectural concrete products of any complexity are technically feasible, so mistakes are more often related to composition and order preparation than to the material.

  • Too many different profiles on one facade — the number of moulds and the lead time grow.
  • Decoration is chosen without regard to insulation thickness and cladding projection.
  • No flashings or drips are provided on projecting details.
  • The texture is approved from a photograph, without a physical sample.
  • Joints of linear elements are not tied to the axes of windows and pilasters.
  • Fixing is not coordinated with the load-bearing capacity of the wall.

Logistics and installation should be considered separately. The length of linear elements and the size of panels are coordinated with vehicle dimensions, the lifting capacity of plant on site and the ease of alignment at height. It is sometimes more sensible to divide large parts into several pieces with a joint that coincides with the divisions of the facade.

All these matters are checked when the working drawings are developed, before the products go into production.

FAQ

Can the dimensions of a catalogue element be changed?

Yes. Length, profile height, radius and thickness are adjusted to the project; for minor changes the existing pattern is used, and for substantial ones a new mould is made. The decision is taken after checking against the facade drawings.

Is it possible to obtain texture samples before ordering?

Yes, texture and colour samples are produced for approval by the architect and the client. The approved sample serves as the benchmark when the products are accepted.

Are catalogue products suitable for interiors?

Yes, GFRC is also used indoors: for wall panels, cornices, portals and columns. The material is non-combustible and moisture-resistant, and its low weight simplifies fixing to partitions.
