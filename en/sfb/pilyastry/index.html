GFRC Pilasters and Rustication

Pilasters and rustication give a wall depth without projecting structures. TECHNOFORMA Group manufactures facade pilasters and rustication from glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) and installs them on site.

Facade pilasters

A pilaster is a flat vertical projection of a wall with a base and capital that follows the proportions of a column. Pilasters divide the facade vertically, frame entrances and corners and establish the building's classical order. The shaft, base and capital are made from GFRC, plain or fluted, to classical orders or in a contemporary interpretation.

Facade rustication

Rustication is stone-like cladding with emphasised joints between blocks. It gives the lower floors a sense of mass and accentuates the corners of the building.

Types of rustication

Smooth rustication with square or V-shaped joints, diamond-point rustication with a faceted surface, rock-faced rustication with a split texture, rusticated quoins and voussoir lintels over openings.

Execution

Rustication is made from GFRC panels imitating blocks; this is faster and lighter than individual stone, and the mould texture reproduces the pattern of natural stone.

Applications

Plinth and ground floors, building corners, entrance areas, piers between windows. Pilasters and rustication are often designed together with columns, cornices and facade panels.

Texture, colour and quality

The surface of GFRC products can be smooth, ground, acid-etched or textured to imitate natural stone; colour is achieved with integral pigmentation or painting. Models and moulds are made in the company's own mould shops, and quality is controlled at every stage, from mixing to packing. Products from one batch are checked against the drawing so that there is no visible difference in tone or geometry on the facade. A sample can be made before series production by agreement.

How GFRC pilasters are constructed

GFRC pilasters are hollow U-section shells with stiffening ribs and cast-in fixings rather than solid masses. The wall thickness is usually 10–20 mm, so a storey-high element weighs several times less than its stone or concrete equivalent and, as a rule, does not require the wall to be strengthened.

The shaft is divided into sections generally 2–3 m long: such pieces are convenient to transport and to plumb. The base and capital are cast separately, and the joints between sections are tied to string courses, flutes or courses of rustication so that the junction does not read. At design stage it is useful to check how the GFRC columns and capitals on the same facade are handled: the proportions of the order must match.

Proportions and dimensions: what to specify in the design

A pilaster reads correctly when its width, projection and height are related to one another and to the storey height. There are no exact universal figures — the proportions are set by the chosen order and the scale of the building — but the guidelines are well known.

  • Projection from the wall plane is most often between one sixth and one quarter of the shaft width; with less projection the pilaster is lost, with more it looks like an attached pier.
  • Shaft height in the classical orders is roughly 7–10 times its width; for two-storey pilasters the width is chosen according to this ratio.
  • The base and capital are wider than the shaft, so the distance to the window reveal is checked against them rather than the shaft.
  • Fluting is justified where the width is sufficient for several full flutes; on narrow pilasters a plain surface or a recessed panel is preferable.

More on setting out the order can be found in the article on column capitals and bases.

How is the layout of facade rustication worked out?

The layout of rustication starts with the course height: it is chosen so that a whole number of courses fits between the plinth, the window sills, the lintels and the string course. A cut course beneath a cornice or above a window is noticed immediately, so the grid is coordinated with the levels of the openings as early as the sketch stage.

GFRC rustication is made as panels that combine several 'stones'. The joint between panels is concealed in the rustication groove, and the junction is not visible on the facade. Typical parameters depend on the scale of the building:

ParameterUsual rangeWhat it affects
Course height300–600 mmthe scale and 'massiveness' of the lower tier
Joint width15–40 mmthe graphic quality of the pattern
Joint depth10–30 mmlight and shade, panel thickness
Length of a 'stone'2–3 times its heightproportions of the masonry, bonding

The corners of the building are covered with one-piece corner elements, so there is no joint or chipping on the arris. Above openings the rustication passes into a flat arch with a keystone, and at the top the tier is finished with a string course or a GFRC cornice.

Fixing pilasters and rusticated panels

The method of fixing depends on the wall construction. On a ventilated facade, pilasters and rusticated panels are hung on the subframe by means of cast-in fixings, with insulation and an air gap retained behind the cladding. On an uninsulated wall, the elements are fixed with anchors through brackets directly to the load-bearing substrate.

The load from the base and the lowest section is transferred to a support shelf or bracket, and the upper sections are restrained against overturning. A movement gap is left between sections and filled with sealant matching the colour of the product.

What mistakes occur in designs with pilasters and rustication?

Mistakes are most often made not in the material but in scale and setting-out. Before moulds are put into production, several points are worth checking:

  • pilasters are positioned without relation to window axes and load-bearing piers;
  • the capital runs into the cornice or overlaps a reveal — there was not enough room in height or width;
  • the rustication course height is not a multiple of the distance between string courses;
  • rustication that is too fine on a tall building — the pattern disappears from a distance;
  • no flashings are provided on bases and string courses, which leads to staining.

Compositional techniques are examined in the article on the rhythm and articulation of a facade, and the combination of rustication, pilasters and cornices on a completed building can be seen at the Dynasty club house.

FAQ

What is the difference between a pilaster and a half-column?

A pilaster is flat and rectangular in section, while a half-column is half of a round column.

Can rustication be done on a ventilated facade?

Yes, rusticated GFRC panels are installed on a ventilated rainscreen facade substructure.

Do you make capitals for pilasters?

Yes, to a classical order or a custom sketch.

How much does a GFRC pilaster weigh?

The weight depends on the section, height and wall thickness. Because a pilaster is a hollow shell usually 10–20 mm thick, it is several times lighter than an element of the same dimensions in stone or solid concrete. The exact weight of each section is stated in the working drawings.

Can pilasters and rustication be installed on an existing building?

Yes, in refurbishment the ornament is fixed to the existing wall or to a new ventilated facade subframe. The substrate is surveyed and the anchors are tested beforehand to confirm the bearing capacity of the wall.

How are the joints between pilaster sections concealed?

The joints are tied to architectural divisions — string courses, the base, the capital, courses of rustication — and filled with sealant matching the colour of the product. On a plain shaft the joint is kept to a minimum and placed at the same level across the whole facade.
