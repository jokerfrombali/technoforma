Dolomite Cladding Stone

Dolomite is a dense, light-coloured carbonate stone used for cladding facades, plinths, steps and interiors. At TECHNOFORMA Group you can buy dolomite from stock or to order, together with cladding design and installation.

What dolomite is

Dolomite is a sedimentary rock made of the mineral dolomite, a calcium magnesium carbonate. It looks similar to limestone but is usually denser and harder, resists abrasion better and takes finishing well. Colours range from white, grey and cream to yellowish, sometimes with pinkish tones.

Applications

Facades

Slabs fixed with adhesive or on a ventilated rainscreen facade substructure, rusticated cladding, profiled elements.

Plinths and steps

Dense dolomite resists abrasion and performs well on plinths, porches and steps.

Interiors and landscaping

Walls, floors, window sills, paving, kerbs.

Dolomite in the regions

Dolomites are widespread in the North Caucasus, including Dagestan; see the Dagestan stone page for details. Imported dolomites are supplied from stock or to order.

Processing

Dolomite is one of the soft stones handled by the company's equipment: sawing, grinding, bush-hammering, rock-face splitting, profiling. For exterior use the surface is treated with a water repellent.

If you need stone for complex decoration with a large number of identical elements, some profiles can be made from glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) with a dolomite texture.

Properties of dolomite for facade cladding

Dense dolomite with low porosity is the right choice for external cladding: it absorbs less water, copes better with freeze-thaw cycles and keeps its finish for longer. Values vary considerably from quarry to quarry, so the test report for the specific batch matters more than averaged reference figures.

For facades in temperate and northern climates, stone from the lower end of the water absorption range is normally selected. How this figure relates to the service life of cladding is covered in the article on water absorption of facade materials.

Which slab thickness and format should be chosen?

Thickness depends on the fixing method and the slab size: the larger the format and the taller the building, the thicker the stone. Slabs of 20–30 mm are usual for adhesive fixing, 30 mm and above for a ventilated system, and 30–40 mm or more for plinths, steps and parapets.

  • Small-format tiles (up to roughly 300×600 mm) — low-rise buildings, adhesive fixing, cladding of reveals and piers.
  • Medium and large slabs (600×900, 600×1200 mm and similar) — ventilated facade with visible or concealed fixings.
  • Profiled elements — cornices, string courses, sills, opening surrounds; these are cut from thick blanks, so the section is agreed against what the cutting allows.

The format is matched to slab and block dimensions: a modular layout reduces waste and the amount of trimming on site. The approach to choosing a section is described in the piece on cladding slab thickness.

Fixing dolomite: adhesive or a subframe?

Dolomite is bonded with adhesive on low buildings over a sound, even substrate; a subframe is used where there is insulation, considerable height or large slabs. In either case the fixing method is set by the design, not by the installer's preference.

Adhesive fixing uses compounds made for natural stone that do not stain a light surface, and slabs above ground-floor level are generally secured mechanically as well, with cramps or anchors. Joints are left open for grouting; butt-jointed laying is not acceptable on a facade, since both stone and wall need a movement gap.

In a ventilated system the slabs rest on clips or on concealed fixings set in kerfs in the edges, with an air gap between the stone and the insulation. For carbonate stone the quality of the kerf and a margin of thickness at the fixing point are important. Details and limitations of this arrangement are given in the article on ventilated facades in natural stone.

Dolomite, limestone or travertine: which to choose for a facade

These three stones are close in colour and origin but differ in density and outdoor behaviour. Dolomite usually sits in the middle: it is harder than most limestones and more uniform than travertine, while still taking profiled work readily.

Dolomitedense, fine-grainedwear resistance, crisp profilessensitive to acids, like all carbonates
Limestonefrom dense to porouswide range of shades, easy cuttingporous grades need protection
Travertinelayered, with cavitiesexpressive patternoutdoors the pores are filled or kept under control

Where one building needs smooth planes, steps and carved ornament alike, dolomite allows everything to be kept in a single material. For comparison, see the pages on limestone for facades and travertine cladding.

Where dolomite facade cladding is appropriate: building types

Dolomite is chosen for facade cladding where a light, calm stone with crisp detailing is required. The approach to selecting grade and format depends on the type of building.

  • Residential complexes and club houses — cladding of lower storeys, entrance areas and portals; higher up the facade, stone is often combined with lighter materials to reduce the load on the walls.
  • Hotels and business centres — large-format slabs on a ventilated system, a consistent joint pattern, and stricter requirements for uniformity of tone within a batch.
  • Public and administrative buildings — plinths, staircases and colonnades, where wear resistance and the ability to replace an individual slab matter.
  • Private houses — small-format tiles fixed with adhesive, window surrounds, porches and retaining walls in the grounds.

On multi-storey projects the decision on stone is taken together with the structural engineer: the weight of the cladding enters the calculation of the subframe and anchors. In seismic areas additional requirements apply to fixings, and this is taken into account as early as the concept stage.

Common mistakes when working with dolomite

Most problems with dolomite cladding stem not from the stone but from grade selection and detailing. The most frequent errors are these:

  • plinth stone is chosen by colour, without data on water absorption and frost resistance;
  • a polished finish is used on steps and landings where a slip-resistant texture is needed;
  • no flashings or drips are provided, so water runs down the face and leaves streaks;
  • acidic cleaners are used, which attack the carbonate surface;
  • slabs from different batches are fixed in one area without first being laid out by tone.

Water-repellent treatment is renewed at the interval set by the product manufacturer, usually once every few years. Which impregnations suit carbonate stone is described in the article on protecting natural stone.

What to prepare for a dolomite cladding estimate

Dimensioned elevation drawings and a clear idea of which areas are to be clad are enough for a preliminary estimate. The fuller the input data, the fewer assumptions in the bill of quantities.

  1. Elevations, sections and plans with levels, and opening sizes.
  2. Wall material and condition, presence of insulation.
  3. Preferred tone, surface finish and slab format.
  4. A list of profiled elements with their sections.
  5. Region of construction and delivery dates.

Repeated complex ornament in large quantities is sometimes more sensibly made in glass fibre reinforced concrete — for example, GFRC cornices with a stone-like texture, leaving the flat areas in natural dolomite.

FAQ

What is the difference between dolomite and limestone?

Dolomite is a calcium magnesium carbonate and is usually denser and harder than limestone.

Where can I buy dolomite stone?

From the company's stock in the Moscow Region or to order; delivery to the regions via the company's warehouses.

Is dolomite suitable for plinths?

Yes, dense varieties perform well on plinths and steps.

What thickness of dolomite is needed for a ventilated facade?

Generally 30 mm or more; the exact figure depends on slab format, fixing type and building height and is determined by calculation in the design.

Does dolomite yellow over time?

Dense dolomite changes tone only slightly. Noticeable darkening is usually caused by soiling and wetting, which is why flashings, drips and periodic water-repellent treatment matter.

Can dolomite be combined with GFRC ornament?

Yes. Flat areas are made in stone, while repeated profiled elements are made in GFRC, with colour and texture matched to the dolomite.
